6. Mineral Classification and Geological Distribution

Minerals are classified as metallic (further split into ferrous — iron, manganese, chromite, nickel, cobalt — and non-ferrous — copper, bauxite, lead, zinc, gold), non-metallic (mica, limestone, dolomite, gypsum, phosphate) and energy/fuel minerals (coal, petroleum, natural gas, uranium, thorium).

The controlling geological fact is that most metallic minerals occur in the ancient crystalline rocks of the Peninsular plateau — the Dharwar and Cuddapah systems of the Archaean shield — while most fuel minerals other than coal occur in the sedimentary basins of the Gondwana belts and the offshore and alluvial tracts. Hence the "mineral belt" concept:

  • North-Eastern Plateau belt — Chhotanagpur (Jharkhand), Odisha, West Bengal, Chhattisgarh. The richest belt, holding iron ore, coal, manganese, bauxite and mica; the basis of the Damodar valley industrial concentration.
  • South-Western Plateau belt — Karnataka, Goa, Tamil Nadu. Iron ore, manganese, limestone; also Kolar and Hutti gold. Lacks coal except lignite at Neyveli.
  • North-Western belt — Aravallis in Rajasthan and Gujarat. Non-ferrous metals — copper at Khetri, zinc and lead at Zawar and Rampura Agucha, plus uranium, mica, gypsum and marble.

Specific locations frequently asked: iron ore — Odisha (Barbil-Joda, Gurumahisani), Jharkhand (Noamundi), Chhattisgarh (Bailadila, exporting through Visakhapatnam, and Dalli-Rajhara), Karnataka (Bellary-Hospet, Kudremukh), Goa. Grades in descending order: magnetite (highest iron content, magnetic), haematite (most abundant and commercially most important in India), limonite and siderite. Manganese — Odisha (largest), Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh (Balaghat), Maharashtra (Nagpur-Bhandara). Bauxite — Odisha (Kalahandi, Koraput — the largest producer), Gujarat, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh (Amarkantak plateau). Mica — India was historically the leading producer; the Koderma-Gaya-Hazaribagh belt of Jharkhand, the Nellore belt of Andhra, and Ajmer in Rajasthan. Copper — Khetri (Rajasthan), Singhbhum (Jharkhand), Balaghat (MP).

Energy minerals. Coal occurs in two systems: the Gondwana coalfields (over 95% of reserves, non-coking and coking bituminous, in the Damodar, Son, Mahanadi and Godavari valleys — Jharia, Raniganj, Bokaro, Karanpura, Talcher, Singrauli, Korba) and the younger Tertiary coalfields of the north-east, Jammu and Kashmir, with high sulphur content. Lignite or brown coal is at Neyveli (Tamil Nadu). Ranked by carbon content: peat → lignite → bituminous → anthracite, anthracite being the highest grade and scarce in India. Petroleum and gas come from Mumbai High, Gujarat (Ankleshwar, Kalol), Assam (Digboi — the oldest, Naharkatiya, Moran) and the Krishna-Godavari basin. Thorium from monazite sands of the Kerala and Odisha coasts is strategically vital given India's three-stage nuclear programme; uranium comes from Jaduguda (Jharkhand) and Tummalapalle (Andhra).

7. Industrial Location Theory and Factors

Industrial location is governed by geographical factors (raw material, power, water, labour, land, climate), economic factors (market, transport, capital, banking) and political factors (government policy, incentives, freight equalisation). Two theoretical anchors are examinable: Weber's least-cost theory, which locates industry to minimise transport and labour cost, using the material index (weight of localised material ÷ weight of product) — an index above 1 means a weight-losing industry that locates near raw material (iron and steel, sugar, cement), while below 1 indicates a weight-gaining industry locating near market (soft drinks, brewing); and Losch's profit-maximisation and market-area approach.

Applications: sugar mills cluster near cane fields because sucrose content falls rapidly after harvesting — hence the shift of the industry's centre of gravity from UP to Maharashtra, where higher recovery rates and cooperative organisation prevail. Cotton textiles were originally market and port-oriented at Mumbai and Ahmedabad, being neither weight-losing nor gaining, and have since decentralised. Aluminium smelting is power-oriented because it is extremely electricity-intensive. Iron and steel is raw-material oriented, requiring iron ore, coking coal, limestone and water in proximity — which explains why almost all integrated plants sit on the Chhotanagpur plateau.

Related concepts worth naming: agglomeration economies and industrial clusters; footloose industries (electronics, IT), which are not tied to raw material and locate on labour, infrastructure and amenity; industrial inertia, where an industry remains after its original locational advantage has disappeared; backward and forward linkages; and growth poles (Perroux), the theoretical basis of India's regional industrial policy.

8. Major Industries and Regions

Iron and steel. Learn the plants with their collaborating countries: TISCO Jamshedpur (1907, the first private and oldest integrated plant, on the Subarnarekha), IISCO Burnpur, Visvesvaraya Iron and Steel at Bhadravati (Karnataka, the first in the south, using charcoal and hydroelectricity), and the public-sector plants of the Second and Third Plans — Rourkela (Odisha, German collaboration), Bhilai (Chhattisgarh, Soviet), Durgapur (West Bengal, British), Bokaro (Jharkhand, Soviet), with later plants at Visakhapatnam (the first shore-based plant, permitting imported coking coal), Salem, Vijayanagar and Bhadravati. India is now among the largest crude steel producers, but depends on imported coking coal — the principal structural vulnerability.

Cotton textiles — the oldest organised industry, spanning organised mills, powerlooms and handlooms; Maharashtra, Gujarat and Tamil Nadu dominate, with Coimbatore as a major centre and Tiruppur for knitwear. Jute is concentrated in the Hooghly basin of West Bengal; Partition left India with most of the mills and Pakistan with much of the raw jute, an important historical point. Sugar is second only to textiles among agro-industries. Cement requires limestone, coal and gypsum, concentrating in Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Andhra and Tamil Nadu. Aluminium smelters sit near cheap power and bauxite — NALCO in Odisha, HINDALCO at Renukoot, BALCO at Korba. Fertiliser plants locate on gas pipelines and ports. Automobiles cluster around Chennai (the "Detroit of India"), Pune-Chakan, Gurugram-Manesar and Sanand. IT and software concentrate in Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Pune, Chennai, Gurugram and Noida — footloose, human-capital driven, and clustered around Software Technology Parks.

The eight recognised industrial regions — Mumbai-Pune, Hooghly, Bengaluru-Chennai, Gujarat, Chhotanagpur, Vishakhapatnam-Guntur, Gurugram-Delhi-Meerut and Kollam-Thiruvananthapuram — should be linked to their locational logic rather than merely listed.

9. Policy, Governance and Sustainability

The legal framework rests on the Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act, 1957. Its 2015 amendment introduced mandatory auction by competitive bidding for mineral concessions, replacing the discretionary first-come-first-served regime that the Supreme Court had criticised; created the District Mineral Foundation, funded by a levy on miners and spent through the Pradhan Mantri Khanij Kshetra Kalyan Yojana on communities affected by mining; and established the National Mineral Exploration Trust. Later amendments opened commercial coal mining to the private sector, ending Coal India's near-monopoly, and created a separate category of critical and strategic minerals where the Centre auctions concessions directly. Minerals are classified as major or minor, with minor minerals regulated by state rules. Offshore minerals have their own statute.

Critical minerals — lithium, cobalt, nickel, graphite, and the rare earth elements — are now central to policy because of electric mobility, batteries, magnets and semiconductors. India has notified a list of critical minerals, created KABIL to acquire overseas assets, and joined the Mineral Security Partnership; the lithium find in Reasi (Jammu and Kashmir) and reserves in Karnataka are frequently in the news. China's dominance of rare-earth processing is the strategic backdrop.

Sustainability content for mains: mining causes deforestation, subsidence, acid mine drainage, dust and silica-related occupational disease, and displacement disproportionately of tribal communities, since a large share of mineral wealth lies in Fifth Schedule areas — which is why the PESA Act, Forest Rights Act gram-sabha consent, and the Samatha and Niyamgiri judgments matter. The Sustainable Development Framework and star-rating of mines, mine closure planning, and the doctrine that natural resources are held in public trust complete the frame.

Trap watch. Haematite is India's commercially most important iron ore, though magnetite has higher iron content. Over 95% of coal reserves are Gondwana, not Tertiary; anthracite is scarce in India. Neyveli is lignite. Sugar is weight-losing and locates near cane; aluminium is power-oriented. Jamshedpur (1907) is the oldest integrated plant, but Visvesvaraya at Bhadravati was the first in the south and Visakhapatnam the first shore-based one. The DMF is funded by miners and spent on affected communities. Minor minerals are regulated by states, not the Centre.
