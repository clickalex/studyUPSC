4. The Mughal Atelier: From Persian Import to Indian Synthesis

The Mughal school is the hinge of Indian painting history because it is the only tradition that connects every other one: it descends from the Persianate manuscript, it absorbs the indigenous Jain and Central Indian idioms, and when it collapses it seeds Rajasthan, the Punjab hills and the successor courts. The founding is precisely datable and examinable: Humayun, returning from Safavid exile in 1555, brought the Persian masters Mir Sayyid Ali and Abdus Samad, and Akbar institutionalised them into the taswirkhana — the imperial atelier — where Persian composition met Indian colour, Indian flora-fauna observation, and the flat-profile conventions of the pre-Mughal Jain and Chaurapanchasika styles. The Hamzanama, the atelier's launch project (some 1,400 large cloth folios illustrating the Dastan-i Amir Hamza, c. 1562–77), is the material proof of synthesis: Persian narrative framing with an increasingly Indian palette and Indian faces. Akbar's later commissions — the Tutinama, the Razmnama (the Persian Mahabharata), the Akbarnama itself — extended the synthesis to Hindu epic material illustrated by Muslim and Hindu masters working jointly, with the celebrated Hindu painters Daswant and Basawan rising to prominence in an atelier whose hierarchy was meritocratic across religion.

Jahangir's reign (1605–27) is the connoisseur peak, and the shift from Akbar to Jahangir is the comparison examiners most reward. Where Akbar commissioned narrative manuscripts — epics, histories, fables — Jahangir commissioned portraits and natural-history studies: individual likenesses of courtiers, dervishes and emissaries, and the flora-fauna albums for which Ustad Mansur (the dodo, the Siberian crane, the zebra) and Manohar are remembered. Two external currents entered under Jahangir: European prints and oil paintings arriving with the Jesuit missions, whose chiaroscuro, haloed angels and globes were absorbed as motifs (the famous \"Jahangir preferring a Sufi shaikh to kings\", with James I of England among the rejected rulers, is dated c. 1618–22), and a new self-consciousness about authorship — Jahangir names his painters in the Tuzuk, ranking them publicly. Shah Jahan (1628–58) then converted connoisseurship into ceremony: the Padshahnama (c. 1636–56, imperial workshops under Bichitr and others) returns to the chronicle format but renders it as jewel-toned court ritual — durbar scenes, formal garden parties, exquisite hashiya (painted borders) — with a polish and formality that mark the school's classicism and, simultaneously, its creative cooling. Aurangzeb's disengagement after 1658 starved the atelier of commissions; masters dispersed to the Rajput courts, the Punjab hills and the successor states, and the \"provincial Mughal\" of Awadh, Murshidabad and Farrukhabad is the afterglow genre — recognisably Mughal in costume and composition, but looser, cheaper and increasingly hybridised with local idioms.

The portable causal claim is that Mughal painting declined not because skill vanished but because patronage withdrew: the atelier was an imperial institution, and when the emperor stopped commissioning, the masters carried the technique to every court that would pay. That dispersal is precisely why the late seventeenth and eighteenth centuries — the empire's political twilight — are the golden age of Rajasthani and Pahari painting. An answer that frames Mughal decline as diffusion rather than death has understood the mechanism; an answer that merely lists emperors has described the symptom.

5. The Rajput Response: Rajasthani Schools and the Ragamala Logic

Rajasthani painting is best understood not as a single school but as a family of court styles united by theme and opposed to Mughal naturalism by temperament. Where the Mughal atelier prized portraiture, observed nature and court ceremony, the Rajput courts prized devotion, love and heroism — Krishna and Radha, the Ragamala and Baramasa cycles, the Ramayana and Bhagavata, hunting and battle — rendered in flat planes of saturated colour, bold line and hieratic composition. The earliest dated anchor is the Chawand Ragamala of 1605 (painted at Chawand near Udaipur, Mewar), which proves that a mature indigenous idiom predates any Mughal influence in Rajasthan; the mid-seventeenth-century Mewar Ramayana (Aranya Kanda 1651, Uttara Kanda 1653) and Sahibdin's Ragamala (1628) then show that idiom at full confidence. Mughal influence arrived later and unevenly — earliest and deepest in the courts closest to imperial service (Amber-Jaipur, Bikaner, whose rulers served as Mughal mansabdars and imported trained masters), latest and thinnest in Mewar, which resisted longest — and the resulting spectrum from \"pure Rajput\" (Mewar, Bundi-Kota) to \"Mughal-inflected\" (Bikaner, Jaipur) is the comparison that organises the whole family.

Each major court then developed a signature that examiners test as a triad of place–patron–motif. Mewar (Udaipur–Chawand): the Chawand Ragamala 1605 and the great Ramayana sets; bold, archaic, narrative. Kishangarh: the outlier masterpiece — under Raja Savant Singh (r. 1748–64, the poet Nagari Das) and his master Nihal Chand, the court produced the Bani Thani (c. mid-18th century, the \"Indian Mona Lisa\"), whose elongated lotus eyes, arched brows and refined profile converted a court singer-poet into the icon of Krishna-bhakti longing; the style is instantly recognisable by its elegant attenuation, Mughal-delicate draughtsmanship in the service of Vaishnava feeling. Bundi-Kota: dense green hunting scenes, elephants, the monsoon Baramasa mood, and the celebrated Kota pursuit compositions. Bikaner: the Mughal-trained atelier (the Usta family, Muslim masters working for Rathor patrons) with fine portraiture and mandala-style compositions. Marwar-Jodhpur and Dhundhar-Amber-Jaipur: bold devotional cycles on the one hand, increasingly Mughalised court portraiture on the other. The Ragamala idea itself — painting each raga and ragini as a personified mood with its season, hour and lovers — is the distinctively Indian fusion the family exists to express: music made visible through Krishnaite poetry, with Bhairavi at dawn, Dipak in lamplight and Megha Malhar in the rains as the canonical stations.

The causal contrast to hold against the Mughal story is one of patronage sociology. The imperial atelier served a single emperor's taste and died when that taste withdrew; the Rajput schools served dozens of courts, temples and Vaishnava sects (notably the Vallabha tradition at Nathdwara, whose pichhwai cloth hangings behind the Shrinathji icon are the temple idiom of the same aesthetic), and therefore survived any single court's eclipse. Decentralised devotional demand outlasted centralised imperial demand — which is why Rajasthani painting has no sharp \"decline\" date and shades continuously into the nineteenth-century bazaar and folk idioms.

6. The Hills and the Deccan: Pahari Brilliance and Sultanate Synthesis

Pahari painting — the art of the Punjab hill states — compresses into a century and a half the most dramatic stylistic arc in Indian art, and its two poles, Basohli and Kangra, are the contrast pair examiners return to most. Basohli (c. 1660–90, under Raja Kripal Pal; the Rasamanjari of the poet Bhanudatta illustrated c. 1695) is the bold pole: intense flat primaries, beetle-wing iridescence in jewellery, staring lotus eyes, crowded passionate compositions — a style whose energy reads as folk idiom suddenly given court resources. Kangra (post-1765, under the Katoch ruler Raja Sansar Chand of Guler-Kangra) is the lyrical pole: cool delicate washes, fine line, moonlit Bhagavata and Gita-Govinda reveries, the Bihari Satsai and the Baramasa rendered as love-in-landscape. The bridge between them is Guler, the pre-Kangra style (c. 1720–60) and specifically the family atelier of Manaku and Nainsukh: Manaku's Gita-Govinda and Ramayana sets (c. 1740s) fuse Basohli passion with new naturalism, while Nainsukh — working for the Mughal-allied Balwant Singh of Jasrota — produced portrait studies of almost Company-School observational precision. The causal sequence is patronage plus pedagogy: Mughal-trained draughtsmanship filtering into hill courts through dispersed masters, meeting indigenous Vaishnava feeling, under rulers rich enough (from the hills' trade and tribute position) to sustain ateliers across generations.

The Deccani schools run the parallel experiment under the Sultanates, and their interest is precisely that they synthesise the other Persianate current — Safavid and immigrant Persian, Arab and African-Habshi court culture — with peninsular Hindu idioms, largely independent of Mughal Delhi until the late seventeenth century. Ahmadnagar produced the earliest Deccani manuscript, the Tarif-i Husain Shahi and the Nujum al-Ulum (c. 1570, an encyclopaedia of astrology and magic with hundreds of miniatures). Bijapur under Ibrahim Adil Shah II (r. 1580–1627) — the poet-king, the Kitab-i Nauras — fused Sufi mysticism, Hindu deities and Persianate portraiture into the most intellectually syncretic court art in India. Golconda under the Qutb Shahis is the opulent pole: lush gold, dancing girls, courtly processions, and an openness to European motifs through the Coromandel trade. Mughal conquest (Bijapur 1686, Golconda 1687) absorbed Deccani masters into the imperial idiom; Hyderabad's Asaf Jahi court then carried a late Mughal-Deccani hybrid into the nineteenth century. The trap to avoid is treating \"Deccani\" as provincial Mughal: chronologically and aesthetically it is an independent formation, and several of its masterpieces predate the Mughal presence in the Deccan entirely.

The nineteenth century then adds the colonial coda, which examiners test as decline-and-response. The Company School (late 18th–19th century, Patna/Murshidabad/Delhi/Lucknow/Tanjore) redirected Indian draughtsmanship to European patron demand: botanical and zoological studies, \"picturesque\" monuments, caste-and-occupation sets — technically accomplished, culturally extractive. Kalighat pat (19th-century Calcutta) is the bazaar counterpoint: swift brush-drawn scrolls satirising babu society and depicting goddesses, sold to pilgrims. The Bengal School (Abanindranath Tagore, Bharat Mata 1905; E.B. Havell's patronage at the Calcutta School of Art; Nandalal Bose, Asit Haldar) then mounted the nationalist response — wash technique, Mughal-Rajput-Japanese revivalism, and an explicit rejection of Company naturalism — which in turn seeded Santiniketan, the modernists (Jamini Roy's pat-derived primitivism, Amrita Sher-Gil's Pahari-Cézanne fusion) and, institutionally, the post-independence Lalit Kala Akademi (1954). The arc from Humayun's Persians to Abanindranath's Bharat Mata is one continuous argument about what Indian painting is for.

7. The Living Traditions: Material, Ritual, and How Examiners Test Them

Folk and tribal painting survives where court painting died because its patron is not a king but a ritual calendar: walls prepared for weddings, floors for festivals, scrolls for epic performance. The examination logic follows directly — each tradition is tested as a chain of community → surface → material → occasion → motif — and answers that supply the full chain score while answers that supply only the state do not. Madhubani-Mithila (Bihar–Nepal, Maithil women): kohbar wedding-chamber walls dense with fish, bamboo, lotus and fertility symbols; two manners, Bharni (filled, colour) and Kachni (line, monochrome); the documented revival credit runs through William Archer's 1934-earthquake-inspection discovery and the 1970s commercialisation that made it India's most recognised folk idiom. Warli (Maharashtra–Gujarat tribal belt): white rice-paste on cow-dung-and-mud walls, the tarpa dance circle and the chauk marriage square, human figures built from circle-triangle-line grammar; the modern visibility is substantially the work of Jivya Soma Mashe (1934–2018). Phad (Shahpura, Rajasthan): fifteen-metre cloth scrolls of the Pabuji and Devnarayan epics, painted by the Joshi family and performed by bhopa-bhopi singer-priests — the painting is a liturgical object, not a picture. Patachitra splits into Odisha (Jagannath-triad icons and mythological cloth panels, prepared with tamarind-seed glue and lampblack) and Bengal (Patua singer-scrolls, the narrative sung as the scroll unrolls). Kalamkari splits into Srikalahasti (hand-drawn pen, temple hangings, mythological narrative) and Machilipatnam (hand-block-printed, Persianate tree-of-life for the Coromandel export trade) — the test point is pen-versus-block, with myrobalan and iron-mordant chemistry underneath both. Around these stand Gond (Madhya Pradesh, Jangarh Singh Shyam's dot-and-dash modernism), Pithora (Bhilala-Rathwa horse-and-creation murals), Cheriyal (Telangana narrative scrolls), Thangka (Ladakh–Sikkim–Tawang Vajrayana iconometry, governed by canonical proportion texts), Sanjhi (Mathura's paper-stencil Vaishnavism), Manjusha (Bhagalpur snake-goddess boxes) and Kalighat (the urban pat bridge to modernism).

Three comparative moves organise this abundance for the examination. First, surface logic: wall traditions (Madhubani kohbar, Warli, Pithora, Gond murals) are architectural and seasonal, repainted for occasions; cloth and paper traditions (Phad, Patachitra, Kalamkari, Cheriyal, Thangka) are portable and performable, carried by singer-priests or monks. Second, authorship logic: several traditions are women's ritual knowledge (Madhubani, Warli domestic walls, Sanjhi preparation) while others are hereditary male-caste occupations (Phad's Joshis, Cheriyal's Nakashis, Thangka's monastic ateliers) — the gender and caste of making is itself testable. Third, protection logic: the Geographical Indications regime now anchors several traditions commercially (Madhubani, Warli, Phad, Cheriyal, Tangaliya-adjacent weaves aside, the Odisha Pattachitra and Bengal Patachitra registrations), and One District One Product and state emporia have converted ritual objects into livelihood crafts — a transition candidates should evaluate, not merely celebrate, since commercialisation standardises motifs and bleaches ritual meaning even as it pays the painter. The closing analytical note is that \"folk\" is not a lesser court art but a different economy of art: court painting died when courts died, while ritual painting lives wherever the ritual is still performed.
