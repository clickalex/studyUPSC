The Feudalism Debate and Its Critics

Early medieval India is the period in Indian historiography where a single interpretive framework was proposed, tested against evidence and then largely abandoned — and understanding that arc is worth more than memorising dynasty lists. D.D. Kosambi, in An Introduction to the Study of Indian History (1956), distinguished "feudalism from above", in which a king extracted tribute directly from producers without creating landed intermediaries, from "feudalism from below", in which grants created a class of lords holding rights over land and labour. R.S. Sharma systematised the second model in Indian Feudalism (1965). His chain of causation ran from the crisis of the post-Gupta state — the "kaliyuga crisis" that D.N. Jha and B.N.S. Yadava traced in the period's own texts, full of omens about inverted social order — to the multiplication of land grants (brahmadeya, agrahara, devadana), which transferred not just revenue but fiscal and judicial rights through the parihara immunities, created sub-infeudation, imposed forced labour (vishti), and produced what he called serfdom. The consequences he drew were political decentralisation, the decay of trade and coinage, the decline of towns, and the closure of the varna system into a proliferation of jatis. For a generation this was the standard reading, and it still shapes the way examination questions are framed.

The critique came from several directions at once. On coinage, D.N. Jha showed that the "dark age" of currency had been overstated, and John S. Deyell's Living Without Silver (1990) demonstrated that north India ran a functioning bullion economy, with Pratihara drammas of reliable silver content circulating in large volume — a shortage of fresh minting is not evidence of commercial collapse. On agrarian structure, B.D. Chattopadhyaya's The Making of Early Medieval India (1994) argued that grants were not a mechanism of decentralising an existing settled order but an instrument of expanding it: they financed the colonisation of forest and wasteland, produced new market towns (nagara, pattana, nagaram), and generated what he called a "third urbanisation". On political form, Hermann Kulke's "integrative state formation" and Burton Stein's segmentary-state model (the latter drawn from African anthropology and much contested for the Cholas) replaced the simple decentralisation thesis with a picture of local autonomy under an integrative imperial centre. On region, R. Champakalakshmi and Y. Subbarayalu showed that the Tamil evidence — a strongly centralised Chola bureaucracy, a monetised economy, merchant guilds and temple corporations — simply does not fit the northern model. And from method, Suvira Jaiswal and others warned against reading normative and literary texts as descriptions of social reality. The category "feudalism" is now largely retired because it imports the European manor and the European serf, neither of which the Indian evidence supplies; but the questions it raised survive, and the current answer is regional rather than uniform — decentralisation in parts of the north, integration in the Chola south, and agrarian and commercial expansion almost everywhere.

The Tripartite Struggle: Why Kannauj, and Who Actually Won

For roughly two centuries, three powers fought over one city. The Gurjara-Pratiharas, whose centre of gravity moved from Bhinmal and Ujjain to Kannauj itself under Vatsaraja and Nagabhata II, held it from the early ninth century to the early tenth, with Mihira Bhoja I (c.836–885) and Mahendrapala I (c.885–910) at the apex. The Palas of Bengal, under Dharmapala (c.770–810) and Devapala (c.810–850), contested it from the east and left the imperial imprint in the Khalimpur and Monghyr copper plates, which record Dharmapala's consecration at Kannauj attended by the lords of Bhoja, Matsya, Madra, Kuru, Yavana, Gandhara and Kira. The Rashtrakutas of the Deccan, ruling from Manyakheta, were the power that repeatedly intervened and never stayed: Dhruva (c.780–793) defeated both Vatsaraja and Dharmapala; Govinda III (c.793–814) did the same again around 800; Indra III (c.914–929) sacked Kannauj in about 915–16 in the campaign that broke Pratihara power permanently; and Krishna III (c.939–967) defeated the Cholas at Takkolam in 949. Why Kannauj? Three reasons, all reinforcing. It was the symbolic capital of Harsha's empire, so holding it conferred imperial legitimacy. It sat in the Ganga–Yamuna doab, the richest agricultural tract in north India, which meant revenue. And it commanded the trunk route linking the Gangetic plain with Gujarat, Rajasthan and the western ports, which meant customs and the movement of armies.

The most vivid external testimony comes from the Arab geographers rather than from Indian sources. Sulaiman's Silsilat al-Tawarikh (c.851) names the four greatest kings of the world — the Caliph, the Emperor of China, the Balhara and the Emperor of Rome — and declares the Balhara the greatest of the four, noting the stability of his realm, the long life of his dynasty and his hostility to the Arabs. "Balhara" is an Arabicisation of Vallabharaja, the Rashtrakuta imperial title, and Sulaiman places his capital at "Mankir" — Manyakheta or Malkhed. Al-Masudi's Muruj al-Dhahab (c.915–43) confirms the capital and reports the striking detail that the Balhara's coins were called Muhammadi dirhams, issued in deference to the Caliph. Against this, the Arab chroniclers also record the Pratihara king as "the most inveterate enemy of the Arabs" (Al-Baladhuri's account of Mihira Bhoja), which is the same coin seen from the other side: the Rashtrakutas courted the Arab traders of the western coast while the Pratiharas fought the Arab governors of Sindh. The struggle ended not with a winner but with exhaustion. After Indra III's sack, the Pratihara feudatories — the Chandelas of Bundelkhand, the Kalachuris, the Paramaras, the Chahamanas — became independent, Kannauj passed to minor rulers, and Mahmud of Ghazni's raids of 1018 removed the city's imperial function altogether. The Rashtrakutas were in turn displaced by the Chalukyas of Kalyani under Tailapa II in 973, and the Palas declined into the Sena takeover of the eleventh century. The tripartite struggle thus has a clean lesson: three states consumed themselves over a symbolic capital while a fourth force — the Ghaznavid cavalry — arrived to collect the exhaustion.

Rajput Origins: Legend, the Foreign-Origin Theory and the Status Question

The Rajput problem is a problem of sources before it is a problem of history. The founding legend is the agnikula myth in Chand Bardai's Prithviraj Raso: at a fire altar on Mount Abu, the sage Vasishtha created four lineages — the Pratiharas, the Chahamanas, the Chalukyas and the Paramaras — to defend dharma after the older kshatriya lines had been destroyed. The decisive point is that this account appears only in the later, sixteenth-century recensions of the text and is absent from the inscriptions of the very dynasties it glorifies, which claim solar or lunar descent instead. That asymmetry is why most historians treat agnikula as a legitimising myth composed for later political purposes.

V.A. Smith and W. Crooke built the foreign-origin theory on the identification of the Gurjaras with Central Asian immigrants and on the appearance of new clan names after the sixth century, arguing that Huna, Scythian and Gurjara groups were assimilated into the kshatriya order — with the fire legend read as an echo of a Central Asian origin ritual. D.R. Bhandarkar and C.V. Vaidya argued the opposite: an indigenous derivation from older kshatriya lineages. The modern synthesis is neither. B.D. Chattopadhyaya and R.S. Sharma emphasised social mobility — tribal chieftains and landed families rising into royal status as the grant economy created new political nodes — while Norman Kolff, in Rajput Rebellion and Empire, argued that "rajput" was in origin an office rather than a lineage: the word rajaputra appears from the eighth century denoting a royal officer or a holder of territory, and only hardened into a hereditary identity by the twelfth, a process Kolff calls "rajputisation" and dates largely to the fifteenth–seventeenth centuries. What made the status was possession of a horse, a sword, a fort and a bard — the bard being the genealogist who could prove antiquity. The bardic corpus itself must be handled accordingly: the Prithviraj Raso is a sixteenth-century expansion around a twelfth-century core, Jayataka's Prithviraj Vijaya is the contemporary and more reliable account, and Nainsi ri Khyat (seventeenth-century Marwar) and the Vamsa Bhaskara are genealogical claims rather than records.

Society, Economy and Culture: What the Records Actually Show

The economic picture is one of expansion under regional variation. In the south, the Chola state ran a monetised economy in copper kasu and gold kahavanu, financed irrigation in the Kaveri delta through tanks and canals, and organised long-distance trade through the Ainnurruvar ("the Five Hundred"), the Manigramam and the Nanadesi guilds, whose charters record convoys, armed caravans and self-administered commercial law. Rajendra I's raid on Srivijaya in 1025 — the capture of Kadaram, Palembang and the Straits settlements — reads best as a trade war over the Malacca route, following Rajaraja I's earlier campaigns in Sri Lanka and the Maldives. Temples functioned as banks: inscriptions record loans to villages, guilds and individuals at twelve to fifteen per cent interest, financing tanks, festivals and the maintenance of attached musicians, dancers and artisans. In the north the same institutions took a different form — Pratihara and Chandella grants, the agrarian settlement of the Malwa and Bundelkhand tracts, and Deyell's bullion economy — and the debate over whether this represents growth or decay is the debate of Section 6 restated in material terms.

Socially, the period produced the crystallisation of jati, tied both to land grants (which settled brahmanical and service castes in new villages) and to the absorption of tribal groups at the periphery, and the rise of occupational assemblies — the Tamil nadu, ur, sabha and nagaram, whose inscribed resolutions (the Uttaramerur records of the Chola Parantaka I, describing election by lot for committee membership) are among the most detailed accounts of local self-government anywhere in the medieval world. The inscriptions also record women as donors — queens, merchant wives and temple servants — which is the strongest evidence we have that property rights extended to women in practice, alongside the darker record of sati noted by Arab travellers and Rajasthani sources and of the devadasi institution attached to major temples.

Culturally this is the age of the vernacular. Kannada produced its "three gems" — Pampa (Vikramarjuna Vijaya, 941), Ponna (Shantipurana) and Ranna (Gadayuddha) — at Rashtrakuta and Chalukya courts. Telugu began its literary history with Nannaya's Mahabharata under the Eastern Chalukya Rajaraja Narendra in the eleventh century, completed by Tikkana and Errapragada (the Kavitraya). Tamil gave Kamban's Ramavataram, Sekkizhar's Periyapuranam under Kulottunga II, and the compilation of the Alvar and Nayanar corpus into the Nalayira Divya Prabandham by Nathamuni, which fed Ramanuja's theology. Sanskrit remained the language of court and scholarship: Rajashekhara's Kavyamimamsa at the Pratihara court of Mahendrapala I; Bilhana's Vikramankadevacharita and Vijnaneshvara's Mitakshara — the digest of Hindu law that still governs Mitakshara-school inheritance — at the Chalukya court of Vikramaditya VI; Someshvara III's encyclopaedic Manasollasa; Hemachandra's grammar and his Kumarapala-charita under the Solankis Siddharaja and Kumarapala; Jayadeva's Gita Govinda at the Sena court of Lakshmanasena; and Kalhana's Rajatarangini (c.1148–50), the first work in the subcontinent that can properly be called historiography, since it names sources and dates. From outside, Alberuni's Kitab-ul-Hind (1030) inventories Indian philosophy, science and caste for a Persianate readership. In architecture the period is the age of the mature temple: the Nagara of the Pratiharas at Osian and Deogarh, the Chandellas' Kandariya Mahadeva at Khajuraho (c.1030), the Solanki Sun temple at Modhera (c.1026–27) and Queen Udayamati's Rani ki Vav (1063); the Dravida of the Cholas — Rajaraja I's Brihadisvara at Thanjavur, completed in 1010, with its sixty-six-metre vimana, and Rajendra I's Gangaikonda Cholapuram (c.1035), Airavatesvara at Darasuram and Kampahareswarar at Tribhuvanam, together the UNESCO "Great Living Chola Temples"; the Vesara of the Chalukyas at Pattadakal (Virupaksha temple, UNESCO 1987), the Rashtrakuta Kailasanatha at Ellora cut monolithically under Krishna I (c.757–783), the Hoysala temples at Belur (1113) and Halebid, and the Kakatiya Ramappa temple (1213, UNESCO 2021). Sculpture produced the Chola Nataraja bronzes, the most reproduced Indian image in the world. The learning centres that carried this culture were monastic as much as royal — Nalanda in decline, Vikramashila and Somapura founded or patronised by the Palas, Valabhi in Gujarat, Ujjain for astronomy, and the Jain bhandara manuscript libraries of Patan, until Bakhtiyar Khalji's destruction of Nalanda and Vikramashila in 1193 closed the period's intellectual infrastructure.

Trap watch. The tripartite struggle was for Kannauj among the Gurjara-Pratiharas, the Palas and the Rashtrakutas — not for Pataliputra or Ujjain; and the Rashtrakutas, based at Manyakheta, were the power that intervened from outside and never held the city. Sulaiman's Silsilat al-Tawarikh (c.851) names four great kings and calls the "Balhara" (the Rashtrakuta ruler, from Vallabharaja) the greatest, placing his capital at "Mankir"; Al-Masudi (c.915–43) confirms both and records the Muhammadi dirhams. Alberuni's Kitab-ul-Hind is 1030 and is not part of that testimony. The agnikula legend appears only in the later recensions of Chand Bardai's Prithviraj Raso, never in the inscriptions of the dynasties it names; Jayataka's Prithviraj Vijaya is the reliable contemporary text. Krishna I cut the Kailasanatha at Ellora (c.757–783); Rajaraja I completed the Brihadisvara at Thanjavur in 1010 and Rajendra I built Gangaikonda Cholapuram c.1035 — never swap the two Chola temples or their builders. Rajendra I raided Srivijaya in 1025; Rajaraja I took Sri Lanka and the Maldives. R.S. Sharma's Indian Feudalism (1965) read land grants as producing decentralisation, urban decay and serfdom; Chattopadhyaya's The Making of Early Medieval India (1994) read the same grants as agrarian expansion and a "third urbanisation"; Deyell's Living Without Silver (1990) dismantled the coinage-decline premise — state the debate as regionally unresolved, not as settled for either side. Nannaya wrote the opening of the Telugu Mahabharata under Rajaraja Narendra in the eleventh century and Tikkana and Errapragada finished it; Pampa's Kannada Pampa Bharata is a century earlier. Kalhana is Kashmiri (c.1148–50), Bilhana Western Chalukya, Rajashekhara Pratihara under Mahendrapala I — three courts, three regions, endlessly confused. Rani ki Vav is Solanki (Queen Udayamati, 1063, UNESCO 2014) and Modhera Solanki too, while Kandariya Mahadeva is Chandella. Bakhtiyar Khalji destroyed Nalanda and Vikramashila in 1193; Mahmud of Ghazni raided Somnath in 1025 and Kannauj in 1018 — different men, different centuries' ends.
