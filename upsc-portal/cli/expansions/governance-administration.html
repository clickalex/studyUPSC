8. The executive in operation — aid, advice, discretion, and deadlock

The trunk maps the Union Executive's anatomy (Arts 52–78); this section supplies the physiology — how power actually flows when the text runs out. Start with the hinge: Art 74(1) — the President acts on the CoM's aid and advice; the 44th Amendment permits returning advice once for reconsideration, after which it binds. Three situations test this design. (i) Hung Lok Sabhas and PM appointment — where no leader is obvious, the President's discretion (inviting the largest pre-poll alliance vs the largest single party, imposing floor-test time limits) becomes outcome-determinative; the SR Bommai-era jurisprudence plus floor-test precedents are the examiner's expected citations. (ii) The Art 78 channel — the PM's duty to keep the President informed is the Constitution's soft check: Presidents from Rajendra Prasad to Narayanan to Kalam used queries, returns-for-reconsideration, and public addresses to slow governments without blocking them. (iii) Ordinances (Art 123) — same force as Parliamentary law, max life session + 6 weeks, but RC Cooper and DC Wadhwa (1986 — anti-repromulgation) police mala fide use; every ordinance-heavy year revives the "ordinance raj" critique.

The Governor's office (Arts 153–162) is the same drama at State level with sharper teeth: appointing CMs in hung assemblies, reserving bills for the President (Art 200's proviso), the timing of assent, and Art 356 reports. Recent Supreme Court interventions — on assent delays, on floor tests, on the limits of pleasure doctrine for Governors as Centre's agents — are the current-affairs layer the trunk flags. The Mains synthesis: the Indian executive is parliamentary in text but situationally discretionary in fact; the examination question is always where discretion ends and partisanship begins. Answer with the Art 74/78/163 triangle: binding advice at the Centre, wider textual discretion in States (Art 163), and the Court as the referee both sides invoke.

9. Parliamentary decline or transformation? The evidence-based verdict

"Parliamentary decline" is the most-set governance essay topic of the decade, and the losing answer is a lament; the winning answer is an audit. Assemble the trunk's machinery — sittings, three sessions, Art 85's six-month rule, quorum one-tenth (Art 100(3)), Question Hour/Zero Hour/adjournment (LS only)/no-confidence (LS only, Art 75(3)), joint sittings (Art 108 — used thrice: Dowry Prohibition 1961, Banking Service Commission 1978, POTA 2002), the money-bill route (Art 110, Speaker's certificate Art 110(3)–(4)) — then test it against four measurable trends. (i) Sitting days — from 150+ in the 1950s to 60–70 in recent years; Budget sessions truncated, Winter sessions washed out. (ii) Bill scrutiny — the share of bills referred to Department-related Standing Committees fell from 60–70% (15th LS era) to single digits in recent Lok Sabhas; JPCs (Bofors 1987, Harshad Mehta 1992, 2G 2011, PDP 2019, ONOE 2024) remain exceptional, not routine. (iii) Disruption economics — Question Hour casualties, suspensions under Rule 374, and passage amid din convert oversight tools into protest theatre. (iv) The money-bill controversy — Aadhaar-as-money-bill, the Puttaswamy (2018) split, and Rojer Mathew (2019) making certification reviewable with the 7-judge reference still pending.

But the audit must record the counter-evidence of transformation: committee reports (PAC's post-CAG scrutiny with the Opposition chair convention; Estimates' 30 all-LS membership; DRSCs' ministry-wise budgets) still shape policy quietly; private members' business, though rarely passed, sets agenda; and digital transparency (Lok Sabha/Rajya Sabha TV archives, PRS data) has made floor behaviour itself examinable. The balanced verdict: Parliament deliberates less but is watched more — decline in scrutiny depth, transformation in visibility. Reforms to prescribe: fixed calendar / minimum sittings (NCRWC, 2002), mandatory committee referral norms, Opposition-day conventions, and research support (the long-pending Parliamentary Budget Office idea).

10. The judiciary's legitimacy equation — independence, accountability, access

Close governance with the trunk's judiciary (Arts 124–147, 214–237) read as a legitimacy equation with three variables. Independence: the collegium (Second Judges 1993 + Third Judges 1998) survived the NJAC experiment — 99th Amendment + NJAC Act 2014 struck down October 2015 (Fourth Judges Case) as a basic-structure violation — but opacity critiques keep the MoP debate alive; appointment delays (Centre sitting on collegium reiterations) are now litigated as constitutional breakdown. Accountability: removal (Arts 124(4), 217 via the Judges (Inquiry) Act 1968) has never succeeded — the Ramaswami (1993), Sen, Dinakaran, and Misra episodes all failed or stalled — which is why in-house procedure, the RTI-on-CJI-office ruling, and the narrow contempt debate (Prashant Bhushan 2020) recur; AIJS (Art 312; 8th and 116th Law Commissions) remains the structural reform that federal objections keep freezing. Access: pendency (crores across tiers), the e-Courts Phase III digital push, commercial courts (2015), and tribunals jurisprudence (L. Chandra Kumar 1997 → Madras Bar Association trilogy 2014/2015/2021) define whether rights on paper reach citizens.

The doctrinal spine for answers: original (Art 131) → writ (Art 32, "heart and soul") → appellate incl. SLP (Art 136) → advisory (Art 143) → review (Art 137) + curative (Rupa Hurra 2002); Art 226 wider than Art 32; Art 227 superintendence; Art 141 binding law. The PIL arc — SP Gupta (1981), Bandhua Mukti Morcha (1984), the 1980s apex phase — must be paired with the modern caution (PIL-to-PAL warnings, costs jurisprudence). Final synthesis line: the Court guards the basic structure (1973) but must itself pass the structure's test — power without accountability is precisely what Kesavananda forbids anyone to hold.
