6. What E-Governance Actually Means

Distinguish two terms that candidates routinely conflate. E-government is the use of ICT to deliver existing government services more efficiently — digitising a form, putting a register online. E-governance is broader: it reshapes the relationship between citizen and state, altering processes, structures and accountability, not merely the medium. The Second Administrative Reforms Commission's eleventh report, Promoting e-Governance: The Smart Way Forward, is the standard reference, and its core insight is that computerising a broken process merely produces a faster broken process — process re-engineering must precede digitisation.

The four interaction types are directly examinable:

  • G2C (Government to Citizen) — service delivery: certificates, pensions, subsidies, land records, utility payments.
  • G2B (Government to Business) — licensing, procurement, tax filing, regulatory clearance (GeM, MCA21, single-window clearances).
  • G2G (Government to Government) — inter- and intra-departmental data flows (e-Office, treasury systems, PFMS).
  • G2E (Government to Employee) — payroll, service records, grievance and training portals.

The evolutionary stages — presence (static information online), interaction (downloadable forms, email), transaction (payments and applications completed online) and transformation (integrated, seamless, back-end re-engineered services) — provide a ready analytical frame for mains answers about where India currently stands. Most Indian services have reached transaction; genuine transformation, requiring interoperable back-ends, remains partial.

7. Institutional Architecture and Missions

The National e-Governance Plan (NeGP), approved in 2006, organised effort into Mission Mode Projects — central (income tax, passport, MCA21, banking, insurance), state (land records, transport, police, agriculture, e-district) and integrated (e-procurement, India Portal, National Service Delivery Gateway). Its core infrastructure pillars are worth naming precisely:

  • SWAN (State Wide Area Network) — connectivity backbone down to the block level.
  • SDC (State Data Centres) — consolidated hosting and storage.
  • CSC (Common Service Centres) — the last-mile physical access points run by Village Level Entrepreneurs, addressing the fact that digital delivery fails where citizens lack devices or literacy.
  • SSDG and the National Service Delivery Gateway — middleware for interoperability.

Digital India (2015) superseded NeGP with three declared vision areas: digital infrastructure as a utility to every citizen; governance and services on demand; and digital empowerment of citizens. Its nine pillars include broadband highways, universal mobile access, the public internet access programme, e-Kranti (electronic service delivery), information for all, electronics manufacturing, IT for jobs, and early-harvest programmes. BharatNet is the optical-fibre programme to connect all gram panchayats — its persistent delays and low utilisation of laid fibre are legitimate criticisms.

Key platforms to cite with accuracy: UMANG (unified mobile app aggregating hundreds of services), DigiLocker (cloud-based document repository with legal validity under the IT Act), e-Office, PFMS (Public Financial Management System, tracking fund flow to the last mile), GeM (Government e-Marketplace for public procurement), e-Courts, e-NAM, SWAYAM and DIKSHA for education, CPGRAMS for grievances, MyGov for participatory governance, and API Setu. The National Informatics Centre is the technology arm; MeitY the nodal ministry; and the Department of Administrative Reforms and Public Grievances administers the National e-Governance Service Delivery Assessment.

8. Digital Public Infrastructure and Direct Benefit Transfer

India Stack is the standard example of digital public infrastructure and is best explained as three layered "unbundlings": an identity layer (Aadhaar with e-KYC and e-Sign), a payments layer (UPI, AePS, the Aadhaar Payment Bridge) and a data layer (DigiLocker and the Account Aggregator framework built on the DEPA consent architecture). The design principle is interoperable public rails on which private innovation is built — contrast this with closed proprietary platforms.

JAM trinity — Jan Dhan accounts, Aadhaar, and Mobile — is the plumbing for Direct Benefit Transfer. DBT's claimed gains are the elimination of ghost and duplicate beneficiaries, reduced leakage, and faster transfer; the government's estimated savings figures should be cited with the caveat that they are contested by economists who argue much of the "saving" reflects definitional changes rather than plugged leakage.

The critical literature is examinable and improves any answer: exclusion errors from biometric authentication failure, especially for manual labourers and the elderly with worn fingerprints; connectivity failure in remote areas; the burden of last-mile banking correspondents; and the argument that DBT shifts transaction costs from the state to the poorest citizen. The K. S. Puttaswamy judgment upheld Aadhaar's constitutional validity for welfare subsidies under Section 7 while striking down mandatory linkage for private services such as bank accounts and mobile connections, and reading down Section 57.

9. Emerging Technologies in Governance

Artificial intelligence is being applied to grievance triage, crop-loss assessment through satellite imagery, tuberculosis screening, and traffic management. The governance concerns to raise: algorithmic opacity in decisions affecting rights, bias reproduced from historical data, and the absence of a statutory duty to explain an automated decision. Blockchain suits land registries, certificate verification and supply-chain traceability because of immutability and disintermediation, with pilots in several states. GIS and remote sensing underpin SVAMITVA, which uses drone surveys to issue property cards for inhabited rural land — a genuinely transformative use because it converts occupancy into bankable title. Big data analytics supports the GST Network's fraud detection.

Also know e-participation and open government data (data.gov.in), social audit as practised under MGNREGA, and the concept of presumption of openness that links e-governance to the RTI regime.

10. Barriers, Risks and the Way Forward

Structure the obstacles rather than listing them randomly:

  • Infrastructural — patchy rural broadband, unreliable electricity, device affordability.
  • The digital divide — along rural/urban, gender (a substantial gap in mobile internet use), income, language (most content remains English-heavy) and disability lines. Accessibility standards for persons with disabilities are mandated under the RPwD Act but weakly enforced.
  • Human and organisational — low digital literacy among both citizens and officials, resistance to change because transparency reduces discretionary rents, weak capacity for procurement of complex IT systems, and high staff turnover.
  • Process — digitisation layered over unreformed procedures; siloed departmental databases that defeat interoperability.
  • Legal and security — data protection, cybersecurity, and the risk of surveillance and function creep when databases are linked.

The governing legal framework: the Information Technology Act, 2000 (with Section 66A struck down in Shreya Singhal for vagueness and overbreadth, and Section 69A upheld for blocking), the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 with its data principal/fiduciary vocabulary and the Data Protection Board, the Aadhaar Act, 2016, and the RTI Act as the transparency counterpart. CERT-In handles incident response and NCIIPC protects critical information infrastructure.

For a concluding paragraph, the durable prescription is: re-engineer before you digitise; design for the least connected citizen rather than the most; keep an assisted and offline channel available as a right, not a concession; mandate interoperability through open APIs and standards; build capacity alongside technology; and anchor the whole in enforceable data protection so that efficiency does not purchase itself with privacy.

Trap watch. E-government is service delivery; e-governance is relational and structural change. CSCs are the last-mile access layer, SWAN the connectivity layer, SDC the storage layer — do not swap them. Digital India subsumed NeGP; it did not create the Mission Mode Projects, which predate it. DigiLocker documents have legal validity under the IT Act. Puttaswamy permitted Aadhaar for Section 7 subsidies but barred mandatory private-sector linkage. Section 66A of the IT Act is void; Section 69A survives.
