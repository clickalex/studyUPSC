4. The Two Houses Compared: Federalism, Function, and the Rajya Sabha Question

The Lok Sabha–Rajya Sabha relationship is India's distinctive answer to bicameralism's universal question — why should the unelected-house equivalent check the people's house at all? — and the answer runs through federalism, not aristocracy. The Rajya Sabha represents States and Union Territories (233 elected by State MLAs through single-transferable-vote proportional representation, plus 12 nominated for distinction in arts, science, literature and social service), with seats apportioned roughly by population (Uttar Pradesh 31, Maharashtra 19, the smallest States 1 each) and one-third retiring every two years across six-year terms — continuity by design, since the House is never dissolved. Its federal logic is visible in its exclusive powers: Article 249 (empowering Parliament to legislate on State List matters in national interest, by two-thirds majority), Article 312 (creating new All-India Services), and the emergency-approval function when the Lok Sabha stands dissolved. Its subordinate logic is equally visible: money bills bypass it (14 days for recommendations only), no-confidence is Lok Sabha-only, and joint sittings (Article 108) weight numbers toward the Lower House's 543 against the Upper House's 245 — which is why all three historical joint sittings (Dowry Prohibition, 1961; Banking Service Commission, 1978; POTA, 2002) ended in government victories.

The Rajya Sabha's contested legitimacy Organises its modern history. The domicile abolition (2003 amendment to the Representation of the People Act — candidates need no longer be electors of the State they represent) plus open ballot (MLAs showing marked ballots to party agents, to prevent cross-voting) converted Rajya Sabha elections from federal representation into party arithmetic — critics call members \"party nominees parked in States they have never lived in,\" and Kuldip Nayar v. Union (2006) upheld the changes 4:1 while conceding the federalism concern. The nomination function (Article 80(1)(a)) supplies the counter-case: Sachin Tendulkar, M.S. Swaminathan, Shabana Azmi, Javed Akhtar, Mary Kom — distinguished Indians entering legislation without electoral machinery — though attendance-and-participation critiques recur with every celebrity nomination. And the Upper-House-as-check function operates most visibly when the government lacks its majority there: the 2014–2020 period (land-acquisition ordinance lapses, the farm-law passage controversy of September 2020 — voice vote amid pandemonium, the Deputy Chairman's conduct questioned) demonstrated both the check's reality and its fragility. The examination's portable comparison is that the Rajya Sabha is weaker than the US Senate (no advice-and-consent, no equal-State representation) but stronger than the British Lords (real legislative veto on ordinary and constitutional bills, federal exclusives, no Parliament-Act override) — a middle power by design.

The composition freeze underlies all of this: Lok Sabha seats frozen on the 1971 Census (42nd Amendment, extended by the 84th to the first Census after 2026 — now Census 2027, reference March 2027), so southern States' family-planning success is not punished by representation loss while northern growth goes unrewarded. Delimitation after 2027 will re-litigate the federation's most sensitive arithmetic — Hindi-belt gains versus southern fears, the women's-reservation (106th Amendment, 2023) implementation sequencing (census first, then delimitation, then the one-third quota) — and every parliament-composition answer should carry the freeze as its closing context: the House's numbers are a 1971 photograph of a 2027 country.

5. How Laws Are Actually Made: Stages, Committees, and the Money-Bill Controversy

The textbook legislative journey — first reading (introduction, with President's prior recommendation for money and 117(3) financial bills), second reading (general discussion, committee scrutiny, clause-by-clause consideration), third reading (passage), second-House repetition, Presidential assent under Article 111 (assent, withhold, or return non-money bills once; suspensive veto overridable by repassage; no pocket-veto time limit — Zail Singh's 1986 postal bill remains the type-case) — is accurate and almost never the whole story. The real legislature is the committee system. Departmentally Related Standing Committees (24 since 1993 — 16 Lok Sabha-serviced, 8 Rajya Sabha-serviced, each with 31 members across Houses) scrutinise demands for grants, Bills referred, and policy subjects; the financial trio — PAC (22 members, Opposition-chair convention, CAG reports as raw material), Estimates (30, Lok Sabha-only, efficiency-and-economy review), Public Undertakings (22, PSU performance) — supplies the accountability spine; and select/joint committees (the farm-law JPC demand, the Data Protection Bill's JPC, the Waqf Amendment Bill's JPC in 2024–25) handle politically charged legislation. Committee proceedings are closed-door and consensus-seeking — which is precisely why they work: party whips relax, expertise speaks, and reports carry cross-party authority that floor debate cannot match.

The system's decay-indicators are quantitative and quotable: Bills referred to committees collapsed from 60–70 per cent in the 14th–15th Lok Sabhas to under 15 per cent in the 16th–17th (the 17th Lok Sabha passed 35 per cent of Bills with under an hour's debate — PRS Legislative Research data); ordinances repromulgated to bypass scrutiny (D.C. Wadhwa, 1987, and Krishna Kumar Singh, 2017, limiting repromulgation without condemning the route); and debate-time diverted to disruption (the productivity percentages that open every session's press coverage). Against this, the money-bill controversy is the constitutional cutting edge: Article 110's closed list (taxation, borrowing, Consolidated Fund custody and appropriation, charged expenditure, audit — clauses (a) to (g)) plus the Speaker's \"final\" certificate created a Rajya Sabha-bypass route, and the Aadhaar Act (2016), the Finance Act 2017's tribunal-restructuring Part XIV, and the PMLA amendments travelled it. Rojer Mathew (2019) doubted the Aadhaar majority's Article 110 reading, held Speaker certification judicially reviewable in cases of constitutional illegality, and referred the interpretation to a seven-judge bench (listed 2023, pending) — so the live law is that certification is reviewable in principle while the substantive test awaits its final articulation. The examination point is structural: money-bill certification is where procedure becomes power — a Speaker's label deciding whether federalism's House gets a vote.

6. Accountability Technologies: Questions, Motions, Money, and Privilege

Parliament holds the executive accountable through four technologies of escalating force, and the escalation order is the lesson. Questions are the daily instrument: Question Hour's first-hour triad (starred — oral answer plus supplementaries; unstarred — written; short-notice — urgent matters with shortened timelines) extracts information and commits ministers on record, while Zero Hour (the Indian innovation — unauthorized, unlisted matters raised after Question Hour) ventilates urgency without notice. The data point to carry is that starred-question answerability correlates with governance quality more reliably than any other procedural metric — ministries that dread supplementaries prepare files. Motions and debates escalate: calling-attention (ministerial statement), adjournment (censure-tinged discussion displacing business), censure (individual minister), no-confidence (Lok Sabha only, against the whole Council of Ministers — consigning the government if carried; the 2023 motion's debate-without-danger as the confident-majority type-case), privilege motions, and substantive motions on policy. Money control is the structural instrument: the Budget as Annual Financial Statement (112), demands for grants voted by the Lok Sabha alone, cut motions (policy — ₹1; economy — specified sum; token — ₹100) as the Opposition's dissent vocabulary, the Appropriation Bill (114), the guillotine (undebated demands voted en bloc on the last day — efficiency purchased with scrutiny), and the charged-versus-voted distinction (112(3) charged items — President's emoluments, judges' salaries, debt charges, tribunal decrees — discussable but not votable). The Consolidated Fund (266(1)), Contingency Fund (267, held by the President/Finance Secretary, advances for unforeseen expenditure), and Public Account (266(2), provident funds and small savings — executive-operated) complete the fiscal constitution.

Privilege (Articles 105 for Parliament, 194 for States) is the accountability technology turned inward — the House's power to protect its own functioning: freedom of speech in the House (105(1)), publication immunity (105(2)), and the undefined \"other privileges\" (105(3) — the pre-1978 reference to Commons privileges, now read as evolved Indian practice). The live law was rewritten by Sita Soren (2024), seven judges, which overruled P.V. Narasimha Rao (1998): legislators taking bribes to vote or speak enjoy no immunity — bribery is not \"in respect of\" legislative speech but its betrayal, and the Court expressly buried the doctrine that had shielded the 1993 JMM-votes scandal. Paired with the Tenth Schedule's anti-defection machinery (paragraph 2 disqualification for voluntary giving-up or whip-defiance; paragraph 4 merger protection at two-thirds; Speaker/Chairman as adjudicator with Kihoto Hollohan (1992) reviewability and Keisham Meghachandra (2020)'s 90-day outer guideline; the Maharashtra 2023–24 Speaker-delay litigation as the live stress test), privilege now frames a coherent doctrine: speech protected, vote-sale prosecuted, defection disqualified — the three rules that keep the House simultaneously free, honest and stable. Quorum (one-tenth), the six-month summoning gap (85), language and voting rules, and the new Parliament House's 2023 inauguration (Sengol installed, 888-seat Lok Sabha chamber built for post-delimitation strength) supply the institutional furniture around the doctrine.

7. Decline-and-Reform: Productivity, Disruption, and the Opposition Question

The \"decline of Parliament\" is the most debated verdict in Indian legislative studies, and the evidence must be held in both directions. The decline case is quantitative: sittings falling from 130-plus days yearly in the 1950s to 60–70 in recent Lok Sabhas; Bills passed with minimal debate (the 17th Lok Sabha's sub-one-hour 35 per cent); committee referrals collapsing below 15 per cent; ordinances and money-bill routes bypassing the Upper House; Question Hour disruptions; suspensions en masse (the December 2023 record — 141 MPs suspended across Houses, the highest in history); and Private Members' business withering (no private member's Bill passed since 1970 — Supriya Sadhana's 1968 passage is the last). The resilience case answers that output legitimacy survives: transformative legislation still passes (GST 2016, IBC 2016, Article 370 reorganisation 2019, the three criminal codes 2023, women's reservation 2023, Waqf 2025); debates that do occur (no-confidence 2023, Constitution-at-75 discussions) draw national audiences; and committees, when used, still produce consensus reports that governments accept. The mature synthesis — and the GS-2 sentence that scores — is that Parliament legislates adequately but deliberates inadequately: the voting machine works, the talking shop sputters, and legitimacy leaks through the gap between laws passed and reasons exchanged.

The reform agenda divides into procedural, structural and political tiers. Procedural: a fixed parliamentary calendar (the 1993-or-earlier demand — 110–120 sitting days guaranteed); guaranteed Opposition time (the British Opposition-day convention, Leaders-of-Opposition recognition statutes); mandatory committee referral (the Rules Committees' perennial recommendation); Prime Minister's Question Time (the Westminster weekly accountability ritual India lacks); and disruption-management (the 2023 suspensions reframed the question — automatic suspension rules versus presiding-officer discretion). Structural: the Office of Profit and disqualification-law clarity (the Lily Thomas 2013 immediate-disqualification rule, Section 8 RP Act, the never-enacted comprehensive criteria); simultaneous elections (the Kovind Committee's 2024 report — constitutional amendments plus logistics, the federalism objection as the live debate); and post-2027 delimitation (the representation arithmetic that will reshape every party's incentives). Political — the tier no rulebook reaches: an Opposition with numbers (the 18th Lok Sabha's restored Leader of Opposition after a decade's gap is the structural precondition for every accountability technology above), a Speaker-Conduct consensus (British Speakers resign party membership; India's Speakers keep it — the Tenth Schedule adjudication controversy flows from that difference), and a media-and-public attention economy that rewards committee reports as much as walkouts. Parliament's future will be decided less by rule amendments than by whether deliberation regains prestige — the variable no procedure can manufacture and every procedure assumes.
