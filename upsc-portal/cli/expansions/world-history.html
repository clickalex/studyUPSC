9. Revolutions Compared: French, Russian, and the Anatomy of Upheaval

Section 7's second PYQ anchor (French versus Russian — bourgeois versus proletarian triggers) is the syllabus's most-examined comparison, and it is won through structured pairing, not parallel narration. The French Revolution (1789–99): fiscal crisis (debt from the American war, the parlements' tax veto, the Estates-General's May-1789 deadlock) → bourgeois leadership (Sieyès's What Is the Third Estate?, the Tennis Court Oath, the Bastille as symbol) → radicalisation (the Declaration of the Rights of Man, the Civil Constitution of the Clergy, war with Austria–Prussia, the September Massacres, the Terror — Robespierre's virtue-and-terror, ~17,000 guillotined) → Thermidor and Bonaparte (the Directory's corruption, the 18-Brumaire coup 1799 — revolution devouring itself into empire). The Russian Revolutions (1917): the February collapse (bread riots, army mutiny, Nicholas's abdication — the bourgeois-democratic phase: Milyukov–Kerensky's Provisional Government, dual power with the soviets) → the October seizure (Lenin's April Theses — \"peace, land, bread\"; the Kornilov affair discrediting the right; the 25-October storming — the Military Revolutionary Committee's near-bloodless coup) → consolidation (Brest-Litovsk 1918, the Civil War 1918–21 — Reds versus Whites-plus-interventionists, War Communism to NEP 1921). The comparison runs on five axes: triggers (fiscal-constitutional versus war-collapse — both regimes bankrupted by war, French by the American war's debt, Russian by the Great War's slaughter); leadership (bourgeois-liberal versus vanguard-party — the Jacobins improvising ideology, the Bolsheviks executing doctrine); radicalisation (Terror-then-Thermidor versus Civil-War-then-NEP — both revolutions betraying their moderates, French through the guillotine, Russian through the Cheka); outcomes (Napoleonic code-plus-empire — equality before law exported by bayonet — versus Soviet state-plus-planning — the world's first claim to have abolished class); echoes (1789's liberty-equality-fraternity as liberalism's charter — Haiti 1791–1804 its radical child — versus 1917's anti-imperialism as decolonisation's accelerant — Ho Chi Minh at Tours 1920, M.N. Roy at Moscow).

The historiographical layer lifts the pairing into the top band. 1789 debated: Soboul's Marxist orthodoxy (bourgeois revolution against feudalism — the class-struggle classic) versus Cobban–Furet revisionism (no self-conscious bourgeoisie, no feudalism left to overthrow — a political-cultural crisis, discourse and contingency) versus the cultural turn (Hunt's political culture, Schama's Citizens — violence as choice, not necessity). 1917 debated: the Soviet hagiography (inevitable proletarian triumph) versus the liberal indictment (coup against a democratising February — Pipes) versus the revisionist social history (Fitzpatrick — revolution from below, workers–soldiers–peasants acting, Bolsheviks surfing) versus the contingency school (the sealed train, Kerensky's errors — October as close-run conjuncture). The examination's \"compare\" rewards the candidate who argues with the historiography visible: state the agreed facts (dates, phases, death-tolls), stage the rival readings (Marxist versus revisionist for both), and close with the candidate's own synthesis (both revolutions began against bankrupt old regimes, both were hijacked by war, both devoured their moderates — and both remade the world less by achieving their ideals than by institutionalising their vocabularies: rights-talk from Paris, planning-talk from Moscow).

10. The World Wars as Systems: Origins Debates, Turning Points, Settlements

Section 4 narrates both wars (MANIA, Schlieffen-to-Versailles, appeasement-to-atomic); this section systematises them — because Mains world-history questions are overwhelmingly \"why\" and \"compare,\" and systems answer whys. WWI origins stage the syllabus's great debate: the Versailles verdict (Article 231 — German war-guilt, the \"stab\" of reparations) → Fischer (1961 — Griff nach der Weltmacht — Germany's September-1914 war aims proving bid-for-mastery continuity, 1914-to-1939 as one arc) → Clark's sleepwalkers (2013 — no one guilty, all sleepwalking — the alliance rigidities, mobilisation timetables, Balkan tripwires — contingency against determinism). The examination-grade answer carries all three plus the structural floor (alliance blocs — Triple Alliance versus Triple Entente; naval arms-racing — Tirpitz versus Fisher; imperial friction — Morocco 1905/1911, Baghdad railway; nationalist kindling — Serbia–Austria, the Sarajevo spark 28 June 1914) and the Indian hinge (the war's colonial dividend-demanded: 1.3 million Indian troops, the Lucknow Pact 1916, the Montagu Declaration 1917 — self-determination promised in Wilson's Fourteen Points, January 1918, and denied at Versailles — the betrayal that radicalised the national movement). WWI settlement: Versailles's trilemma (punish Germany, honour self-determination, build collective security — achieving none fully: reparations unpayable — Keynes's Economic Consequences, 1919, as contemporary demolition; Mittel-Europa's successor states as grievance-warehouses; the League disarmed — America absent, enforcement absent).

WWII origins restage the debate with higher stakes: Versailles-dissatisfaction (the \"unfinished\" thesis — the 20-year armistice, Foch's prophecy) → appeasement (Munich 1938 — Chamberlain's deterrence-failure versus the revisionist buying-time-for-rearmament — the documentary duel) → Lebensraum ideology (Hitler's Mein Kampf programme — the intentionalist spine: war as plan, not accident) → League system-failure (Manchuria 1931, Abyssinia 1935 — sanctions unapplied, aggression unpriced). The course condenses to turning-point logic (Stalingrad 1942–43 — the Eastern Front as the war's graveyard, two-thirds of German losses; Midway 1942 — the Pacific pivot; El Alamein 1942 — the Mediterranean close; Overlord 6 June 1944 — the second front; Hiroshima–Nagasaki 6/9 August 1945 — the atomic end — Alperovitz's revisionism — diplomacy-toward-Moscow — versus the invasion-casualty defence). WWII settlement: Yalta–Potsdam's spheres (the Cold War's conception — disagreement institutionalised), Nuremberg (crimes-against-humanity jurisprudence — the individual-liability revolution), the UN (1945 — Security-Council veto as great-power realism inside collective-security idealism), Bretton Woods (dollar-gold, IMF–World Bank — the economic constitution), and decolonisation's trigger (empires bankrupted, legitimacy spent, the Atlantic Charter's promises weaponised by the colonised — §3's waves as WWII's consequence). The two-wars synthesis: WWI destroyed the old order without building a new one (Versailles's trilemma failed); WWII destroyed more completely and built durably (UN–Bretton Woods–decolonisation–Cold War as the 1945 settlement — the order still half-standing).

11. Cold War to Multipolarity: Doctrines, Crises, and India's Navigation

Section 5 sketches containment-to-unipolarity and Section 6 continues to the present; this section supplies the doctrinal spine — because Cold-War questions reward the candidate who names the doctrine behind each crisis. Containment (Kennan's Long Telegram 1946 — \"patient but firm\"; Truman Doctrine 1947 — aid to Greece–Turkey as universalised anti-communism; Marshall Plan 1948 — $13 billion of recovery-as-strategy; NATO 1949 — collective defence Article 5; NSC-68 1950 — the militarisation blueprint) met its mirror (Cominform 1947, Comecon 1949, Warsaw Pact 1955 — the Soviet glacis institutionalised). The crisis sequence reads as doctrine tested: Berlin Blockade 1948–49 (airlift vindicating resolve), Korea 1950–53 (containment's first hot war — MacArthur's rollback fantasy fired by Truman — limited war's doctrine born), Cuba 1962 (the apex — thirteen days, quarantine-versus-strike, the Kennedy–Khrushchev backchannel — crisis-management as the superpower skill, the hotline its hardware), Vietnam 1955–75 (containment's graveyard — domino theory's failure, McNamara's body-count modernism defeated by Giap's protracted war — the lesson that broke American certainty), Afghanistan 1979–89 (the mirror-graveyard — the USSR's Vietnam, America's Stinger-proxied revenge — blowback's textbook), détente (SALT I 1972–ABM–Helsinki 1975 — arms-control plus human-rights-basket — the Dual-Track 1979 and Second Cold War 1979–85 — the Euromissile crisis — before Gorbachev's \"new thinking\" — glasnost–perestroika–Sinatra-doctrine — unintentionally dissolving the empire: 1989's revolutions, the Wall 9 November 1989, the USSR's Christmas-1991 end).

The end debated (Reagan-pressure — SDI plus the arms-race the USSR couldn't afford — versus Soviet-rot — the command economy's stagnation, Afghanistan's bleeding, the nationalities waking — versus contingency — Gorbachev-the-accidental-liquidator, without whom stagnation might have persisted decades) leads to the unipolar-to-multipolar arc: the 1990s unipolar moment (Iraq 1991 — the UN-authorised coalition; liberal internationalism's zenith — EU Maastricht 1992, WTO 1995, the \"end of history\" mood) → post-9/11 reordering (Afghanistan 2001, Iraq 2003 — the unipolar overreach that discredited interventionism) → multipolar contestation (China's rise — WTO 2001, BRI 2013, the South-China-Sea grey-zone; Russian revisionism — Crimea 2014, Ukraine 2022 — the 2025 diplomatic thaw unsettled; the Global South's NAM-2.0 — de-dollarisation narratives, BRICS expansion — Egypt–Ethiopia–Iran and more; Gaza 2023–25 reshaping UN/ICJ terrain). India's navigation threads the whole arc: non-alignment (Bandung 1955 — Belgrade 1961 — Nehru–Tito–Nasser–Sukarno–Nkrumah — conscience plus room-for-manoeuvre) → the 1971 tilt (Indo-Soviet treaty — alignment without alliance) → post-Cold-War multi-alignment (QUAD + BRICS + SCO simultaneously — the essay-bank's \"method, not ideology\" — strategic autonomy as portfolio management: G20 presidency 2023 with AU induction, IMEC–Chabahar connectivity geometry, the reform pitch — UNSC expansion, WTO revival — as enlightened self-interest). The Cold-War-to-multipolarity answer-formula: doctrine → crisis → test-result → India's position — four beats, every time.

12. The World-History Answer Method: Mains-Only Quality, PYQ Shapes, Source Discipline

The host's orienting note (Mains-only — quality, not trivia) is the method's foundation: world-history answers are arguments with dates, never chronicles with opinions. The answer architecture runs: thesis-first opening (the \"why\" answered in sentence one — \"WWI's origins lie less in German design than in alliance rigidity weaponised by mobilisation timetables\" — Fischer situated, Clark centred, candidate judging); three-to-four analytical paragraphs (each a cause-cluster or comparison-axis with its dated evidence — Sarajevo 28 June 1914, Versailles Article 231, Munich 1938, Stalingrad 1942–43, Cuba 1962, the Wall 9 November 1989 — dates as argument-punctuation, roughly one per paragraph, never lists); the historiographical flash (one debate named per answer — Fischer-versus-Clark, Soboul-versus-Furet, orthodox-versus-revisionist Cold-War origins — the flash proving the candidate reads history as argument); and the India-link close (the Lucknow Pact, Bandung, multi-alignment — world history bent homeward in the final two lines, because the GS-1 examiner prices the India connection). Diagrams are rare but telling: the alliance-bloc sketch (1914, 1949), the decolonisation-wave timeline (1947–1994), the doctrine-crisis flowchart — one where it compresses, never decoration.

PYQ shapes (§7's anchors) reduce to four recurring moulds, each with its template. Effects-on-India/colonies (\"WWI's effects on colonies and self-determination\"): mechanism-chain (troops-and-taxes → promises — Montagu 1917, Fourteen Points January 1918 → betrayal — Rowlatt, Versailles's colonial silence → radicalisation — Non-Cooperation's 1920 launch) — cause-tracing with the betrayal as pivot. Compare-revolutions/regimes (\"French versus Russian,\" \"Italian versus German unification\"): axis-table (five axes — triggers, leadership, radicalisation, outcomes, echoes — §9's template) — pairing, never parallel narration. Weigh-a-proposition (\"Africa violently, Asia constitutionally\"): refine-and-weigh (grant the pattern — Algeria 1954–62 versus India 1947 — then complicate: Vietnam's war, Kenya's Mau Mau, Indonesia's 1945–49 war on the Asian side; Ghana 1957, Senegal, Tanzania peaceful on the African — the proposition half-true, violence correlating with settler-presence and metropolitan intransigence, not continent). Industrial-impact (\"Industrial Revolution's impact on Indian handicrafts\"): the deindustrialisation debate staged (Dutt–Bagchi — tariff-tyranny plus Lancashire competition, the Dhaka-muslin case — versus Morris–Roy sceptics — productivity gaps, the mill counter-evidence — with the candidate's weighed verdict). Source discipline (§8): Norman Lowe as the narrative floor (one chapter per unit, read twice), NCERT Themes (Class XI) as the Indian-perspective supplement, Joll (or Clark/Furet chapters) as the historiographical ceiling — one debate-source per unit maximum, because Mains rewards one deployed debate per answer, not a bibliography. The method's close: world history is the GS-1 paper's quality differentiator — attempted by all, argued by few — and the candidate who opens with a thesis, punctuates with dates, flashes one debate, and lands the India-link writes in the few's company.
