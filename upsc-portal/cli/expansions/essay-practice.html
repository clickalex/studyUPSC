The Paper Has Changed: Both Sections Are Now Philosophical

The single most consequential fact about the Essay paper in the last three cycles is that the old division between the two sections has collapsed. For most of the period from 2013 to 2024, Section A carried the abstract and philosophical topics while Section B reliably produced something grounded — governance, economy, technology, society, agriculture — so a candidate could prepare two different essays and choose. In 2025 (paper written on 22 August) Section A offered "Truth knows no colour", "The supreme art of war is to subdue the enemy without fighting", "Thought finds a world and creates one also" and "Best lessons are learnt through bitter experiences"; Section B offered "Muddy water is best cleared by leaving it alone", "The years teach much which the days never know", "It is best to see life as a journey, not as a destination" and "Contentment is natural wealth; luxury is artificial poverty". In 2026 (21 August) Section A offered "Oxymorons reflect the ironies of life", "A grateful mind is very beautiful", "A thorn is a changed bud" and "When two elephants fight, it is the grass that gets trampled"; Section B offered "Nature is the symbol of the spirit", "A well-educated mind will always have more questions than answers", "Shelving hard decisions is the least ethical course" and "A good leader is one who follows the followers". That is the second consecutive year with no socio-economic topic in either section. The form is unchanged — 250 marks, two essays of 125 each, four topics per section, 1,000–1,200 words each, three hours — but the demand has shifted decisively from information to interpretation.

Two registers nonetheless survive inside the philosophical field, and recognising them is the first act of selection. The abstract–literary register ("A thorn is a changed bud", "Nature is the symbol of the spirit", "Oxymorons reflect the ironies of life") rewards voice, imagery and control of transitions, and punishes data-dumping. The applied–ethical register ("When two elephants fight, it is the grass that gets trampled", "Shelving hard decisions is the least ethical course", "A good leader is one who follows the followers") still admits the General Studies arsenal — great-power rivalry and small states, centre–state friction and trade wars; decision paralysis and moral courage in administration; representation against statesmanship, populism and servant leadership — but it admits it only as illustration of an argument, never as the argument itself. Candidates with strong GS content should choose from the applied register; candidates with a genuine reflective voice should not be frightened of the abstract one. What must be abandoned is the strategy of preparing a ready-made "women and empowerment" or "artificial intelligence" essay and bending a topic to fit it: examiners see hundreds of the same pre-fabricated essay, and the bend is visible in the first paragraph.

Five Argument Architectures That Fit Abstract Topics

An abstract topic is not a licence to roam; it is a request for a spine. Five architectures cover almost the whole field, and each should be rehearsed twice before the examination so that the choice takes seconds rather than minutes.

The definitional–dialectical spine opens by defining the topic's key term in your own words, states the proposition the topic seems to advance, tests it against a counter-case, and resolves. It suits paradoxes and transformed metaphors — "A thorn is a changed bud" asks whether adversity is a change of substance or only of appearance, and the essay earns its marks by refusing the comfortable reading. The concentric-circles spine moves outward from the self to family and community, then the state, then the world, then time or the cosmos; it is the natural fit for value statements such as "A grateful mind is very beautiful" or "Nature is the symbol of the spirit", and its danger is that the circles become a list unless one claim is restated at each scale in a different form. The historical-arc spine traces an idea through three epochs — pre-modern, colonial or industrial, and contemporary digital — and shows what persisted and what changed; it produces depth without needing data. The domain-sweep spine is the familiar political–economic–social–technological–ecological–ethical sweep, legitimate only when a single argument threads the domains, since unthreaded it degenerates into the very "GS answer in essay clothes" that is marked down. The case-led inductive spine opens with two concrete vignettes, derives the principle from them, and then tests the principle's limits; it is the strongest choice for applied topics like "When two elephants fight, it is the grass that gets trampled", where the small-state, small-farmer or small-business case supplies the induction.

Two anti-patterns deserve naming because they account for most sub-100 essays. The quote museum strings together four or five quotations with connective sentences and no thesis of its own; a quotation is evidence of reading only if it is then argued with. The GS transplant imports headings, subheadings, statistics and a "way forward" paragraph wholesale; it is legible and it scores like a General Studies answer because it reads like one. Flowing prose with visible paragraph transitions is what an essay is.

The Dimension Bank, the Reading Diet and the Example Notebook

The example notebook prescribed in Section 5 needs one addition for a philosophical paper: alongside the six species of example, store interpretation cards. Each card carries a claim, the lens it belongs to, one illustration and — crucially — one counter-illustration, so that the argument can be tested rather than merely decorated. Ten cards per theme, across eight themes (self and character; knowledge and doubt; justice and mercy; power and restraint; nature and limits; technology and human agency; time and change; leadership and followership), is a preparation that survives any topic the Commission sets, because these eight themes are what the last decade of topics has actually been about.

The reading diet should be small, repeated and Indian-anchored rather than wide and skimmed. One essayist read closely — Montaigne, Orwell, or Tagore's essays — teaches the reflective voice better than any handbook. One Indian political text held at hand, from Hind Swaraj, Annihilation of Caste, Nationalism, Vivekananda's Chicago addresses or Radhakrishnan's essays, supplies the moral vocabulary that abstract topics demand and that a purely Western diet cannot. One scientist who writes well — Rachel Carson, Peter Medawar, Lewis Thomas, Siddhartha Mukherjee — supplies the discipline of argument from evidence. One biography of an administrator, a scientist or a reformer supplies the case material that vignettes need. To these add three fixed reference points: the Preamble and the Directive Principles, two or three paragraphs from judgments whose reasoning is genuinely philosophical (Puttaswamy on privacy and dignity, Navtej Johar on constitutional morality, M.K. Ranjitsinh on balancing renewables against a species), and a handful of passages from the Upanishads, the Gita and the Dhammapada, which supply the transformation-and-attachment imagery that topics like "a thorn is a changed bud" are built on.

Two drills make the bank usable under a clock. The first is the ten-second skim already prescribed: after writing, read only the first and last paragraphs and the first sentence of each body paragraph, and ask whether a stranger could reconstruct the argument. The second is handwriting stamina — 1,100 legible words in eighty-five minutes is a physical skill, and it is lost by candidates who practise only on keyboards until November. Timed longhand, weekly, is non-negotiable.

Diagnosing the Score: What Separates 120 from 80

Essay marking is not published as a rubric, but the bands are consistent enough to be described, and describing them is what makes feedback usable. An essay in the 120-plus band has one thesis visible in the first paragraph, restated in transformed form in the last; five or six dimensions woven into a single argument rather than listed; at least one serious counter-view engaged and answered rather than gestured at; illustrations that are specific and, where personal, generalised within the paragraph; and closure that reframes the topic. An essay at 100–115 has all of this except the counter-view, or has the counter-view and mechanical transitions. At 80–95 the essay is either the GS transplant — headings, data, no argument — or the memoir — anecdote, sentiment, no claim. Below 80 the topic has drifted, the quotations are carrying the essay, or it is unfinished.

Three fixes have the highest leverage and should be applied in order. First, write the thesis as a single sentence before drafting and test every paragraph against it; a paragraph that cannot be connected to that sentence is cut, however good it is. Second, install one genuine counter-view in the second half and answer it — this is the most reliable single marker of a mature essay and the most commonly absent. Third, close by reframing, not by summarising: the last paragraph should say something the first paragraph could not have said. Beyond these, the selection discipline matters more than candidates think — choose the topic you can argue, not the one you know most about, and never attempt a topic whose key term you cannot define in one line.

The final eight weeks should narrow rather than widen: one full paper a week under a three-hour clock, one rewrite of the weaker essay the next morning from memory, and consolidation of the notebook into the twenty quotations and thirty examples the programme already prescribes, plus the interpretation cards. The same notebook then serves the personality test, which asks reflective questions of exactly the kind the paper now sets.

Trap watch. The word limit is 1,000–1,200 words per essay, two essays, one from each section, 125 marks each — writing 1,600 words on the first essay forfeits the second. Since 2025 both sections have been philosophical and the 2026 paper contained no socio-economic topic at all, so preparing a stock governance essay for "Section B" is the most common preparation error of this cycle. Headings, subheadings and bullet points are permitted but make an essay read like a General Studies answer; flowing prose with sentence transitions is what is rewarded. Quotations are not scored as knowledge, and a misattribution is worse than silence: "Be the change you wish to see in the world" and "An eye for an eye leaves the whole world blind" do not appear in Gandhi's writings, and "I disapprove of what you say, but I will defend to the death your right to say it" is Evelyn Beatrice Hall writing about Voltaire. "Injustice anywhere is a threat to justice everywhere" is genuine — King's 1963 Birmingham letter. Personal anecdotes are allowed but must be generalised inside the paragraph, and an invented story with specific detail is the easiest inconsistency for an examiner to catch. The essay must be written in the medium declared in the application; switching language mid-paper is penalised. Finally, allocation: roughly fifteen minutes of selection and blueprinting, eighty-five to ninety minutes per essay, and ten minutes of polish — not two hours on the first topic and forty minutes on the second.
