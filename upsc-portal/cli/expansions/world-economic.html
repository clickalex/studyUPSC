Global Resource Distribution and Economic Activity

Economic activity is conventionally classified into primary (extraction — agriculture, mining, fishing, forestry), secondary (manufacturing and construction), tertiary (services) and, in modern usage, quaternary (knowledge, research, information) and quinary (high-level decision-making). The Clark-Fisher sequence holds that as economies develop, employment shifts from primary through secondary to tertiary — a progression India partially bypassed.

Agricultural systems to distinguish: shifting cultivation (slash and burn, known as jhum in north-east India, milpa in Central America, ladang in Indonesia, chena in Sri Lanka) — sustainable only at low population densities and long fallow; intensive subsistence agriculture of monsoon Asia, dominated by wet rice, small holdings and heavy labour input; plantation agriculture, a colonial legacy of single-crop, capital-intensive estates producing tea, coffee, rubber, oil palm and sugarcane for export, and hence vulnerable to price volatility; extensive commercial grain farming of the Prairies, Pampas and Steppes, highly mechanised with low yields per hectare but very high output per worker; mixed farming of Western Europe; dairying, requiring cool moist climates and proximity to urban markets; market gardening or truck farming near cities; and nomadic herding and commercial livestock ranching. Note the standard contrast: intensive subsistence has high yield per hectare and low yield per person; extensive commercial farming is the reverse.

Mineral and energy geography. Iron ore reserves and production centre on Australia, Brazil, China, India and Russia; the great iron and steel districts historically located on coalfields (Ruhr, Pittsburgh, Donbas) but, as coal inputs per tonne fell and ore came to be imported, shifted to coastal sites — Japan being the classic case of an industry built entirely on imported ore and coal, located at tidewater. Copper is dominated by Chile and Peru; bauxite by Australia, Guinea and China; gold by China, Australia and Russia. Critical minerals now carry strategic weight: lithium concentrated in the "lithium triangle" of Chile, Argentina and Bolivia plus Australia; cobalt overwhelmingly in the Democratic Republic of Congo; rare earths dominated by China in both mining and, more decisively, processing. This concentration is the geopolitical driver behind India's Critical Mineral Mission, KABIL, and the Mineral Security Partnership.

Energy: conventional crude oil concentrated in the Persian Gulf, Russia, the United States and Venezuela; the US shale revolution transforming it into a net exporter and weakening OPEC's pricing power; natural gas in Russia, Iran, Qatar and the US, with the LNG trade decoupling gas from pipeline geography; coal in China, India, the US, Australia and Indonesia. Renewable geography follows physical endowment — solar in the desert belt, wind in coastal and plains corridors, hydro in mountainous well-watered regions, geothermal along plate boundaries in Iceland, Kenya's Rift and the Philippines.

Industrial Location, Global Value Chains and Trade Routes

Weber's least-cost theory holds that industry locates to minimise transport, labour and agglomeration costs. The material index — the ratio of localised raw material weight to product weight — determines orientation: an index above one means weight-losing raw materials and location near the source (iron and steel, sugar, cement, copper smelting); below one means weight-gaining and location near the market (bottled beverages, brewing, assembly). Footloose industries — electronics, software, precision goods — have low transport sensitivity and locate on skills, amenity and connectivity. Agglomeration economies explain clustering: shared labour pools, specialised suppliers and knowledge spillovers, as in Silicon Valley, Bengaluru, Shenzhen and the Third Italy industrial districts.

Global value chains are the organising fact of modern production: a single product is designed in one country, its components made in several others, assembled in a low-cost location and sold worldwide. The smile curve captures the distribution of value — high at the two ends in R&D, design and branding on one side, and marketing, distribution and after-sales on the other, and lowest in the middle, in physical assembly. This is precisely why India's mobile-phone assembly success generates large export numbers but modest domestic value addition, and why the policy goal is to move up the curve into component manufacture and design. Reshoring, nearshoring and friend-shoring are the post-pandemic and post-Ukraine responses to supply-chain fragility, and "China plus one" is the diversification strategy India seeks to capture.

Development, Inequality and the Global Economic Order

The North-South divide, popularised by the Brandt Line, remains a useful shorthand but is increasingly blurred by the rise of China, the Gulf economies, Southeast Asia and India. Explanations for divergence to marshal in an answer: colonial extraction and the entrenchment of commodity-exporting economies; institutional quality and property rights; human capital; geography, including tropical disease burden and landlockedness; the resource curse or paradox of plenty, whereby resource wealth produces Dutch disease, rent-seeking and conflict rather than development; and terms-of-trade deterioration for primary exporters, the Prebisch-Singer hypothesis.

Measures beyond GDP: GNI per capita in PPP terms, which corrects for price differences; the HDI; the Gini coefficient and the Lorenz curve; the MPI; and the World Bank's income classification into low, lower-middle, upper-middle and high income — India being lower-middle income and facing the middle-income trap, the risk of losing low-wage competitiveness before acquiring innovation capacity.

Institutions of the global economic order: the IMF for balance of payments support and surveillance, with quota-based voting and SDRs; the World Bank Group (IBRD, IDA for the poorest countries on concessional terms, IFC for the private sector, MIGA for guarantees, ICSID for disputes); the WTO for trade rules and dispute settlement, currently hampered by the paralysis of its Appellate Body; the OECD; UNCTAD, created as the developing world's forum; and the newer institutions reflecting a shifting order — the New Development Bank of the BRICS and the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank. Groupings to place accurately: G7, G20 (which India chaired in 2023, securing permanent African Union membership), BRICS and its expansion, OPEC and OPEC+, ASEAN, the EU as the deepest integration, USMCA, Mercosur, the African Continental Free Trade Area, CPTPP and RCEP.

Contemporary fault lines worth citing: supply-chain weaponisation and export controls in semiconductors; the energy transition and critical mineral competition; the carbon border adjustment mechanism, which developing countries read as green protectionism; debt distress in low-income countries and the Common Framework's slow restructuring; the fragmentation of the trading system into blocs; and demands for climate finance, including the loss and damage fund, and for reform of the multilateral development banks.

Trap watch. A material index above one means weight-losing material and raw-material-oriented location. Intensive subsistence farming has high yield per hectare but low yield per worker; extensive commercial grain farming is the opposite. The smile curve puts assembly at the bottom of value addition. The Suez Canal has no locks; Panama does. IDA lends concessionally to the poorest, IBRD on near-market terms. The EEZ and trade chokepoints are separate concepts — Malacca and Hormuz are straits, Suez and Panama are canals. The Prebisch-Singer hypothesis concerns declining terms of trade for primary exporters.
