6. Why the Regime Changed Shape: From Kyoto's Quotas to Paris's Ratchet

The treaty chain is best learned as a sequence of design failures and corrections, because every feature of the Paris Agreement exists as an answer to something that broke under Kyoto. Kyoto (1997) was top-down: it fixed binding emission targets for Annex-I parties, created a compliance committee with real teeth, and invented three flexible mechanisms — the Clean Development Mechanism, Joint Implementation and emissions trading — so that reductions could be bought where they were cheapest. The logic was sound and the arithmetic was not. The United States signed but never ratified, the Senate having passed the Byrd-Hagel Resolution refusing any treaty that did not bind developing countries; China and India, as non-Annex-I parties, carried no targets at all; and Canada withdrew in 2011 rather than face non-compliance penalties. By the end of the first commitment period the parties carrying binding targets accounted for a rapidly shrinking share of global emissions, so the instrument could not, even if fully honoured, have moved the trajectory. The Doha Amendment created a thin second commitment period (2013–20) that few major emitters joined.

Meanwhile the science changed the question. Once cumulative emissions — a carbon budget — became the organising concept, a static allocation of quotas looked arbitrary and politically impossible to negotiate. Paris (2015, in force 2016) therefore inverted Kyoto's architecture: obligations became procedural rather than substantive. Every party must prepare, communicate and maintain a Nationally Determined Contribution, and must submit a progressively more ambitious one every five years (the ratchet, resting on "progression" and "highest possible ambition"), report through a single Enhanced Transparency Framework — the rulebook for which was completed at COP26 in 2021 — and submit to a Global Stocktake every five years. There are no sanctions for missing a self-set target; the enforcement mechanism is publicity. Differentiation survived but was re-worded: common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities now apply "in the light of different national circumstances", the formula that let China and India accept a universal structure without a binary Annex list. The temperature goal — "well below 2°C" with efforts toward 1.5°C — plus Article 2's adaptation and finance-flows limbs, and Article 6's market and non-market mechanisms (6.2 internationally transferred mitigation outcomes, 6.4 the central mechanism, 6.8 non-market approaches), completed the package; Article 6 was the last piece operationalised, at Glasgow and then Baku.

The design lesson is examinable in itself. Paris survived a second United States withdrawal — announced in January 2025 and effective a year later — precisely because the obligations are self-set and the machinery is transparent rather than punitive. A regime built on quotas collapses when a large emitter leaves; a regime built on disclosure and five-yearly comparison merely registers the absence.

7. The Recent Arc: the NCQG, Belém and the Shift from Targets to Implementation

COP29 at Baku (2024) replaced the old finance goal with the New Collective Quantified Goal: developed countries to provide at least $300 billion a year by 2035, with a call to scale total climate finance from all sources to $1.3 trillion a year. It also finally closed the Article 6 rulebook. The NCQG's weaknesses are as examinable as its number: no sub-target for adaptation, no grant-equivalence requirement, no widening of the contributor base beyond the 1992 Annex list, and a headline figure that developing-country delegations — India among them — called an optical illusion against a needs estimate several times larger.

COP30 at Belém (November 2025) was the first COP whose central text was about implementation rather than ambition. It adopted the Global Mutirão decision alongside a Belém Political Package that included a new Gender Action Plan; called for at least a tripling of adaptation finance by 2035; confirmed operationalisation and replenishment cycles for the Loss and Damage Fund; opened a two-year work programme on climate finance including Article 9.1; and launched two voluntary bodies — a Global Implementation Accelerator and the Belém Mission to 1.5. What it did not do was adopt a fossil-fuel roadmap. More than eighty countries backed Brazil's proposal, the draft carried it, and the adopted text fell back on reaffirming COP28's "UAE Consensus" language on transitioning away from fossil fuels. The presidency responded outside the formal decision: COP30 president André Corrêa do Lago announced two presidency-led roadmaps, one to halt and reverse deforestation and one on a just, orderly and equitable transition away from fossil fuels, while Colombia and the Netherlands undertook to co-host the first international conference on fossil-fuel phase-out in April 2026 — an initiative endorsed at the closing plenary but living beside the UNFCCC rather than inside it. The analytical reading is that the frontier of ambition has moved to coalitions of the willing, alongside instruments such as carbon border measures and the fossil-fuel non-proliferation treaty proposal.

COP31 in 2026 will be held at Antalya in Türkiye under an unusual shared presidency that ended a two-year deadlock inside the Western European and Others Group: Türkiye holds the presidency and the logistics, while Australia's climate and energy minister Chris Bowen presides over the negotiations, with a pre-COP in the Pacific and attention to a Pacific Resilience Fund. The arrangement matters for India because it makes the Pacific and small-island vulnerability a structural theme of the next round.

8. India's Position: the NDC Ladder and the Carbon Market

India's contributions form a ladder whose logic is worth understanding rather than memorising. NDC 1.0 (2015) promised a 33–35 per cent cut in emissions intensity of GDP from 2005 levels, 40 per cent non-fossil installed capacity and a 2.5–3 billion tonne carbon sink by 2030. NDC 2.0 (August 2022) raised intensity to 45 per cent and non-fossil capacity to 50 per cent by 2030 and added the Lifestyle for Environment strand. NDC 3.0, approved by the Union Cabinet on 25 March 2026 for submission covering 2031–35, commits to a 47 per cent intensity reduction from 2005 levels, 60 per cent non-fossil installed capacity by 2035 and a carbon sink of 3.5–4.0 billion tonnes of CO₂ equivalent, with qualitative commitments on resilient infrastructure, low-cost finance, capacity building and LiFE. The net-zero-by-2070 pledge from the Panchamrit at Glasgow sits alongside as a long-term low-emission development strategy, not as an NDC target — a distinction questions regularly probe.

Delivery has run ahead of two of the three targets. India reached its 2005–20 intensity goal early (a 36 per cent reduction by 2020 against a 33–35 per cent pledge) and crossed 50 per cent of installed capacity from non-fossil sources in 2025, five years before its own deadline: about 263 GW, or 51.5 per cent of installed capacity, by November 2025 and roughly 283 GW by March 2026, with record renewable additions of about 44.5 GW during 2025, solar approaching 133 GW and wind at 54 GW. The gap is therefore not capacity but absolute emissions, which continue to grow with demand — the point at which the carbon market enters.

The market's statutory base is the Energy Conservation (Amendment) Act 2022; the Carbon Credit Trading Scheme was notified in June 2023 with detailed compliance regulations in July 2024. The Bureau of Energy Efficiency, as administrator, notified obligated sectors and entities in April and June 2025, and sectoral greenhouse-gas intensity targets began to be notified from October 2025 with compliance obligations for FY2026–27; the first trading of Carbon Credit Certificates is expected on the power exchanges (IEX and PXIL) in October 2026. The institutional map is: Ministry of Power as nodal ministry, MoEFCC notifying emission targets, a National Steering Committee above them, BEE administering and issuing certificates, the Grid Controller of India Limited maintaining the registry, and the Central Electricity Regulatory Commission regulating trade.

Its design differs from the European model in one decisive respect. India's scheme is an intensity-based baseline-and-credit market: an entity earns one Carbon Credit Certificate per tonne of CO₂ equivalent by which it beats a notified intensity target (emissions per unit of output), and must surrender certificates for any shortfall. It absorbs the Perform, Achieve and Trade scheme's energy-intensive sectors in stages, covers Scope 1 and Scope 2 emissions and initially CO₂ and perfluorocarbons, permits unlimited banking but no borrowing, and allows exchange trading only at the outset. Because the constraint is per unit of output rather than absolute, the market can be fully successful and yet coincide with rising national emissions — which is consistent with an intensity-based NDC and is precisely the criticism raised against it.

On the science side, the Sixth Assessment cycle (2021–23) established the claims the regime now runs on: warming of about 1.1°C above 1850–1900 by 2011–20, human influence "unequivocal", a remaining carbon budget of the order of 500 GtCO₂ from 2020 for a 50 per cent chance of holding 1.5°C, and methane's global warming potential of roughly 27–30 over a century but about 80 over twenty years — the reason short-lived climate forcers are now a separate policy object. The seventh cycle began with the Bureau elections of July 2023; the Plenary in Istanbul in January 2024 settled on three Working Group contributions plus a Synthesis Report due by late 2029, with a Special Report on Climate Change and Cities and a Methodology Report on Short-lived Climate Forcers also in the cycle, and the Lima Plenary of October 2025 agreeing the content of a 2027 Methodology Report on carbon dioxide removal, carbon capture, utilisation and storage.

Trap watch. Kyoto bound only Annex-I parties, was never ratified by the United States, and lost Canada in 2011; Paris binds the process (submit, update, report) and not the outcome, which is why it survived a second US withdrawal. The $100 billion a year was the 2009 Copenhagen pledge; the Baku NCQG is $300 billion a year by 2035 from developed countries with a call for $1.3 trillion from all sources — the two numbers are constantly confused. Loss and damage was agreed at COP27 Sharm el-Sheikh (2022) and operationalised at COP28 Dubai (2023). Glasgow (COP26) said "phase-down of unabated coal"; Dubai (COP28) said "transitioning away from fossil fuels"; Belém (COP30) adopted no new fossil-fuel language and only reaffirmed the UAE Consensus, with the two roadmaps announced by the presidency outside the decision. COP31 is at Antalya in Türkiye, not Australia, though an Australian presides over the negotiations. India's net-zero year is 2070 and belongs to the long-term strategy, not the NDC; NDC 3.0 (March 2026) covers 2031–35 with 47 per cent intensity and 60 per cent non-fossil capacity, and the earlier 50 per cent capacity target for 2030 was met in 2025. The Panchamrit's "50 per cent of energy requirements from renewables" is about energy, while the NDC's percentages are about installed capacity. In the carbon market, one Carbon Credit Certificate equals one tonne of CO₂ equivalent while a PAT Energy Savings Certificate equals one tonne of oil equivalent; BEE administers, CERC regulates trading and the Grid Controller of India holds the registry. The IPCC's AR7 Synthesis Report is due by late 2029, not 2027 — 2027 is the CDR/CCUS methodology report.
