India's Industrial Trajectory: From Licensing to Liberalisation

Independent India's industrial policy passed through four distinct phases, and questions frequently hinge on attributing a feature to the right one.

The Industrial Policy Resolution of 1948 established a mixed economy, reserving arms, atomic energy and railways as a state monopoly. The IPR of 1956, the "economic constitution" of the Nehru-Mahalanobis era, went much further: it divided industry into Schedule A (seventeen industries exclusively for the state), Schedule B (twelve progressively state-owned) and Schedule C (the residual private sector), and it privileged heavy capital goods on the logic that a machine-building base would allow self-sustaining growth. The Mahalanobis model of the Second Five Year Plan is the intellectual parent of this choice. The instruments were the Industries (Development and Regulation) Act, 1951 — the licence-permit regime — and the MRTP Act, 1969, which restrained the growth of large houses, together with the FERA restrictions on foreign exchange and the reservation of hundreds of products for small-scale industry.

The results were mixed by design. A diversified industrial base, public-sector capability in steel, heavy engineering, space and atomic energy, and reduced import dependence for capital goods were genuine achievements. Against these stand the costs: capital-intensity that failed to absorb labour, the neglect of consumer goods and exports, chronic capacity shortages, rent-seeking around licences, and the low productivity captured in the phrase "the Hindu rate of growth." The 1991 New Industrial Policy, triggered by the balance of payments crisis, abolished industrial licensing for all but a handful of industries, cut the reserved list from seventeen to a very small number, abolished the MRTP threshold clearance, opened the door to automatic-route FDI, and initiated disinvestment. Today licensing survives only for defence, tobacco, explosives, hazardous chemicals and the like, and only atomic energy and railway operations remain substantially reserved.

The structural puzzle of the present is premature deindustrialisation: manufacturing's share of GDP has stagnated near 15–17% rather than rising to the 25% of the East Asian path, and India moved from agriculture largely into services, skipping the labour-absorbing factory stage. Explanations to marshal: rigid labour regulation and the resulting preference for capital intensity and contract labour; the missing middle of firms that never grow past the small-scale threshold; infrastructure and logistics costs; land acquisition difficulty; the inverted duty structure; and low scale, which forecloses export competitiveness.

MSMEs: Definition, Contribution and Constraints

The MSMED Act, 2006 originally defined the sector by investment in plant and machinery, with separate and lower thresholds for services. The 2020 revision replaced this with a composite criterion of investment and turnover and, crucially, removed the manufacturing–services distinction:

Micro₹1 crore₹5 crore
Small₹10 crore₹50 crore
Medium₹50 crore₹250 crore

The purpose of the change was to end the disincentive to grow — firms had been deliberately staying small and fragmenting to retain benefits — and to remove the incentive to under-invest in machinery. Registration is now through Udyam, which is free, paperless and self-declared, integrated with PAN and GST.

MSMEs contribute roughly 30% of GDP, about 45% of exports and employ over 11 crore people, second only to agriculture, across some 6.3 crore enterprises of which the overwhelming majority are micro and largely unregistered and informal. That informality is the master constraint from which the others follow.

Constraints, each with its policy counterpart:

  • Credit. The formal credit gap runs to lakhs of crores because MSMEs lack collateral, audited accounts and credit history, so they rely on costly informal finance. Responses: priority sector lending obligations, CGTMSE collateral-free guarantees, MUDRA loans, the TReDS platform for discounting trade receivables, the ECLGS pandemic credit line, and Account Aggregator-enabled cash-flow lending using GST and UPI data in place of collateral.
  • Delayed payments. Working capital is locked in receivables from large buyers and government. The MSMED Act mandates payment within 45 days with compound interest at three times the bank rate thereafter, enforced through MSEFC facilitation councils and the Samadhaan portal; the Income Tax Act's Section 43B(h) now disallows the buyer's deduction until payment is made.
  • Technology and productivity — obsolete machinery, low R&D, weak quality certification. Responses: technology centres, the ZED certification scheme, Lean manufacturing and design schemes.
  • Market access — countered by the Public Procurement Policy requiring central ministries and PSUs to source 25% from MSEs, including sub-quotas of 4% from SC/ST and 3% from women enterprises, and by GeM and the ONDC network.
  • Skills, infrastructure and clusters — addressed through the MSE-CDP cluster programme, SFURTI for traditional industries, and PMEGP for new micro-enterprise creation.

Contemporary Industrial Policy: Make in India to PLI

Make in India (2014) targeted 25 sectors with the goal of raising manufacturing to 25% of GDP and creating 100 million jobs; its instruments were FDI liberalisation, ease-of-doing-business reform and the Invest India facilitation agency. It underdelivered on the manufacturing share, which is the standard critique, but it did shift the policy conversation. Atmanirbhar Bharat (2020) reframed self-reliance not as autarky but as competitiveness and supply-chain resilience, explicitly rejecting the import-substitution connotation.

The Production Linked Incentive scheme is the sharpest current instrument: an incentive paid as a percentage of incremental sales of goods manufactured in India, over a base year, subject to threshold investment. Its logic differs from earlier subsidies in being outcome-linked rather than input-linked, time-bound, and targeted at achieving scale. Fourteen sectors are covered, including mobile and electronic components, pharmaceuticals and APIs, medical devices, automobiles and auto components, advanced chemistry cell batteries, textiles, food processing, telecom, drones, white goods, solar modules and specialty steel. Results are strongly uneven — mobile phone manufacturing and exports have transformed, while several other sectors have disbursed little. Critiques: the risk of picking winners, that value addition is shallow assembly rather than deep component manufacturing, fiscal cost, and neglect of labour-intensive sectors such as garments and leather where India's comparative advantage lies.

Enabling reforms to cite alongside: the National Infrastructure Pipeline and PM Gati Shakti for multimodal logistics coordination; the National Logistics Policy aiming to cut logistics costs from about 13–14% of GDP towards single digits; the National Single Window System for clearances; the reduction of corporate tax to 22%, and 15% for new manufacturing units; the four labour codes consolidating twenty-nine laws on wages, industrial relations, social security and occupational safety, raising the retrenchment threshold to 300 workers; the IBC providing an exit mechanism, since a credible exit is a precondition for entry; the Semiconductor Mission; the National Manufacturing Mission; and industrial corridors such as the Delhi-Mumbai and Chennai-Bengaluru corridors anchored on dedicated freight corridors. Balance any answer by noting the persistent constraints of land acquisition, state-level implementation variance, judicial delay in contract enforcement, and the skills mismatch.
