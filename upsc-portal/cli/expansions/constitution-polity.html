The basic-structure saga — Parliament vs Court, 1951–2007

The trunk's amendment table (§7) lists the cases; this section argues them as a thirty-year dialogue about who is supreme — Parliament, or the Constitution. Act one — Parliament ascendant (1951–67). Shankari Prasad (1951) held amendment-power unlimited (Art 368 is constituent power, not "law" under Art 13) — the 1st Amendment's 9th Schedule (land-reform immunity) and "reasonable restrictions" survived; Sajjan Singh (1965) confirmed, though two judges doubted. Act two — the Court's counter (1967). Golaknath (1967) reversed: FRs are transcendental, beyond Art 368's reach — Parliament, stung, passed the 24th Amendment (1971) declaring Art 368 amendments are not Art 13 "law" and FRs amendable. Act three — the settlement (1973). Kesavananda Bharati (1973, 13 judges, 7:6): Parliament may amend anything but must not destroy the basic structure — supremacy of the Constitution, republican-democratic form, secularism, separation of powers, federalism, judicial review, FRs in essence, free and fair elections, rule of law. Note the trunk's Preamble corollary: the Preamble is part of the Constitution (Kesavananda) though unenforceable (Berubari 1960), and amendable short of basic structure (hence the 42nd's socialist/secular/integrity insertions stand).

Act four — the stress test (1975–80). The 42nd ("Mini-Constitution," 1976) tried to end the dialogue — DPSP primacy over FRs, amendment-immunity from review, bound President, 5→6-year Lok Sabha — and the 44th (1978) repealed the EmergencyCore damage (restored 5 years, one-time return of advice, "armed rebellion" replacing "internal disturbance" in Art 352, property demoted to Art 300A). Minerva Mills (1980) then constitutionalised the moral: "the Constitution, not Parliament, is supreme" — FR–DPSP harmony itself is basic structure (Art 31C's 39(b)–(c) immunity survives; its "all-DPSP" extension died). Act five — the long tail. Waman Rao (1981) and I. R. Coelho (2007): 9th-Schedule insertions after 24 April 1973 (Kesavananda's date) are reviewable on basic-structure touchstone — closing the 1951 loophole with a 1973 timestamp. The Mains moral: basic structure is not judicial supremacy but constitutional supremacy — it binds the Court too (which can only identify, never invent, basic features) and it is India's wholly original export to comparative constitutional law (no US parallel; the EU's constitutional-identity doctrine is the nearest cousin).

Federalism's live wire — delimitation, simultaneous polls, and the Governor

The trunk's federalism sections (§§8–9) give the architecture (Seventh Schedule: Union ~100 / State ~61 / Concurrent ~52 after the 42nd's transfers; residuary Art 248 + Entry 97 with the Centre; Arts 245–255 legislative, 256–263 administrative, 268–293 financial; 16th FC 2026–31 Panagariya; emergencies 352/356/360 with Bommai 1994 fencing 356) and the live debates; this section supplies argued positions for each. Delimitation: the 84th Amendment (2001) froze Lok Sabha totals until the first census after 2026, so post-2027-census redistribution is now due — Southern States, demographically successful, fear representation loss to the Hindi heartland; the April 2026 defeat of the 131st Amendment Bill (fast-track women's reservation + 2011-census delimitation) proved the North–South divide is the federal question of the decade. Argue both formulae honestly: population-fidelity (one person, one vote, one value) vs federal-equity (don't punish family-planning success) — with the 106th Amendment's women's reservation (Art 334A, post-delimitation trigger) hostage to the same arithmetic. One Nation One Election: the Kovind committee (September 2023) recommended phased return to the 1951–67 simultaneity; the Constitution (129th) Amendment Bill 2024 (+ UT Laws Bill) sits with a JPC. Argue cost-and-governance continuity for, and federal distinctiveness (national waves swamping State verdicts; premature-dissolution mechanics; 356-abuse risk) against.

The GST Council (Art 279A) is the counter-exhibit — shared sovereignty that works: Centre–State weighted voting, consensus practice, rate/compensation disputes negotiated rather than litigated. The Governor is the exhibit that doesn't: assent delays, Art 200 reservations, "constitutional head vs alternative power centre" litigation (the trunk's Titaghur-style caution), with the Court repeatedly fixing the principle that Governors have no pocket veto. Synthesis line: Indian federalism runs on three different fuels — the Council's cooperation, the Commission's (Art 280) arbitration, and the Court's supervision — and every contemporary crisis is a dispute about which fuel applies: delimitation is arbitration-starved, ONOE is cooperation-dependent, and the Governor's office is supervision-tested.

The FR–DPSP–FD triangle and five answer frames

The trunk's Parts III/IV/IVA exposition (§§5–6) is Prelims-complete (six FRs: 14–18 equality with 15(4)/(5)/(6) reservation enablers and Art 17's absoluteness under PCR 1976; 19–22 freedom with 19(2)–(6) restrictions, Art 20's triple shield, Art 21's Maneka (1978) due-process reading plus 21A/86th; 23–24 exploitation; 25–28 religion; 29–30 minorities; Art 32's five writs as the "heart and soul" itself; Arts 12/13/31C/33/34/35 scaffolding; DPSP's socialist–Gandhian–liberal-intellectual classification — 38/39/39A(42nd)/41–43, 40/43/43B(97th, partly struck 2021)/44/45(86th), 47/48/48A(42nd)/49/50/51; eleven duties under 51A via Swaran Singh/42nd + 86th). The Mains value-add is the triangle relationship: FRs justiciable constraints on the state, DPSPs non-justiciable instructions to the state, FDs non-enforceable reminders to the citizen — with Minerva's harmony doctrine (neither FRs nor DPSPs may swallow the other) as the governing law. Amendment history walks the triangle: 1st (restrictions + 9th Schedule) → 24th (FRs amendable) → 25th (31C for 39(b)–(c)) → 42nd (DPSP-uber-alles attempt + 39A/48A/FDs + UCC kept alive by courts) → 44th (correction) → 86th (21A + 45 + 51A(k) completing the education circuit) → 97th (cooperatives 43B) → 101st (GST federalism) → 103rd (EWS 10%, Janhit Abhiyan 2022, 3:2) → 104th (SC/ST extension, Anglo-Indian sunset) → 106th (Nari Shakti, Art 334A).

Five frames close the lesson — and the queue. (i) "Borrowed yet original": deploy the trunk's sources table (1935 Act's skeleton; Britain's parliamentarism; America's rights + review + VP; Ireland's DPSP + RS nominations + President's election; Canada's strong-centre federation; Australia's Concurrent List + joint sitting + trade freedom; Weimar's emergency suspension; USSR's duties + justice ideals; France's republican virtues; South Africa's Art 368 + RS elections; Japan's "procedure established by law") — then Ambedkar's defence: borrowing the principles, authoring the synthesis (parliamentary government with entrenched judicial review exists almost nowhere else). (ii) "Quasi-federal" (Wheare): federal form, unitary spirit — single citizenship (Arts 5–11), appointed Governors, residuary Centre, 249/250/253/356 overrides. (iii) "Lawyer's paradise" (Jennings): Preamble + 22 Parts + 395 Articles + 8 Schedules → ~470/25/12 — length as the price of trust deficit in 1949. (iv) Amendment-mode questions: simple majority (Arts 2/4/169/Schedules — "not amendments") vs special majority (FRs/DPSP/rest) vs special + half-State ratification (President's election, judicature, lists, RS representation, Art 368 itself). (v) The making story (M. N. Roy 1934 → Congress 1935 → Nehru 1938 → August Offer 1940 → Cripps 1942 → Cabinet Mission 1946 → 9 December 1946, Sinha/Prasad/Mukherjee–Krishnamachari, Objectives Resolution 13 December 1946, B. N. Rau advising, Drafting Committee 29 August 1947, Ambedkar chairing 7, 2-11-18, ₹64 lakh, adopted 26 November 1949 — Constitution Day since 2015 — enforced 26 January 1950 for 26 January 1930): recite it as India's founding epic, because that is what it is.
