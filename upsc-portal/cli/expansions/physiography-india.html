6. The Six Physiographic Divisions

India is conventionally divided into the Northern and North-Eastern Mountains, the Northern Plain, the Peninsular Plateau, the Indian Desert, the Coastal Plains and the Islands. The organising geological fact is that the Peninsular block is the oldest — a stable Gondwana fragment of Archaean gneisses and schists — while the Himalaya and the Northern Plain are the youngest, produced by the collision of the Indian and Eurasian plates and the infilling of the resulting Tethys geosyncline foredeep. Every contrast between north and peninsula follows from that difference in age and origin.

7. The Himalaya — Longitudinal and Regional Divisions

Longitudinally, from north to south, there are three parallel ranges plus the trans-Himalayan zone:

  • Trans-Himalaya — the Karakoram, Ladakh, Zaskar and Kailash ranges, north of the Indus-Tsangpo suture. K2 (Godwin Austen) in the Karakoram is the highest peak in India-claimed territory. The Siachen and Baltoro are among the largest glaciers outside the polar regions.
  • Himadri / Greater Himalaya — the loftiest and most continuous range, averaging about 6,000 m, containing Kanchenjunga (India's highest), Nanda Devi, Nanga Parbat and, in Nepal, Everest. Composed of crystalline granites and gneisses; the perennial snowline and the source of all the great rivers.
  • Himachal / Lesser Himalaya — 3,700–4,500 m, highly compressed and folded, of unfossiliferous sediments and altered rock. Contains the famous ranges — Pir Panjal (the longest), Dhauladhar, Mussoorie, Nag Tibba, Mahabharat — and the hill stations of Shimla, Mussoorie, Nainital and Darjeeling, together with the Kashmir valley and the alpine Kashmir and Kangra meadows called marg and bugyal.
  • Shiwaliks / Outer Himalaya — 900–1,100 m, the youngest range, built of unconsolidated gravel and alluvium eroded from the ranges above. Longitudinal valleys between the Shiwaliks and the Lesser Himalaya are called duns — Dehra Dun, Kotli Dun, Patli Dun. Being loose sediment, the Shiwaliks are highly prone to landslides and erosion.

Regionally, from west to east, the divisions are Punjab (or Kashmir) Himalaya, Kumaon Himalaya, Nepal Himalaya and Assam Himalaya, separated by the Indus, Sutlej, Kali, Teesta and Dihang rivers respectively — the standard river-boundary sequence.

Two features recur in questions. Himalayan passes: Zoji La (Srinagar–Leh), Banihal (the Jawahar tunnel), Pir Panjal, Rohtang, Shipki La, Bara Lacha La, Lipulekh, Mana, Niti, Nathu La and Jelep La (Sikkim), Bomdi La (Arunachal). Syntaxial bends: the Himalaya turns sharply south at Nanga Parbat in the west and at Namcha Barwa in the east, where the Brahmaputra executes its Great Bend.

The Purvanchal or Eastern Hills — the Patkai Bum, Naga, Manipur, Mizo (Lushai), Barail and Garo–Khasi–Jaintia hills — are the southward continuation of the Himalayan structure, of soft sandstone, running north–south. The Meghalaya plateau is geologically part of the peninsular block, separated from it by the Malda gap — a favourite trap.

8. The Northern Plain

An aggradational plain of alluvium up to great depth, formed by the Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra. Divided west to east into the Punjab plain (dominated by doabs — Bist, Bari, Rachna, Chaj, Sind — and marked by bet flood plains and khadar), the Ganga plain and the Brahmaputra plain.

The relief-based belts, running parallel from the foothills southward, are examined almost every year:

  • Bhabar — a narrow porous belt of pebbles and gravel at the foothills, where streams disappear underground. Unsuited to agriculture; supports forest.
  • Terai — south of the Bhabar, where the streams re-emerge, creating a marshy, damp, densely forested and once malarial belt, now largely reclaimed for cultivation, especially in Uttarakhand and UP.
  • Bhangar — the older alluvium, forming terraces above the flood level, darker, containing calcareous kankar nodules. In the Ganga–Yamuna doab it is called the barind and bhur uplands.
  • Khadar — the newer alluvium of the active flood plains, renewed annually, lighter and more fertile.

Also know the reh or kallar saline efflorescence of over-irrigated tracts, and the Chambal badlands of ravine erosion.

9. The Peninsular Plateau, Desert, Coasts and Islands

The plateau is a tableland of ancient crystalline rock, broadly triangular, tilted so that most rivers flow east. Its divisions: the Central Highlands north of the Narmada (Malwa plateau, Bundelkhand, Baghelkhand, Chhotanagpur — the last being the "Ruhr of India" for its mineral wealth, with the Rajmahal hills at its eastern edge), and the Deccan Plateau south of it (Maharashtra plateau of Deccan Trap basalt weathering into black regur soil, Karnataka plateau, Telangana plateau).

The bounding hills: the Aravallis (north-east to south-west, among the world's oldest fold mountains, highly eroded, with Guru Shikhar on Mount Abu the highest point); the Vindhyas and Satpuras flanking the Narmada rift; the Maikal and Mahadeo ranges.

Western Ghats (Sahyadri) — continuous, higher (average 900–1,600 m), rising abruptly from the coast as an escarpment, crossable only through passes: Thal Ghat, Bhor Ghat and Pal Ghat. They are a biodiversity hotspot and the source of the peninsular rivers. The Palghat gap separates the Nilgiris from the Anaimalai; Anaimudi (2,695 m) in the Anaimalai is the highest peak in peninsular India, with the Cardamom hills and Palani hills radiating from it. Doda Betta is the Nilgiri high point.

Eastern Ghats — discontinuous, lower, irregular, dissected by the great east-flowing rivers; the Javadi, Shevaroy, Nallamala and Mahendragiri hills. The two Ghats meet in the Nilgiris.

Indian Desert (Thar) — west of the Aravallis, receiving under 150 mm of rain; features include longitudinal and transverse barchan dunes, ephemeral streams, playa lakes called dhrian and rann, and the Luni as its only notable river.

Coastal plains. The western coast is narrow, submergent, with estuaries, lagoons and backwaters (Kerala's kayals) but no large deltas; divided into the Kachchh–Kathiawar coast, Konkan, Kanara and Malabar. The eastern coast is broad, emergent, with well-developed deltas of the Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri; divided into the Northern Circars and the Coromandel coast. Chilika and Pulicat are its major lagoons. The submergent/emergent contrast explains why ports on the west coast are natural and deep while those on the east require dredging.

Islands. The Andaman and Nicobar group in the Bay of Bengal is the emergent extension of the Arakan Yoma–Purvanchal tectonic arc, hence hilly and of sedimentary and volcanic origin; Barren Island is India's only active volcano, and Saddle Peak the highest point. The Ten Degree Channel separates Andaman from Nicobar; the Great Channel separates Great Nicobar from Sumatra; Indira Point on Great Nicobar is India's southernmost point. The Lakshadweep group in the Arabian Sea is of coral origin — atolls and reefs on the Chagos-Laccadive submarine ridge — with Minicoy the largest, separated from the rest by the Nine Degree Channel and from Maldives by the Eight Degree Channel.

Trap watch. Bhabar is where streams vanish; Terai is where they re-emerge. Bhangar is older with kankar; khadar is newer and more fertile. The Meghalaya plateau belongs to the peninsular block, not the Himalaya. Anaimudi, not Doda Betta, is peninsular India's highest peak. The western coast is submergent with estuaries; the eastern is emergent with deltas. Andaman and Nicobar are tectonic/volcanic, Lakshadweep is coral. Barren Island is the only active volcano. The Shiwaliks are the youngest and most landslide-prone range.
