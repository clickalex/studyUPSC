The Collegium: How Judicial Appointments Actually Work

India appoints its higher judiciary by the most judge-dominated method in any major democracy, and the method appears nowhere in the constitutional text — it was written by the judges themselves across four cases. The text of Articles 124 and 217 requires only Presidential appointment \"after consultation\" with the CJI and others; the meaning of \"consultation\" was litigated into \"concurrence\" and then into institutional primacy. The First Judges Case (S.P. Gupta, 1981) held consultation non-binding — executive primacy, forged in the post-Emergency distrust of the Court. The Second Judges Case (1993) reversed it: the CJI's opinion, formed in consultation with colleagues, has primacy — the collegium was born. The Third Judges Case (1998, Presidential reference) formalised it: the CJI plus four seniormost judges for Supreme Court appointments, the CJI plus two for High Court transfers and appointments (with the relevant Chief Justice and seniormost colleagues consulted for HC origins). The Fourth Judges Case (2015, 4:1) struck down the 99th Amendment and the NJAC Act, 2014 — Parliament's attempt to replace the collegium with a six-member commission (CJI, two judges, Law Minister, two eminent persons with a veto-any-two rule) — holding judicial primacy in appointments part of basic structure. The journey from 1981 to 2015 is thus a full arc: executive primacy litigated into judicial primacy, then defended against legislative reversal.

How the system operates in practice is the examination's real demand. Vacancies are initiated by High Court collegiums (Chief Justice plus two seniormost), vetted by the state executive and the Intelligence Bureau, decided by the Supreme Court collegium, and formally appointed by the President — who may return a recommendation once for reconsideration but must appoint if it is reiterated. The friction points are structural: the Memorandum of Procedure (the appointment rulebook) has been contested since the Court ordered its revision in the 2015 judgment's companion order, with the government's national-security and seniority clauses never mutually settled; segregation (the executive clearing some collegium names while sitting on others) and indefinite withholding of reiterated names have provoked repeated Court rebukes without resolution; and the collegium's own opacity — no published criteria, no recorded reasons, no application process — sustains the criticism that a self-perpetuating body selects in secret. The diversity data sharpens the critique: High Court and Supreme Court benches remain overwhelmingly upper-caste and male (the first Dalit CJI, K.G. Balakrishnan, 2007; the Court has never had a Dalit or Adivasi woman judge, and only eleven women judges in its history to 2024), with collegium resolutions since 2017 publishing bare outcomes but not the deliberations that would show diversity considered.

The comparative judgment completes the analysis. The United States confirms federal judges through Senate hearings (political, transparent, polarised); the United Kingdom appoints through the Judicial Appointments Commission created by the Constitutional Reform Act 2005 (independent, criteria-published, diversity-mandated, with the Lord Chancellor's limited veto); South Africa interviews candidates in public before the Judicial Service Commission. India's collegium is uniquely independent but unaccountable — the NJAC judgment preserved the independence while conceding, in several concurring opinions, that the system's transparency must improve. Reform proposals short of constitutional amendment therefore cluster on process: a published MoP with timelines, recorded collegium reasons, a secretariat for candidate research, diversity data in resolutions, and fixed executive-response windows. An answer that narrates the four cases, names the friction points, and lands on MoP-plus-transparency as the viable reform has covered appointments at the depth the paper demands.

The Jurisdiction Ladder in Practice: From 131 to Curative

The Supreme Court's jurisdiction is a ladder of escalating rarity, and each rung has a use-case the examination tests. Original jurisdiction (Article 131) covers federal disputes — Centre versus State(s), State versus State — over legal rights: the Court has heard river-water suits (Cauvery, Krishna), the expelled-reference litigation, and, in the trust-deficit era, States suing the Union directly (Kerala's 2024 borrowing-limits suit, West Bengal's CBI-consent suit, Tamil Nadu's education-funds suit), making 131 the live edge of federalism litigation. Writ jurisdiction (Article 32) enforces Fundamental Rights directly — the \"heart and soul\" guarantee. Appellate jurisdiction (Articles 132–134) hears constitutional, civil and criminal appeals on certification or as of right in narrow classes (notably death sentences). Special leave (Article 136) is the discretionary \"widest door\" — appeal from any court or tribunal (armed-forces tribunals excepted), grantable on substantial questions, grave injustice or the need to settle law; its very width is why the Court drowns in 90,000-plus pending matters, with scholars perennially urging a certiorari-style filter the Court perennially resists. Review (Article 137) permits re-examination for error apparent, usually by circulation without oral hearing. Curative jurisdiction — invented in Rupa Ashok Hurra (2002) — allows a final petition after review's dismissal on narrow grounds (violation of natural justice, bias, abuse of process), heard by the three seniormost judges plus the review-bench judges; its grants are vanishingly rare (the 2024 AGR-dues curative dismissal and the rare admitted cases prove the rule), which is precisely the point — finality with a conscience window.

Three further powers complete the ladder's top. Advisory jurisdiction (Article 143) lets the President refer questions of law or fact of public importance — the Court may opine (it refused the 1994 Ram Janmabhoomi reference's core question) and the opinion does not bind as precedent, though its authority is near-decisive in practice (Berubari, 1960 — cession of territory needs constitutional amendment; the Cauvery and Punjab-termination references). Complete justice (Article 142) empowers decrees \"necessary for doing complete justice\" — the doctrinal basis for the Bhopal settlement, inter-faith marriage transfers, and most prominently the Ayodhya verdict (2019), where the unanimous five-judge bench awarded the disputed site for a Ram temple while allotting five acres elsewhere for a mosque, expressly invoking 142 to \"mould relief\" beyond ordinary title law. Law-declaring power (Article 141) makes Supreme Court decisions binding on all courts — the stare-decisis anchor — with Article 144 commanding all authorities to act in aid of the Court. The synthesis is that the ladder runs from right (131, 32) through permission (136, 137, curative) to grace (142, 143) — and the Court's docket crisis is substantially the consequence of 136's permission being sought as though it were a right.

High Courts mirror the ladder with one decisive difference: Article 226's trigger is wider (\"any other purpose\" — any legal right, not only FRs) while its grant is discretionary, and Article 227's superintendence extends over all courts and tribunals in the territory — the provision Chandra Kumar (1997) constitutionalised as basic structure. Below them, district judges are appointed by the Governor \"in consultation with\" the High Court (Article 233, with seven years' Bar practice required), and postings, promotions and leave rest with the High Court (Article 235) — the control-and-consultation design that insulates the subordinate judiciary from the state executive, at least formally. Twenty-five High Courts (the 1862 trio of Calcutta, Bombay and Madras oldest; Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, 2019, newest) sit atop this pyramid, with common High Courts (Punjab–Haryana at Chandigarh; the seven-sister coverage of Gauhati) preserving the reorganisation era's compromises.

Tribunals, PIL, and Justice Outside the Courtroom

Tribunals were the Emergency-era promise of expert, speedy justice — Articles 323A (administrative tribunals; the CAT, 1985) and 323B (tax, industrial, land, election and other tribunals), inserted by the 42nd Amendment — and their history since is a tug-of-war between expedition and independence. Sampath Kumar (1987) upheld the CAT experiment subject to High-Court-equivalent service conditions; Chandra Kumar (1997) struck down the ouster of High Court review, holding 226/227 jurisdiction over tribunal orders part of basic structure; the R. Gandhi series (2010, 2020) constitutionalised the NCLT–NCLAT while insisting on judicial-member dominance and fixed tenures; and the Tribunal Reforms Act, 2021 — abolishing several appellate tribunals and reworking tenure — saw key provisions struck down in Madras Bar Association IV (2021) for violating the independence conditions the Court had laid down (minimum five-year tenure, judicial predominance in selection). The live tribunal map — NCLT–NCLAT (company law, IBC appeals to the Supreme Court), NGT (2010, environmental jurisdiction with expert members), AFT (armed forces), SAT (securities), CAT (service matters) — thus operates under judge-made independence guarantees the executive tests with each amendment. The examination's portable rule is that tribunals may supplement but never supplant: supplement the docket with expertise, but supplant neither High Court review nor judicial independence.

Public Interest Litigation is the complementary innovation — justice for those who cannot reach court, through relaxed locus standi. The invention (Hussainara Khatoon, 1979, undertrial prisoners; S.P. Gupta, 1981, crystallising representative standing; the epistolary jurisdiction treating postcards as writs) converted the Court into the forum of last resort for bonded labour, environmental degradation, custodial violence and governance failures. Its maturity brought the abuse docket: publicity-seeking petitions, political weaponisation, and Court-overreach critiques — answered by the misuse-checks doctrine (credentials of the petitioner, correctness of motive, State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaufal, 2010, laying down PIL-maintainability guidelines) and the Court's own periodic self-restraint (costs on frivolous PILs, refusal to run policy). The third pillar, alternative dispute resolution, now has statutory spine: Lok Adalats under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 (NALSA to DLSA; awards deemed civil-court decrees with no appeal — the finality that makes National Lok Adalats dispose of lakhs of cases per sitting), Gram Nyayalayas (2008 Act, mobile village courts, unevenly operationalised), and the Mediation Act, 2023 (pre-litigation mediation made effectively mandatory, mediated settlements enforceable, the Mediation Council of India constituted). The synthesis is three-track justice — constitutional courts for rights, tribunals for expertise, ADR for volume — with the volume track carrying the only realistic hope against pendency.

Pendency, Access, and the Reform Agenda

The numbers define the crisis: over 5.39 crore cases pending across Indian courts as of December 2025 (4.76 crore in district and subordinate courts, 63.66 lakh in High Courts, 92,101 in the Supreme Court — the apex backlog up 11.4 per cent in two years), against a judge–population ratio of roughly 15 per 10 lakh against the Law Commission's recommended 50. The causes are structural rather than personal: filings grow with rights-awareness and economic complexity while disposals stagnate on vacancies (High Courts routinely 25–30 per cent vacant, district courts worse), procedure rewards adjournment (the CPC–CrPC–Evidence trilogy, now the 2023 new codes, still permit the adjournment economy), the State itself is the largest litigant (government appeals clog every level — the National Litigation Policy has been promised since 2010 and never operationalised into binding appeal-thresholds), and infrastructure (courtrooms, staff, digital access) lags sanctioned strength. The consequence the President termed the \"Black Coat Syndrome\" — public distrust of a justice system where the average civil suit spans years and executions longer — is the legitimacy cost that makes pendency a governance question, not merely a management one.

The reform stack divides into technology, procedure and structure. Technology is the furthest advanced: the e-Courts Mission Mode Project (phases I–III, with Phase III's saturation approach), the National Judicial Data Grid (real-time pendency data — the source of every figure above), virtual and hybrid hearings normalised post-Covid, the FASTER system for electronic transmission of bail and release orders, e-filing and e-payment, SUVAS AI translation of judgments into scheduled languages, and live-streaming of Constitution Bench hearings from 2022. Procedure carries the 2023 new-code promise (BNSS timelines for investigation and judgment, zero-FIR and e-FIR, plea-bargaining expansion) plus case-flow management (differentiated tracks for old cases, commercial courts with strict timelines under the Commercial Courts Act). Structure holds the contested agenda: an All-India Judicial Service (Article 312 enables it; High Courts and States resist it), regional benches of the Supreme Court (Law Commission recommended; the Court itself opposes, fearing dilution of authority), retirement-age and tribunal-rationalisation reforms, binding National Litigation Policy thresholds, and legal-aid saturation (NALSA's front offices, tele-law, pro bono panels — access without affordability is access denied). The closing judgment for answers is comparative and causal: technology monitors the crisis, procedure trims it, but only structure (judges, incentives, State-litigation discipline) can end it — and until filings fall below disposals, every reform merely slows the growth of arrears.
