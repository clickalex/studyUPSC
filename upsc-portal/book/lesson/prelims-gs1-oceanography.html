4. Ocean Relief, Temperature and Salinity

Oceans cover about 71% of Earth's surface, unevenly distributed — the southern hemisphere is dominated by water, the northern by land, which has profound consequences for climate, since water heats and cools more slowly than land.

Relief divisions: the continental shelf, the gently sloping submerged extension of the continent averaging about 8% of ocean area but of enormous economic value — the world's great fishing grounds lie here because sunlight penetrates to the bottom sustaining plankton, and most offshore petroleum and gas is found on shelves; the continental slope, the true boundary between continental and oceanic crust, dissected by submarine canyons; the continental rise; and the abyssal plain, the flattest surface on Earth, covering the largest share of the ocean floor. Superimposed features are the mid-ocean ridges (the longest mountain chain on the planet, sites of sea-floor spreading and hydrothermal vents whose chemosynthetic ecosystems require no sunlight), trenches at subduction zones (the Mariana Trench's Challenger Deep being the deepest point), seamounts, flat-topped guyots, atolls and banks.

Temperature. Surface temperature decreases from the equator poleward and decreases with depth. The vertical structure has three layers: a warm, well-mixed surface layer to about 200 m; the thermocline, where temperature falls rapidly with depth and which acts as a barrier to vertical mixing; and the cold, near-uniform deep layer. The thermocline is permanent in the tropics, seasonal in mid-latitudes and largely absent in polar waters, where the water column is nearly isothermal. Controls on surface temperature: latitude, unequal land-water distribution, prevailing winds (offshore winds promote upwelling of cold water, onshore winds pile up warm water), ocean currents and enclosure — enclosed seas in the tropics are warmer than open oceans at the same latitude.

Salinity is the total dissolved salts in grams per kilogram of seawater, averaging about 35 parts per thousand, with sodium chloride dominant. The key principle is that salinity depends on the balance between evaporation and precipitation plus freshwater inflow. Hence salinity is highest in the subtropics around 20–30°, under the descending, dry subtropical highs where evaporation is intense — not at the equator, where heavy rainfall dilutes it. This equatorial dip is a classic prelims trap. Extremes: Lake Van in Turkey, the Dead Sea and the Great Salt Lake are hypersaline enclosed water bodies; the Red Sea is the most saline open sea because of high evaporation and negligible river input, while the Baltic Sea is among the least saline owing to heavy river discharge and low evaporation. Within India, the Arabian Sea is more saline than the Bay of Bengal, which receives the Ganga-Brahmaputra discharge and heavy monsoon rainfall — a difference that also affects cyclone behaviour and stratification. The halocline is the zone of rapid salinity change; salinity increases density, and together with temperature governs thermohaline circulation.

5. Circulation: Currents, Upwelling and the Conveyor Belt

Surface currents are driven chiefly by prevailing winds, modified by the Coriolis force, differences in temperature and salinity, and the configuration of landmasses. The resulting pattern is a set of gyres, clockwise in the northern hemisphere and anticlockwise in the southern. The universal rule: warm currents move poleward along the western sides of ocean basins, that is along the eastern coasts of continents, and cold currents move equatorward along the eastern sides of basins, along the western coasts of continents. This single rule explains why western continental margins in the subtropics host coastal deserts — Atacama with the Peru current, Namib with the Benguela, and the Western Australian and Sahara coasts — since cold water suppresses evaporation and stabilises the air.

Currents to know by name and effect: the Gulf Stream and North Atlantic Drift, which keep north-west Europe far milder than its latitude warrants and Norwegian ports ice-free; the Labrador cold current, whose meeting with the Gulf Stream at the Grand Banks produces dense advection fog and once the world's richest cod fishery; the Kuroshio and cold Oyashio off Japan, meeting near Hokkaido; the Canary, California, Benguela and Peru (Humboldt) cold currents; the Antarctic Circumpolar Current, the largest by volume, unobstructed by land; and the seasonally reversing monsoon currents of the North Indian Ocean, unique in the world, flowing clockwise in summer and anticlockwise in winter in direct response to the monsoon wind reversal.

Upwelling is the rise of cold, nutrient-rich water from depth, typically where offshore or alongshore winds and Ekman transport — the net movement of surface water at right angles to the wind, to the right in the northern hemisphere — draw surface water away from the coast. Upwelling zones off Peru, Namibia, California and Somalia support a hugely disproportionate share of world fish catch. Downwelling is the converse, oxygenating deeper layers but nutrient-poor at the surface.

Thermohaline circulation, the "global conveyor belt," is driven by density differences rather than wind. Cold, saline and therefore dense water sinks in the North Atlantic and around Antarctica, flows along the ocean floor, and upwells in the Indian and Pacific Oceans, returning as a warm surface flow over a cycle of roughly a thousand years. It transports heat globally, and the concern that freshwater from Greenland ice melt may reduce surface density and weaken the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation is among the most-cited climate tipping points.

Waves and tides. Waves are wind-generated; water particles move in near-circular orbits with little net forward transport until the wave breaks in shallow water. Tides result from the gravitational pull of the Moon and Sun and the centrifugal effect of the Earth-Moon system. Spring tides — highest range — occur at new and full moon when Sun, Earth and Moon are aligned (syzygy); neap tides — lowest range — at the quarter moons when they are at right angles (quadrature). The Moon's tidal influence is roughly twice the Sun's despite its far smaller mass, because tidal force varies with the cube of distance. Most coasts experience semi-diurnal tides. Funnel-shaped estuaries amplify range, giving the Bay of Fundy the world's highest tides and, in India, the Gulf of Khambhat and Kachchh their tidal energy potential. Tidal bores occur in the Hooghly and Brahmaputra.

6. Ocean Resources, Law and Threats

  • Resources: fisheries and aquaculture
  • offshore oil and gas
  • polymetallic nodules on the abyssal floor containing manganese, nickel, cobalt and copper, for which India holds a pioneer investor allocation in the Central Indian Ocean Basin and is developing the Samudrayaan crewed submersible under the Deep Ocean Mission
  • gas hydrates or methane clathrates in the Krishna-Godavari basin
  • placer deposits and monazite sands
  • tidal, wave and ocean thermal energy conversion
  • desalination
  • and marine biotechnology.

UNCLOS, 1982 — the constitution of the oceans — defines the maritime zones measured from the baseline: internal waters; the territorial sea to 12 nautical miles, where the coastal state has full sovereignty subject to innocent passage; the contiguous zone to 24 nm for customs, immigration, fiscal and sanitary enforcement; the Exclusive Economic Zone to 200 nm, conferring sovereign rights over resources but not sovereignty, so navigation and overflight remain free; the continental shelf to 200 nm, extendable to 350 nm on a submission to the Commission on the Limits of the Continental Shelf; and the high seas and the deep seabed Area, declared the common heritage of mankind and administered by the International Seabed Authority. The BBNJ Agreement of 2023 addresses biodiversity beyond national jurisdiction and marine protected areas on the high seas.

Threats: ocean acidification as absorbed carbon dioxide forms carbonic acid, lowering pH and impairing calcifying organisms — corals, molluscs and pteropods; coral bleaching, the expulsion of symbiotic zooxanthellae under thermal stress, which is reversible if brief but lethal if prolonged; sea-level rise from thermal expansion and ice melt, threatening deltas, small island states and coastal cities; marine plastic and microplastics accumulating in gyres; eutrophication from fertiliser runoff creating hypoxic dead zones, of which the Bay of Bengal and Gulf of Mexico are prominent examples; overfishing and destructive bottom trawling; ballast-water invasive species; oil spills; and underwater noise. Institutional responses to cite: SDG 14, the UN Decade of Ocean Science, the MARPOL convention, India's Blue Economy policy framework, the Coastal Regulation Zone notification, and the PM Matsya Sampada Yojana.

Trap watch. Salinity peaks in the subtropics, not the equator, where rainfall dilutes it. Warm currents hug the eastern coasts of continents. The EEZ gives sovereign rights over resources, not sovereignty; the territorial sea is 12 nm and the contiguous zone 24 nm from the baseline. Spring tides occur at new and full moon. The Moon's tidal effect exceeds the Sun's because tidal force varies inversely with the cube of distance. The Bay of Bengal is less saline than the Arabian Sea. Bleaching is the loss of zooxanthellae, not the death of the coral itself. Guyots are drowned flat-topped seamounts.
