Detailed Study

The full treatment of this lesson.

CSAT reasoning block: syllogisms, relations, arrangements, statement-logic. Method-solve, don't intuition-solve.

1. Syllogisms (Venn method)

  • Draw minimal-overlap Venns per statement; test each conclusion against ALL valid diagrams — follows only if true in every case; 'possibility' cases = some-can-be.
  • Conversion pairs: All A are B
  • Some B are A; No A is B
  • No B is A; Some A are B
  • Some B are A (Some-not does not convert).
  • Two negative premises → no conclusion; two particulars → no universal conclusion.

2. Blood relations & direction sense

  • Family-tree: build symbolically (♂-♀, =, vertical line), decode from the reference person backwards; watch 'only son/daughter' and in-law chains.
  • Direction: sketch compass with N-up; final displacement via Pythagoras; left-right turns are relative to facing — recompute the facing at every turn.

3. Puzzles & arrangements

  • Linear-circular seating: fix one anchor from a definite clue; negative clues become eliminations in a grid; circular left/right depends on inward-outward facing — the classic trap.
  • Scheduling-matching puzzles: table with attributes as columns; tick-cross matrix; verify the completed chain from both ends before answering.

4. Statement-assumption-conclusion-argument

  • Assumption = something unstated that MUST be true for the statement to make sense (not merely possible).
  • Course-of-action: prefer actionable, addressable-at-cause options over extreme or irrelevant ones.
  • Strengthen-weak: find the argument's link (premise→conclusion); strengthen bridges it, weaken breaks it; extremes and new-topics are decoys.
  • Clock-calendar numerics: odd-days method for calendar; mirror-image clock = 11:60 − time; angle = |30H − 5.5M|.

⚡ MCQ hooks

  • 'Some-not' never converts; 'All' converts to 'Some' — conversion table ask.
  • Two negatives = no conclusion — syllogism shortcut.
  • Circular seating left/right flips with outward facing — trap.
  • Direction: displacement = shortest-path ask; recompute facing each turn.
  • Assumption = must-be-true; possibility ≠ assumption.
  • Clock: angle formula |30H − 5.5M|; calendar: odd-days.

5. Syllogisms, Statements and Formal Logic

Syllogism questions give two or more statements and ask which conclusions necessarily follow. The governing principle is the same as in comprehension: accept the statements as true even if they are absurd in reality, and derive only what must follow. "All cats are stones" is a perfectly acceptable premise.

  • The four standard propositions are: A — universal affirmative, "All S are P"
  • E — universal negative, "No S is P"
  • I — particular affirmative, "Some S are P"
  • O — particular negative, "Some S are not P". The reliable method is Venn diagrams: draw the arrangement that satisfies all premises, then test each conclusion by asking whether an alternative valid diagram exists in which the conclusion fails. If such a diagram exists, the conclusion does not follow.

Conversion rules worth memorising, since they resolve many questions instantly: "All S are P" converts only to "Some P are S", never to "All P are S". "No S is P" converts fully to "No P is S". "Some S are P" converts to "Some P are S". "Some S are not P" does not convert at all. Two further rules eliminate most wrong options: no conclusion follows from two particular premises, and no conclusion follows from two negative premises; also, if one premise is particular the conclusion must be particular, and if one is negative the conclusion must be negative.

Complementary pairs are the standard trap in "either-or" options. When neither of two conclusions individually follows, but together they exhaust all possibilities — typically an I and an O statement with the same terms, such as "Some A are B" and "Some A are not B" — then "either I or II follows" is correct. Recognising the A-and-O or E-and-I complementary structure saves considerable time.

Statement and assumption questions use the negation test: negate the candidate assumption and check whether the statement collapses. If it does, the assumption is implicit. Assumptions are unstated, necessary and taken for granted; anything explicitly stated in the passage is by definition not an assumption. Statement and conclusion requires that the conclusion be strictly derivable; statement and argument asks whether an argument is strong — strong arguments are directly relevant, address the core issue and are not merely emotive, hypothetical or based on individual example. Cause and effect questions require care with the direction of causation and with the possibility of a common third cause. Course of action questions accept a proposed action only if the problem is real, the action is feasible and it addresses the cause rather than the symptom.

6. Arrangements, Blood Relations, Directions and Coding

Seating and linear arrangements are the highest-yield analytical set because they are entirely mechanical once a method is fixed. The procedure: read all conditions before drawing anything; classify them as definite (fixes a position) or relative (fixes an order); start from the most restrictive definite condition; represent circular arrangements with a diagram noting whether people face the centre — because "left" and "right" reverse when facing outward, which is the commonest source of error; and when two arrangements remain possible, carry both forward as cases rather than guessing. For two-variable puzzles — person, city, profession, colour — use a grid and mark both confirmations and eliminations, since eliminations often determine the answer before confirmations do.

Blood relations. Draw a family tree with a fixed convention: a horizontal double line for spouses, a vertical line for parent to child, and symbols for gender. Never infer gender from a name or a profession. Work backwards from the person whose relation is asked. For coded relations such as "A + B means A is the father of B", translate each symbol before combining. The standard trap is the "only son of my grandfather's only son" style of statement, which resolves cleanly on a diagram and almost never in the head.

Direction sense. Always draw, always mark north at the top, and track cumulative displacement rather than each step. Final distance from the start is usually found by the Pythagorean theorem on net east-west and net north-south displacement. Watch two traps: a turn "to the left" is relative to the direction the person currently faces, not to the map; and shadow-based questions depend on the Sun being in the east in the morning, so the shadow falls to the west, and the reverse in the evening.

  • Coding-decoding. Identify the mechanism first: letter shift by a fixed number
  • positional value where A is 1 and Z is 26
  • the opposite letter or EJOTY-style mapping where A pairs with Z, B with Y and so on, the pair always summing to 27
  • reversal
  • substitution of whole words
  • or a mixed rule. In substitution coding — "if green is called blue, blue is called red" — answer strictly by the substitution chain. Number and symbol series follow a limited repertoire: arithmetic and geometric progressions, squares and cubes with an offset, alternating series, differences of differences, and prime sequences. If a series resists analysis, compute successive differences
  • if those form a pattern, the series is solved.

7. Data Sufficiency, Data Interpretation and Practice Strategy

Data sufficiency gives a question and two statements and asks which are sufficient — never for the answer itself. This is the crucial discipline: do not solve, determine only whether a unique answer is obtainable. Evaluate statement I alone, then statement II alone while deliberately forgetting statement I, and only then both together. The commonest errors are carrying information from the first statement into the evaluation of the second, and accepting a statement that yields two possible values as sufficient — sufficiency requires a unique determination. A quadratic yielding two positive roots is insufficient; one yielding a positive and a negative root may be sufficient if the context requires a positive quantity.

Data interpretation covers tables, bar and line graphs, pie charts, caselets and mixed sets. Practical rules: read the units and the axis scale first, and note whether values are absolute or percentages — the classic trap is a chart in which one entity has a higher percentage but a lower absolute value because the bases differ. In pie charts remember that 3.6 degrees equals one per cent. Approximate aggressively when options are widely spaced; compute precisely only when they are close. Master percentage change, the distinction between per cent and percentage point, ratios, averages and weighted averages, since virtually all DI reduces to these. For "by what per cent is A more than B", the base is B; reversing the base is the most frequent careless error in the paper.

Practice strategy. Reasoning is a skill acquired by repetition under time pressure, not by reading solutions. Work previous year CSAT papers from 2011 in full, timed conditions, then re-solve every wrong question from scratch a week later without looking at the solution. Build a personal method sheet — one page per question type recording the exact procedure you will follow — and rehearse until the procedure is automatic, because in the examination hall the bottleneck is decision-making, not calculation. Maintain an error log classified by cause: misread condition, arithmetic slip, wrong base, direction reversal, sufficiency over-reach. Learn the skip decision: an arrangement puzzle that has not yielded a definite placement within ninety seconds should be abandoned and revisited in the second pass, because such puzzles either open quickly or not at all. Given that the paper is qualifying at 33% with one-third negative marking, the winning approach is to secure the mechanical, high-certainty question types — arrangements, blood relations, directions, simple series, straightforward DI — and treat the abstract and unfamiliar as optional.

Trap watch. Accept syllogism premises as true regardless of real-world falsity. "All A are B" converts only to "Some B are A". No conclusion follows from two particular or two negative premises. Complementary pairs make "either-or" correct. In circular arrangements, left and right reverse for anyone facing outward. Never infer gender from a name in blood relations. In data sufficiency, evaluate each statement in isolation and never actually solve; two possible values means insufficient. In a pie chart one per cent equals 3.6 degrees. For "A is what per cent more than B", the base is B.

Revision Digest

Condensed for the last mile before the exam.

Method anchors

  • Draw for directions, blood relations, seating — never hold diagrams in head.
  • For series: check difference first, then ratio, then alternating patterns, then alphabetic shifts (A=1 mapping memorised).
  • Puzzles: build the grid skeleton first; place "not"-clues last.
  • Syllogisms: draw the minimal Venn satisfying given premises; ask "is the conclusion forced in ALL such Venns?".

⚡ Rapid-fire with answers

  1. Series: 4, 11, 25, 53, ? — differences ×2+3 pattern → 109 (check: 53×2+3).
  2. Alpha series: AZ, CY, EX, FW, ? → GV (first letters +1; second −1).
  3. Number-logic: In a class, Ram is 12th from left, 18th from right. Total students? 29 (12+18−1).
  4. Blood: Pointing to a photograph: "She is my mother's brother's only sister's daughter." That's cousin (maternal aunt's daughter). Trap: if "only sister" is mother herself → sister! Read "only sister" as aunt → cousin.
  5. Direction: 5 km N → 3 km E → 5 km S. Displacement from start? 3 km E.
  6. Seating row: 6 people, D sits third to left of F; A adjacent to D's left; C not at ends... solve the full grid on paper; trap = "third to the left" means 3 positions away ( | |D| | |F| ) — count carefully.
  7. Coding: If WATER = XZUDS (letter shift pattern +1,+2,−1,−2,+1?) — verify a stated code logic from options; drill habit: write the shift row above the letters before computing.
  8. Syllogism: Some books are pens. Some pens are erasers. Conclusion offered: "Some books are erasers." → Does not follow. Second offered: "Some pens are books." → Follows (conversion of 'some').
  9. Clock: 3:15 angle between hands? |30×3 − 5.5×15| = 7.5°.
  10. Calendar: 1 Jan 2024 Monday. 1 Jan 2025? Wednesday (leap year advances 2). 1 Jan 2026? Thursday (normal year +1).
  11. Odd-one: 121, 144, 169, 152 → 152 (others perfect squares).
  12. Statement-conclusion: "All managers are leaders; no leader is lazy." → "No manager is lazy" follows (chain through universal).

Puzzler grid drill (4 min)

  • A, B, C, D, E live on floors 1–5 of a building. A lives above B. C is not on the top floor. D is immediately below C. E is not adjacent to A. Method: let globals first — A>
  • B
  • D=C−1
  • C≠5. Try C=4
  • D=3. Remaining 1,2,5 for A,B,E with A>
  • B and E non-adjacent to A
  • A=2,B=1,E=5 (E adjacent to A? floors 5 vs 2 not adjacent ✓). Solution: 1-B,2-A,3-D,4-C,5-E.

Data-sufficiency shape

"Is x even?" (i) x divisible by 4 (ii) x divisible by 6 — Answer class: (i) alone sufficient (implies even); classic CSAT framing with both/together templates.

Fail-modes to kill

  • "Immediately to the left" vs "somewhere to the left".
  • Negative premises ("not both") misconverted to " neither".
  • Calendar leap-year correction applied twice across century years (1900 not leap; 2000 leap).
  • Cube/counting problems: mark visible vs hidden faces before counting painted small cubes.

Set 1 — syllogism & relations

Q1

Statements: All pens are books. Some books are copies. Conclusions: I. Some copies are pens. II. Some pens are books. Which follows?

  1. (a)only I
  2. (b)only II
  3. (c)both
  4. (d)neither
Show answer
Correct: (b)(All pens→books reverses to Some books→pens = II; I not certain)
Q2

Statements: No cat is a dog. All dogs are animals. Conclusion: some animals are not cats:

  1. (a)follows
  2. (b)doesn't follow
  3. (c)can't say
  4. (d)data insufficient
Show answer
Correct: (a)(dogs are animals, no dog is cat ⇒ those animals aren't cats)
Q3

Pointing to a photo, Ravi said, 'She is the daughter of my grandfather's only son.' The girl is Ravi's:

  1. (a)sister
  2. (b)cousin
  3. (c)aunt
  4. (d)niece
Show answer
Correct: (a)(grandfather's only son = Ravi's father; his daughter = Ravi's sister)
Q4

A man walks 3 km north, turns right walks 4 km. Displacement from start:

  1. (a)7 km
  2. (b)1 km
  3. (c)5 km
  4. (d)6 km
Show answer
Correct: (c)(3-4-5 triangle)
Q5

If MOTHER is coded as ODVJHT, FATHER is coded as:

  1. (a)HCVJGT
  2. (b)HCVJHT
  3. (c)GCVJGT
  4. (d)HCUJGT
Show answer
Correct: (a)(+2 shift each letter)

Set 2 — puzzles & statement logic

Q6

Five friends sit in a row facing north: P is right of Q but left of R; S is at the extreme right; T is left of Q. Who sits middle?

  1. (a)P
  2. (b)Q
  3. (c)R
  4. (d)T
Show answer
Correct: (b)(order: T, Q, P, R, S)
Q7

In a queue, A is 7th from front, 12th from back. Queue length:

  1. (a)18
  2. (b)19
  3. (c)20
  4. (d)17
Show answer
Correct: (a)(7+12−1)
Q8

Statement: 'All employees must attend the safety drill.' Assumption: I. drills improve safety. II. employees can attend. Which is implicit?

  1. (a)I only
  2. (b)II only
  3. (c)both
  4. (d)neither
Show answer
Correct: (c)(both implicit)
Q9

A clock shows 3:30. Angle between hands:

  1. (a)90°
  2. (b)75°
  3. (c)105°
  4. (d)60°
Show answer
Correct: (b)(|30×3 − 5.5×30| = |90−165| = 75°)
Q10

Statement: 'The city improved street lighting; night accidents fell.' Which weakens the causal claim?

  1. (a)lights installed citywide
  2. (b)accident fall began before the lighting change
  3. (c)residents welcomed lights
  4. (d)lighting costs rose
Show answer
Correct: (b)(fall predates cause — breaks link).

Answers

  • 1-b (All pens→books reverses to Some books→pens = II; I not certain)
  • 2-a (dogs are animals, no dog is cat ⇒ those animals aren't cats)
  • 3-a (grandfather's only son = Ravi's father; his daughter = Ravi's sister)
  • 4-c (3-4-5 triangle)
  • 5-a (+2 shift each letter)
  • 6-b (order: T, Q, P, R, S)
  • 7-a (7+12−1)
  • 8-c (both implicit)
  • 9-b (|30×3 − 5.5×30| = |90−165| = 75°)
  • 10-b (fall predates cause — breaks link).
