4. The Human Development Approach and Its Measures

The human development approach, developed by Mahbub ul Haq and Amartya Sen and institutionalised in the UNDP's Human Development Report from 1990, redefines development as the enlargement of people's choices rather than the growth of output. Sen's capability approach supplies the philosophy: what matters is not the commodities a person owns nor the utility she derives, but her capabilities — the substantive freedoms to achieve the functionings she has reason to value, such as being well-nourished, literate, healthy and able to participate in community life. Income is a means, valuable only instrumentally; two people with identical incomes may have radically different capabilities because of disability, gender, social exclusion or the local environment — the problem of conversion factors.

Sen's four instrumental freedoms — economic facilities, social opportunities, transparency guarantees and protective security, alongside political freedoms — are the standard scaffolding for a mains answer on why growth alone is insufficient.

The Human Development Index is the geometric mean of three dimension indices:

  • A long and healthy life — life expectancy at birth.
  • Knowledge — mean years of schooling for adults aged 25 and above, and expected years of schooling for children of school-entry age.
  • A decent standard of living — GNI per capita at purchasing power parity, entered in logarithmic form to reflect the diminishing importance of income at higher levels.

The switch from an arithmetic to a geometric mean in 2010 matters conceptually: it reduces substitutability between dimensions, so poor performance in one cannot be fully offset by excellence in another.

Companion indices are frequently confused and should be kept distinct. The IHDI discounts the HDI for inequality in each dimension; the gap between HDI and IHDI is the "loss due to inequality." The Gender Development Index is the ratio of female to male HDI. The Gender Inequality Index measures loss in achievement across reproductive health (maternal mortality and adolescent birth rates), empowerment (parliamentary seats and secondary education) and labour market participation. The Multidimensional Poverty Index, developed with the Oxford Poverty and Human Development Initiative on the Alkire-Foster method, uses ten indicators across health, education and standard of living, with a person counted as poor when deprived in at least one-third of weighted indicators; it yields both a headcount ratio and an intensity, whose product is the MPI. India's National MPI, published by NITI Aayog, adds indicators for maternal health and bank accounts, giving twelve in all.

5. India's Human Development Record: Achievements and Deficits

India sits in the medium human development category, with a rank near the 130s, but the aggregate conceals two more revealing facts: a substantial loss due to inequality when moving from HDI to IHDI, and enormous interstate divergence. Kerala, Goa, Himachal Pradesh, Tamil Nadu and the north-eastern states such as Mizoram and Sikkim record HDI values comparable to upper-middle-income countries, while Bihar, Jharkhand, Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh and Odisha lag far behind. The Kerala model is the classic case of high human development achieved at modest income through early land reform, public investment in education and health, high female literacy, effective decentralisation and social mobilisation — evidence that human development does not simply follow income.

Health. Life expectancy has risen from about 32 years in 1947 to roughly 70. The infant mortality rate, under-five mortality rate and maternal mortality ratio have all fallen substantially, with the MMR now around 97 per lakh live births against the SDG target of 70. The total fertility rate has dropped to about 2.0, below the replacement level of 2.1 — the single most consequential demographic fact of the decade, with implications for ageing, the closing of the demographic window and interstate migration. The deficits are equally clear: public health expenditure is only around 1.9% of GDP against the National Health Policy 2017 target of 2.5%; out-of-pocket expenditure remains among the world's highest and pushes millions into poverty annually; the rising burden of non-communicable diseases coexists with unfinished communicable disease control, the classic double burden; and rural-urban and public-private disparities in provision persist.

Nutrition is India's most stubborn failure. NFHS-5 shows stunting (low height for age, indicating chronic undernutrition) at about 35%, wasting (low weight for height, indicating acute undernutrition) at about 19%, underweight at 32%, and anaemia in over half of women and children — with anaemia having worsened. The coexistence of undernutrition with rising obesity is the double burden of malnutrition. The Asian enigma — that South Asian child nutrition is worse than sub-Saharan Africa's despite higher incomes — is explained by maternal undernutrition and low birth weight, poor sanitation and open defecation causing environmental enteropathy, low maternal status and early marriage, and inadequate infant feeding practices.

Education. Near-universal enrolment at the elementary level under the RTE Act, 2009 and Article 21A coexists with a learning crisis documented by the ASER surveys and the NAS, high dropout rates at the secondary level, teacher absenteeism and vacancies, and low female labour force participation despite rising female educational attainment — the "gender paradox" of Indian development.

6. Policy Framework, SDGs and the Critique

Flagship interventions to deploy as evidence: Ayushman Bharat, combining Health and Wellness Centres for comprehensive primary care with PM-JAY, the insurance arm covering ₹5 lakh per family per year for the bottom 40%, together with the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission; the National Health Mission with its ASHA workforce; POSHAN Abhiyaan and Mission Poshan 2.0 integrating ICDS, supplementary nutrition and behaviour change; Mid-Day Meal / PM POSHAN, which raises enrolment, attendance and nutrition simultaneously and has strong evidence behind it; the National Food Security Act, 2013 covering two-thirds of the population with legal entitlements; Samagra Shiksha and the National Education Policy 2020 with its 5+3+3+4 structure, foundational literacy and numeracy mission NIPUN Bharat, and the 6%-of-GDP spending goal; Beti Bachao Beti Padhao; Swachh Bharat, whose sanitation gains link directly to nutrition outcomes; and Jal Jeevan Mission for household tap water, which reduces the drudgery and time-poverty of women.

SDGs. The 2030 Agenda's 17 goals and 169 targets, monitored in India through NITI Aayog's SDG India Index, place human development at the centre — SDG 1 poverty, 2 hunger, 3 health, 4 education, 5 gender, 6 water and sanitation, 10 inequality. The organising principle is "leave no one behind," which shifts attention from averages to the worst-off, and is precisely the argument for disaggregated data.

Critiques of the HDI that make an answer analytical: it omits political freedom, security, environmental sustainability and subjective well-being; it uses national averages and so hides inequality, distribution and regional divergence unless the IHDI is used; the equal weighting of the three dimensions is arbitrary; data quality and comparability are weak in developing countries; and it is a relative ranking, so a country can improve absolutely yet fall in rank. Alternatives worth naming: the Genuine Progress Indicator, Bhutan's Gross National Happiness, the OECD Better Life Index, the Social Progress Index, and India's own Ease of Living Index and National MPI.

Trap watch. HDI uses GNI per capita at PPP, not GDP, and in logarithmic form; the aggregation is a geometric mean since 2010. The GDI is a ratio of female to male HDI, while the GII measures loss in achievement — they are different things. Stunting is height-for-age, wasting is weight-for-height. The MPI counts a person poor at one-third weighted deprivation; India's National MPI has twelve indicators, the global one ten. India's TFR has fallen below the replacement level of 2.1. Health spending target under NHP 2017 is 2.5% of GDP, education 6% under NEP 2020. Mahbub ul Haq created the HDI; Sen supplied the capability framework.

Index-anchor flash.

⚡ HDI flash

  • HDI 1990 UNDP (Haq-Sen) · dims → life, schooling (mean+expected), GNI-pc · IHDI inequality · PHDI planet (2020).
  • GII → health-empowerment-labour · GDI → f/m HDI ratio · India → medium band, ~130/193.

⚡ MPI flash

  • MPI 2010 = UNDP+OPHI · 3 dims, 10 indicators · Alkire-Foster · India MPI = NITI, NFHS-based.
  • NITI MPI → 24.85% (2015-16) → 14.96% (2019-21) · ~11% extrapolation.

⚡ Poverty-line flash

  • Chain → Alagh 1979 → Lakdawala 1993 → Tendulkar 2009 (21.9%) → Rangarajan 2014 (~29.5%) → MPI era.
  • Calories → 2400/2100 · WB extreme line → $2.15/day 2017 PPP.

⚡ Welfare flash

  • NFSA 2013 → 67%, PHH 5 kg, AAY 35 kg/hh · ONORC 2019 · ePOS-TPDS · PMGKAY extension.
  • SECC 2011 → deprivation targeting · NSAP pensions · DBT stack.

⚡ Trap flash

  • HDI income = GNI per capita PPP — GDP options wrong.
  • MPI: 3 dimensions / 10 indicators — number pair.
  • Tendulkar = 2009 report with 2011-12 base — date pairing.
  • ONORC = portability of rations (not cash).
  • GDI ≠ GII (ratio vs index of three inequalities).

  3. (c)2, 3 and 4
  4. (d)1, 2, 3 and 4

Q2

In the Human Development Report 2025, India was placed at rank:

  1. (a)116
  2. (b)130
  3. (c)134
  4. (d)142
Show answer
Correct: (b)130/193, HDI 0.685.
Q3

The Periodic Labour Force Survey for 2023–24 reported which of the following? 1. Labour force participation rate (15+, usual status) of about 60% 2. Unemployment rate of about 3.2% 3. Female labour force participation rate below 25%

  1. (a)1 and 2
  2. (b)2 and 3
  3. (c)1 and 3
  4. (d)1, 2 and 3
Show answer
Correct: (a)Female LFPR was 41.7%.
Q4

The Global Multidimensional Poverty Index is jointly published by:

  1. (a)World Bank and IMF
  2. (b)UNDP and the Oxford Poverty and Human Development Initiative
  3. (c)NITI Aayog and UNICEF
  4. (d)ILO and WHO
Show answer
Correct: (b)UNDP–OPHI since 2010.
Q5

Consider the following statements about India's poverty estimation: 1. The Tendulkar methodology estimated 21.9% poverty for 2011–12. 2. The Rangarajan Committee's higher estimate of 29.5% was officially adopted. 3. NITI Aayog's national MPI uses 12 indicators across three dimensions.

  1. (a)1 and 2
  2. (b)1 and 3
  3. (c)2 and 3
  4. (d)1, 2 and 3
Show answer
Correct: (b)Rangarajan report was never adopted.
Q6

The Global Hunger Index score is computed from four indicators. Which of the following is NOT one of them?

  1. (a)Child wasting
  2. (b)Child stunting
  3. (c)Under-five mortality
  4. (d)Anaemia among women
Show answer
Correct: (d)Fourth indicator is undernourishment.

Q8

  3. (c)8+4

Q9
