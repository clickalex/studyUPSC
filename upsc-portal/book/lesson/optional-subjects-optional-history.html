Answer Strategy

  • Historiography is the differentiator: "Cambridge school reads X as…; nationalist historiography reads…; subaltern studies recovers…" — deploy in every analytical answer.
  • Period-map discipline: P1 map questions (10-marker maps of sites/inscriptions) are free marks with practice.
  • Structure by theme inside chronology: economy/society/religion/polity frames per period block.
  • Quote sparingly but precisely: inscription lines (Allahabad pillar's Samudragupta claims), Abul Fazl categories, Gandhi's 1922 arrest-statement line.

Scoring Profile and Fit

  • Best for: humanities graduates; reading-lovers with writing stamina; GS-1 modern-history aspirants wanting compounding.

7. How Paper 1 Ancient–Early Medieval Is Won: Sources, States, and the Feudalism Debate

Paper 1's ancient block is won through source-criticism plus state-formation narratives — the candidate who says how we know before saying what happened outscores the candidate who narrates without sourcing. The sources unit must be carried as four classes with one debate each: archaeology (stratigraphy, NBPW–Ochre-Colour–Black-and-Red horizons, the Arikamedu–Taxila excavation lineages — Wheeler's method as the method debate); epigraphy (Ashokan edicts as contemporary record, Allahabad pillar as court poetry, Aihole as rival testimony — the \"inscriptions lie upward\" caution); numismatics (punch-marked to portrait to dinar — coinage as sovereignty, economy and art in one artefact); and literature (Vedic–Sutra–epic–Puranic strata, Sangam anthologies, Buddhist–Jain canons — genre governing reliability, corroboration as method). Prehistory (Soan–Narmada–Bhimbetka sequences; Mehrgarh's 7000-BCE Neolithic; the Harappan triad — town planning's grid-and-drainage, the undeciphered script's 375-odd signs, decline theories from Wheeler's \"Indra's massacre\" through Marshall's floods to the Ghaggar-desiccation and Aryan-migration syntheses) then feeds the state-formation spine: Vedic assemblies (sabha–samiti–vidatha) to mahajanapadas (Magadha's material advantages — iron, elephants, Ganga surplus); Mauryan centralisation (saptanga, dhamma-mahamattas, the edict-as-communication thesis) to post-Mauryan devolution (Indo-Greek portraiture, Kanishka's 78-CE era, Satavahana land-grants); Gupta classicism (Prayaga Prashasti's aryavarta–dakshinapatha policy, the agrahara grant economy) to Harsha's transitional assembly-state (Xuanzang's Nalanda, the Kannauj–Prayag rituals).

The feudalism debate is early medieval's gravitational centre and must be carried as a three-cornered historiographical fight. R.S. Sharma (Indian Feudalism, 1965): land grants with fiscal–judicial immunities from the Gupta period onward created a hierarchy of intermediaries (samantas), demonetisation and trade decline forced a closed village economy, and the jajmani system bound peasants — feudalism \"from above\" (state grants) and \"from below\" (peasant subjection). Harbans Mukhia (1979–80): India lacked feudalism's European essentials — no serfdom (peasants taxed, not bound), no manorial demesne, no contractual vassalage — proposing the free-peasantry–tributary-state alternative. B.D. Chattopadhyaya and the integrative school: grants as agrarian expansion and state formation (Brahmanas civilising tribal frontiers, temples as settlement nuclei — the \"early medieval transformation\" as growth, not crisis). The scoring answer states the agreed facts (grants multiplied, samantas thickened, Chola–Pala–Rashtrakuta–Gurjara-Pratihara regional states flourished, temple economies boomed — Thanjavur's Rajaraja I, Khajuraho's Chandellas, Konark's Gangas), then positions the interpretations as rival explanations of one evidence base. The Chola local-self-government sub-debate (Uttaramerur inscriptions — ur–sabha–nagaram autonomy celebrated by Sastri, qualified by Stein–Karashima as Brahmin-brahmadeya dominance) and the Indian Ocean trade line (Geniza letters, Chola naval expeditions — the 1025 Srivijaya raid — Arab–Chinese ceramics at Indian ports) complete the block: state, locality and ocean as the three scales of early-medieval analysis.

Temple architecture (Nagara–Dravida–Vesara with one dated monument each), coinage–trade linkages (the \"money-economy\" controversy — Sharma's shortage versus John's Deyell's continuity), and the Bhakti–Tantric religious currents (Alvar–Nayanar to Shankara's mathas) supply the culture-economy texture — but the block's marks cluster on states and debates, so revision time follows that gradient.

8. How Paper 1 Sultanate–Mughal Is Won: Institutions and the Agrarian Order

The medieval block rewards institutional comparison anchored in revenue systems — the candidate who explains how each state extracted surplus explains the state. The Sultanate spine runs iqta (Iltutmish's transferable revenue-assignments, Balban's audit discipline, Alauddin's measurement-and-cash nexus) → market control (the four markets, shahna-i-mandi, dagh–chehra — military-fiscal logic, terror-dependent, dying with its maker) → Tughlaq experimentation (token currency's mint-monopoly failure, Daulatabad's reversal, Firoz's canal-and-charity consolidation) → Lodi devolution (Afghan tribal kingship, gaz-i-Sikandari) — with Vijayanagara as the counter-system (amara-nayaka hereditary warlordism, varaha gold, horse-trade monopoly, the segmentary–fiscal debate) and the Bahmani–successor Deccan as the third idiom (taraf administration, Mahmud Gawan's audit, the Deccani–Afaqi sociology). The examination's favourite medieval question is implicitly comparative (Sultanate versus Mughal centralisation; iqta versus jagir; Alauddin versus Sher Shah revenue logic) — build the comparison tables in notes, because the candidate with the table writes the pairing while the candidate without it writes two descriptions.

The Mughal agrarian order is the block's summit and must be learned through the Aligarh school (Irfan Habib's Agrarian System of Mughal India, 1963): the zabti measurement system (Sher Shah's zabt refined by Todar Mal — ten-year average yields, cash schedules, the dahsala), the mansab–jagir nexus (ranked military-bureaucratic service paid in transferable revenue-assignments — the zat–sawar distinction, the dagh–chehra inheritance), the agrarian crisis thesis (jagir transferability incentivising short-term over-extraction → peasant flight and revolt — Jat, Satnami, Sikh uprisings as fiscal rebellions → the \"Deccan ulcer\" multiplying unfunded claims → imperial collapse), and the counter-theses (Athar Ali's nobility-composition data — the Mughal elite's ethnic arithmetic; Richards's Deccan-revenue revision; Blake's patrimonial-bureaucratic model; the \"great firm\" thesis — Bayly–Washbrook on banking houses as empire-makers). Sulh-i-kul (Akbar's universal tolerance — Din-i-Ilahi debated, mahzar 1579, Rajput marriage-alliance statecraft, Todar Mal–Birbal–Rahim inclusivity) against Aurangzeb's reversal (jizya 1679, temple orders, Deccan wars — the Sarkar-versus-revisionist debate on whether bigotry or overextension killed the empire) is the religious-policy pairing every answer needs. The Maratha polity (Shivaji's ashtapradhan–chauth–fort system; the Peshwa confederacy's tribute economics; Gordon's \"new\" Maratha history versus Sarkar's nationalist epic) and the 18th-century debate (decline — Sarkar, the \"bleeding Deccan\" — versus continuity — Bayly, Alam–Subrahmanyam's successor states, Marshall's \"Indianisation of the empire\" — vigorous regional economies under Nawabi–Maratha–Mysore rule) close Paper 1: the century that decides whether British conquest rescued a corpse or murdered a patient.

9. How Paper 2 Modern India Is Won: Land, Drain, and the Nationalist Readings

Paper 2's British-rule block runs on three settlements and one drain. The land settlements must be compared as political economies, not memorised as dates: Permanent Settlement (Cornwallis, 1793 — Bengal–Bihar–Odisha zamindars as proprietors, revenue fixed in perpetuity — the \"paternal landlord\" fantasy producing rack-renting, subinfeudation and the 1859–1885 tenancy-protection sequel); Ryotwari (Munro–Read, Madras–Bombay, 1820s — direct state–peasant settlement, periodic revision — the \"paternal despot\" model producing over-assessment and Deccan riots, 1875); Mahalwari (North-Western Provinces, 1822/1833 — village–estate (mahal) collective liability — the Bird–Thomason compromise). Each settlement's social effect (zamindar–moneylender–tenant triangles; tribal land-loss — Santhal–Munda rebellions as settlement revolts; commercialisation — indigo, opium, cotton — as forced market-integration) is the analysis; the deindustrialisation debate (Dutt–Gadgil–Bagchi: handloom collapse under Lancashire imports and tariff tyranny; Morris–Roy sceptics: productivity-gap realism, the jute–cotton mill counter-evidence) and the drain theory (Naoroji's Poverty and Un-British Rule, 1901 — Home Charges, salary remittances, the moral indictment; Dutt's economic history; the Habib–Bagchi refinements versus the \"drain as interest-payments\" minimisers) supply the nationalist-economics spine. Famine policy (the 1880–1901 Famine Codes after the Deccan–1876 and 1896–97 horrors; the \"Malthusian\" versus \"entitlement\" — Sen's 1981 demolition — readings; the Bengal 1943 coda — Amartya Sen's entitlement proof-text) and the constitutional line (Regulating 1773 → Pitt's 1784 → Charter Acts → 1858 transfer → Councils Acts → Morley–Minto 1909 → Montford 1919 → 1935 Act) complete the rule-apparatus: extraction, starvation-management and legitimation as the three faces of the Raj.

The national movement must be learned as events-plus-readings, because every major episode carries its historiographical triad. The formation debate (Congress 1885 — safety-valve (Lala Lajpat Rai's charge, Hume-as-conduit) versus nationalist ripening (Bipan Chandra) versus Cambridge factionalism (Anil Seal — presidency rivalries in nationalist dress)); Swadeshi (1905–08 — the boycott–swadeshi–national-education trinity; Surat's 1907 Moderates–Extremists split — Tilak–Gokhale as method-rivals); revolutionary currents (Anushilan–Ghadar–HRA — the \"individual heroism\" phase; Bhagat Singh's 1929–31 ideological turn — jail-notebook Marxism); Home Rule (1916–18 — Besant–Tilak as the organisational bridge); Gandhi's interventions (Champaran–Kheda–Ahmedabad 1917–18 as experiments; Non-Cooperation 1920–22 — Khilafat alliance, Chauri Chaura's 1922 suspension; Civil Disobedience 1930–34 — Dandi, the Gandhi–Irwin Pact, Karachi's rights resolution; Quit India 1942 — \"Do or Die,\" the parallel governments — Satara's Prati Sarkar, Ballia, Tamluk — the underground's Jayaprakash–Lohia network) — each read three ways (nationalist: awakening–sacrifice–victory; Cambridge: brokerage, faction, the Raj conceding to pressure; subaltern: Guha's Elementary Aspects — peasant insurgency's autonomous domain, Shahid Amin's Chauri Chaura event–metaphor, Chatterjee's nationalist thought as derivative discourse). The Left (1925–47 — CPI, CSP, the 1946 naval mutiny–Tebhaga–Telangana arc), communalism's trajectory (separate electorates 1909 → Lucknow Pact 1916 → Khilafat–collapse → 1937 elections → Lahore Resolution 1940 → Direct Action 1946 → Partition — the Brass–Pandey–Jalal readings), and the negotiations (Cripps 1942, Wavell Plan–Simla 1945, Cabinet Mission 1946, Mountbatten Plan June 1947 — the transfer's haste as Partition's accelerant) close the movement: the examination rewards the candidate who can argue any episode in all three historiographical voices.

Early independent India (Patel's integration — Hyderabad's 1948 Police Action, Junagadh's plebiscite, Kashmir's 1947–48 war-and-reference; Constitution-making; the Nehruvian consensus — Mahalanobis planning, non-alignment's Bandung–Belgrade arc, the linguistic reorganisation to 1956; the 1964-cutoff debates — war, succession, devaluation) is the short coda — high-yield per page because rarely over-prepared.

10. How World History Is Won: Arcs, Theories, and the Case-Study Method

World history is the optional's breadth challenge — five centuries, six continents — and the winning strategy is arcs plus cases: each unit learned as one causal arc illustrated by two anchored cases, never as narrative soup. The Enlightenment-to-revolutions arc (print–reason–rights → American 1776 (no-taxation-without-representation, the constitutional-convention laboratory) → French 1789 (Sieyès to Thermidor — the Marxist–revisionist–cultural debate: Soboul's bourgeois revolution versus Furet–Cobban's sceptics versus Hunt's culture) → Haitian 1791–1804 (the Black Jacobins — James — slavery's only self-emancipation) → Latin American 1810s (Bolívar–San Martín, the creole revolutions)); the industrialisation arc (British take-off — coal–iron–textile–canal, the enclosure–factory–urban sequence; the social-history response — Thompson's Making of the English Working Class, Engels's Manchester; the second industrialisers — German–American–Japanese state-led paths — Gerschenkron's backwardness thesis); the nation-state arc (Italian–German unifications — Cavour–Bismarck as contrasting unifiers, the 1848 \"springtime\" prelude; Eastern-question nationalisms — Greek 1821 to Balkan 1912–13); the imperialism arc (Hobson's underconsumption → Lenin's monopoly-finance-capital → Cain–Hopkins's gentlemanly capitalism → Gallagher–Robinson's \"imperialism of free trade\" — theories debated through cases: Congo, South Africa's mineral revolution, the Scramble's Berlin 1884–85 rules).

The twentieth century runs as crisis-and-order: World War I (Fischer's German-aims thesis versus sleepwalkers — Clark; Versailles's war-guilt–reparations–self-determination trilemma — Keynes's Economic Consequences as contemporary verdict); the inter-war crisis (the Depression — Friedman–Schwartz monetary versus Keynesian demand readings, Smoot–Hawley's tariff spiral; Italian Fascism — Mussolini's squadristi-to-state march; German Nazism — Weimar's structural weaknesses, the Sonderweg debate — Fischer–Wehler versus Blackbourn–Eley, the Holocaust's intentionalist–functionalist historiography — Hillgruber–Broszat); World War II (appeasement — Chamberlain's deterrence-failure versus revisionist buying-time; the Eastern Front as the war's graveyard — Stalingrad–Kursk; atomic decision — Alperovitz's revisionism versus the invasion-casualty defence); decolonisation (India 1947 as negotiated transfer, Algeria 1954–62 as war, Ghana 1957 as sub-Saharan first, Vietnam as anti-imperial epic — Fanon's Wretched as theory, the neo-colonialism — Nkrumah — coda); and the Cold War (origins — orthodox versus revisionist versus post-revisionist; Cuban 1962 as apex; détente–Second Cold War–Gorbachev's \"new thinking\" — the end debated: Reagan-pressure versus Soviet-rot versus contingency). Norman Lowe supplies the narrative floor; the historiography dossier (one reading per debate — Furet, Thompson, Fischer, Fanon, Gaddis) supplies the ceiling. The examination's world-history questions are overwhelmingly \"why\" and \"compare\" — carry causes and pairings (French–Russian revolutions, Italian–German unifications, British–French decolonisations), never chronicles.

Trap watch. Wheeler's Indra-massacre Harappan decline is abandoned — carry desiccation-plus-shift syntheses, not invasions. Sharma (feudalism-from-grants) versus Mukhia (no-serfdom rebuttal) versus Chattopadhyaya (formation-through-expansion) — three corners, one evidence base. Stein's segmentary Vijayanagara answers Sastri's centralised empire; Subrahmanyam's fiscal-military core synthesises. Habib's jagir-crisis explains Mughal decline economically; Athar Ali's nobility data explains it factionally — deploy both. The 18th century is Bayly–Alam continuity against Sarkar decline — know which century you are arguing about. Naoroji's drain (1901) needs the Habib–Bagchi refinement and the Morris sceptic noted. Chauri Chaura (February 1922) suspends Non-Cooperation; Dandi (March–April 1930) opens Civil Disobedience; \"Do or Die\" (August 1942) opens Quit India — three Gandhian openings, three dates. Subaltern studies (Guha, Amin, Chatterjee) recovers autonomous domains against both nationalist and Cambridge readings — deploy the triad, never one voice. Fischer (German war-guilt continuity) versus Clark (sleepwalkers) is the WWI-origins pair; Soboul (bourgeois 1789) versus Furet (discourse 1789) is the French pair. Berlin 1884–85 regulated the Scramble; Bandung 1955 launched non-alignment's Afro-Asian arc. Map questions (Paper 1, 10 markers) are free marks — atlas hours from week one.

Previous Year Questions

9 questions

Paper-wise patterns framed on recurring themes (official papers carry verbatim texts). Model approaches included.

Paper 1 — pattern questions

Q1

"The Harappan decline is a palimpsest of environmental, hydrological and social readings." Discuss. (15 marks)

Approach: decline-theory ledger — Ghaggar-Hakra desiccation, monsoon weakening (palaeoclimate proxies), river shifts, trade-network disruption, elite-crisis readings → caution: no single-cause verdict → de-urbanisation into late-Harappan cultures → conclude: layered transformation, not annihilation.

Q2

Evaluate the Mauryan state through Kautilyan and Ashokan lenses. (15 marks)

Approach: Kautilya — centralised treasury-army-spy architecture (saptanga frame) vs Ashokan inscriptions — dhamma as welfare-morality state, provincial viceroys → tensions: coercive-administrative vs ethical-integrative → legacy readings (Thapar's synthesis) → map: pillar/rock-edict spread.

Q3

"The feudalism debate is about the state as much as about land." Examine. (10 marks)

Approach: Sharma's feudalism-from-above/below (land grants, decentralisation, peasantry subordination) → Mukhia's critique (Indian exception: no serfdom, mobility evidence) → state form: samanta hierarchy → verdict: debate refined into early-medieval state-formation frames (integrative polity model).

Q4

Assess the Chola local-self-government evidence and its historiography. (10 marks)

Approach: Uttaramerur inscription — sabha committees (kudavolai system) → qualifications/disqualifications → historiographic caution: elite-brahmadeya skew; "self-government" romanticism vs state-integration readings → credit with caveats.

Q5

Explain the mansab-jagir system's crisis in the 17th century. (10 marks)

Approach: design (zat-sawar ranks, escheat logic) → pressures: be-jagir crisis, price inflation (Abul Fazl vs 17th-c indices), succession wars → consequences: agrarian strain, Deccan campaigns → debate link: Mughal decline theses (satellite-states vs crisis readings).

Paper 2 — pattern questions

Q6

"The Permanent Settlement froze agriculture and unlocked property." Analyse. (10 marks)

Approach: design (Cornwallis, zamindar-as-collector) → effects: absconding auctions, subinfeudation, rent-receiver economy → agrarian stagnation vs secure-property claim tested (productivity data) → comparison: ryotwari's state-facing ryot → long shadow: land-reform era targets.
