4. The Slave Dynasty to the Khaljis: Building the Sultanate State

The Sultanate's first century (1206–1320) is the story of a military occupation converting itself into a state, and each reign contributes one institution to the conversion. Qutbuddin Aibak (1206–10) — Muhammad Ghori's manumitted slave-commander, ruling from Lahore — left little beyond the Quwwat-ul-Islam mosque's screen and the Arhai-din-ka-Jhonpra: a garrison regime, not yet a sultanate. Iltutmish (1211–36) built the actual state: shifting the capital to Delhi, institutionalising the iqta system (revenue assignments to muqtis in lieu of salary, transferable and audited — the fiscal-military spine of the Sultanate), issuing the tanka–jital bimetallic coinage (silver tanka, copper jital — India's first sustained Islamic coinage), completing the Qutub Minar complex, and organising the Chihalgani (the Corps of Forty slave-nobles) as both administration and kingmaker collective. His daughter Razia (1236–40) — the only woman sovereign of the Sultanate, ruling unveiled, leading armies, appointing the Abyssinian Jamaluddin Yaqut to high office — tested whether the slave-military oligarchy would accept a woman's authority; her deposition and death answered no, and the lesson (gender trumped competence in the Chihalgani's calculus) is the examination's portable point about military-slave regimes.

Balban (1266–87) then supplied the Sultanate's theory of power: kingship as divine shadow (zil-i-Ilahi), Persian court etiquette (sijda prostration, paibos foot-kissing), a feared spy system (barids), and the systematic destruction of the Chihalgani itself — the slave-nobles who had made and unmade sultans were executed, exiled or broken, and Turkish exclusivity was enforced against Indian-Muslim aspirants. Balban's reign is thus the paradox to carry: the strongest Slave sultan left the weakest succession (his chosen heir Muhammad dead against the Mongols, the infant Kaiqubad collapsing into the Khalji coup of 1290), because despotism personalised power it could not institutionalise. The Khalji revolution (Jalaluddin, 1290–96, murdered by his nephew Alauddin) was three ruptures at once: racial (Khaljis were Afghanised Turks, not the Ilbari slave elite), geographic (the centre of gravity shifting toward the Deccan), and doctrinal — Alauddin (1296–1316) explicitly subordinated the ulema to raison d'état (\"I do not know whether this is lawful or unlawful; whatever I think to be for the good of the State, that I decree\" — Barani's famous report), taxed the Hindu agrarian surplus at half the produce (kharaj at 50 per cent in the Doab, measured by the biswa standard), and built the first permanent standing army paid in cash — the fiscal-military machine that funded both the Mongol repulses (1297–1308, culminating in the slaughter that ended the invasions) and the southern thrusts.

The southern thrusts deserve causal attention because they redrew India's political map. Malik Kafur's Deccan campaigns (1307–11) — Devagiri's Yadavas reduced to tribute, Warangal's Kakatiyas plundered, the Hoysalas and Pandyas raided to Madurai — were plunder raids, not annexations: the Sultanate extracted the south's accumulated temple wealth (the Warangal booty famously funding Alauddin's projects) without attempting to govern it. That restraint was strategic (the north's Mongol frontier consumed the army) and temporary — Muhammad Tughlaq would attempt the annexation Alauddin avoided, with consequences the next section traces. Chittor's fall (1303, the Padmini tradition that Malik Muhammad Jayasi's Padmavat, 1540, would immortalise two centuries later) and Ranthambore (1301) meanwhile consolidated Rajputana's east — sieges, jauhar and the Rajput–Sultanate accommodation pattern (service in exchange for internal autonomy) that the Mughals would inherit and perfect.

5. Alauddin's Market Reforms and the Tughlaq Experiments: Political Economy at the Extremes

Alauddin's market control is the most examined economic policy of the medieval period, and its logic must be grasped as military-fiscal, not welfarist. A large standing army paid in cash required either high salaries (fiscally impossible) or low prices (administratively achievable): Alauddin fixed prices across four markets (grain, cloth, horses–cattle–slaves, miscellaneous), licensed merchants, created the shahna-i-mandi (market controller) with draconian enforcement (Barani's severed-ears anecdotes), monopolised grain movement (no hoarding, state granaries, transport permits), and cut out intermediaries — financing cheap soldiers by squeezing traders and peasants simultaneously. The control's prerequisites were the agrarian squeeze (50 per cent kharaj, measurement, the abolition of muqti perquisites) and the military-administrative pair of dagh (horse-branding against substitution fraud) and chehra (descriptive rolls of soldiers against muster fraud) — the audit technologies that made a cash-paid army possible. The system worked while Alauddin lived and collapsed with him (1316), because it depended on terror rather than institutions — the examination's portable verdict on command economies without consent.

Muhammad bin Tughlaq (1325–51) — literate in logic, astronomy and Persian poetry, fluent in the administrative sciences, and the Sultanate's most ambitious failure — ran the complementary experiment: rationalist reform without political feasibility. The four \"projects\" each encode a lesson. The token currency (bronze/copper coins at silver-tanka value, c. 1330) was monetarily sound in conception (fiduciary coinage, centuries before Europe's) and catastrophic in execution — no state monopoly on minting, so every household became a mint (Barani: \"the house of every Hindu became a mint\"), forgery exploded, and the Sultan had to redeem the tokens at ruinous cost. The Daulatabad transfer (1327 — Delhi's elite marched 1,500 km to Devagiri, renamed the second capital, then the project reversed) grasped the Deccan's strategic importance correctly and the human cost catastrophically. The Khurasan expedition (a 370,000-strong army raised for Central Asia, then disbanded — paid salary wasted) and the Qarachil expedition (the Kumaon hills disaster, the army destroyed by terrain and disease) demonstrated logistical overreach. Only the agricultural programme (diwan-i-amir-kohi — state loans/taccavi, model farms, well-digging) pointed forward, to Firoz's canals and eventually to Mughal taccavi. Ibn Battuta's Rihla — eight years at the Tughlaq court (1334–42) as qazi, then the Maldives–China odyssey — supplies the outsider's portrait: a generous, learned, terrifying sovereign whose gifts and executions arrived with equal unpredictability.

Firoz Shah Tughlaq (1351–88) governed as Muhammad's photographic negative: where his cousin experimented, Firoz consolidated — four canals (Yamuna–Sutlej lines watering the Hissar–Firozabad belt), the diwan-i-khairat (charity for orphans and widows) and diwan-i-istihqaq (pensions), an employment bureau (diwan-i-bandagan, controversially stuffed with 180,000 slaves), the Firoz Shah Kotla complex (with Ashokan pillars transported from Topra and Meerut — antiquarianism as legitimation), the Hauz Khas madrasa, and jizya's systematic re-imposition alongside Brahmin inclusion (previously exempt). The verdict to carry is that Firoz's benevolence was fiscally consumptive (assignments made hereditary, army bloated with slaves, no new revenue) — the state lived off Muhammad's accumulated reserves, and when Timur sacked Delhi (1398), the husk collapsed into the Sayyid–Lodi twilight. Alauddin squeezed, Muhammad experimented, Firoz spent: three political economies, one arc of rise and exhaustion.

6. Society, Faith, and the Sufi–Bhakti Ferment

The Sultanate's social order stratified along two axes — religion and race — and the axes' interaction is the analysis. The ruling ashraf (foreign-origin Muslims — Turks, Persians, Afghans, Sayyids) monopolised high office under the early Slave–Khalji regimes (Balban's Turkish exclusivity as the extreme), while Indian converts (ajlaf) and Hindus filled the revenue machinery's lower rungs (the khuts, muqaddams and patwaris without whom no Sultan collected a dam). The jizya (poll tax on non-Muslims, remitted for Brahmins till Firoz, for the indigent and monks throughout) functioned simultaneously as fiscal instrument, status marker and theological statement — its imposition–composition history across regimes (Alauddin's severity, Muhammad's indifference, Firoz's systematisation) tracks each Sultan's ulema bargain. Slavery was institutional (military slaves as the Slave dynasty's ruling class, Firoz's bandagan as welfare-employment, female slaves as the harem–household economy), and the ulema — theologians staffing the diwan-i-risalat, qazi-ships and madrasas — pressed perpetually for sharia maximalism against sultans who ruled a 90-per-cent non-Muslim population and mostly chose pragmatism (Alauddin openly, Muhammad Tughlaq intellectually, even Firoz selectively).

Against this official Islam flowered the Sufi orders, the Sultanate's deepest cultural legacy. The Chishtis (Moinuddin Chishti of Ajmer, d. 1236; Qutbuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki; Fariduddin Ganjshakar of Pakpattan; Nizamuddin Auliya of Delhi, d. 1325 — \"Hunuz Dilli dur ast,\" Delhi is yet far, to the threatening Sultan; Nasiruddin Chiragh Dehlavi) preached poverty, love and distance from courts — their khanqahs feeding the poor, their sama music drawing multitudes, their shrines becoming the subcontinent's enduring pilgrimage geography. The Suhrawardis (Bahauddin Zakariya of Multan) accepted state patronage and wealth, embedding orthodoxy in the Punjab–Sind elite. The Firdausis (Sharfuddin Yahya Maneri of Bihar) lettered the Hanafi synthesis into vernacular reach. The Chishti–court tension (Nizamuddin refusing Alauddin's summonses; Muhammad Tughlaq coercing saints into state service) is the examination's portable drama: spiritual authority rivalling temporal power, with the populace voting by pilgrimage. Parallel ran the Bhakti seeds: Ramananda's northward Ramanuja-lineage devotion (the Adhyatma Ramayana milieu, Kabir and Ravidas in the next century's full flowering), the Nath–Siddha substrate, and Maharashtra's Varkari beginnings — Hindu devotionalism democratising worship just as Sufism democratised Islam, the twin Bhakti–Sufi ferment that would define the 15th–16th centuries' religious revolution.

Women's and labouring history supplies the closing analytical notes. Razia's fall notwithstanding, Sultanate women of the elite (Shah Turkan, Malika Jahan, the Lodi queens) wielded regency power; lower-order women laboured in the agrarian–artisanal economy the chronicles ignore — the begar and bonded obligations, the slave markets of Delhi and Multan, the devadasi–courtesan continuums. The economy underneath (tankas and jitals, the biswa and gaz-i-Sikandari metrologies, Firoz's canals, Alauddin's granaries, the horse–slave–textile trades, the Multan–Delhi–Lakhnauti commercial axis) sustained a Persianate court culture — Barani's Tarikh-i-Firoz Shahi and Fatwa-i-Jahandari (the mirror-for-princes that doubles as the Sultanate's political theory), Amir Khusrau's impossible versatility (courtier of seven sultans, Khazain-ul-Futuh and Nuh Siphir, the khayal–tarana–qawwali attributions, Hindavi verses that seed Urdu), Isami's Futuh-us-Salatin (Bahmani legitimation), Yahya Sirhindi's Sayyid chronicle — a historiography so rich that the Sultanate is India's first period knowable week-by-week, provided the courtier's flattery is discounted.

7. Architecture of Power: From Spoilia to the Lodi Garden Tomb

Sultanate architecture is a technology transfer written in stone — the true arch, the dome, the minaret and geometric ornament arriving with the Ghurids and Indianising across three centuries — and each phase has its diagnostic monument. The Mamluk phase (1192–1290) built in spoilia: the Quwwat-ul-Islam mosque (1193–98, reusing temple pillars under a corbelled screen — the arch announced but not yet engineered), the Qutub Minar (Aibak's first storey, Iltutmish's three, Firoz's two — victory tower, mazina and cosmic axis in fluted sandstone), the Arhai-din-ka-Jhonpra (Ajmer), and Iltutmish's own tomb (the first Islamic tomb in India, its mihrab-dense interior prefiguring the tomb-garden to come). The tomb of Balban (the first true arch — a modest ruin carrying a revolutionary voussoir) closes the apprenticeship: by 1290 the engineers could span.

The Khalji phase (1290–1320) spans triumphantly: the Alai Darwaza (1311 — the first true dome on squinch arches, red sandstone and marble inlay rather than spoil, calligraphic bands as ornament — the single monument every answer must name), Siri's walls, the Hauz Khas tank, and Jamait Khana's mosque-line. The Tughlaq phase (1320–1414) builds in the \"crisis style\" — sloping battered walls (Tughlaqabad's cyclopean ramparts, the tomb of Ghiyasuddin), grey quartzite austerity, octagonal innovating (Khan-i-Jahan Tilangani's tomb), and Firoz's welfare architecture (Kotla, madrasas, canal-head towns) — power expressed as mass rather than ornament, with the Kalan Masjid and Khirki's cruciform plan showing the style's late sophistication. The Sayyid–Lodi phase (1414–1526) perfects the garden tomb: square and octagonal types (the octagon — Khan-i-Jahan to Mubarak Sayyid to Sikandar Lodi — as the Sultanate's signature), the double dome (Sikandar Lodi's tomb — inner ceiling, outer profile, the device the Mughals would monumentalise), glazed-tile ornament, and the Lodi Gardens ensemble (Bara Gumbad, Shisha Gumbad, the Bara–Shisha mosque pair) — elegy in sandstone for a dying sultanate. The provinces counterpoint Delhi throughout: the Sharqi Atala–Jama pair at Jaunpur, Bengal's Adina–Eklakhi brick (Sikandar Shah's Adina — the subcontinent's largest mosque at building), Malwa's Jahaz Mahal–Hindola Mahal (Mandu's Afghan romanticism), Gujarat's Champaner–Ahmedabad synthesis (Sidi Saiyyed's tree-of-life jali in the next century's maturity) — regional Sultanates proving the style had become Indian before the Mughals arrived to crown it.

Trap watch. Iltutmish (not Aibak) institutionalised the iqta, issued tanka–jital, shifted to Delhi and organised the Chihalgani; Aibak ruled from Lahore and left the Qutub's first storey. Razia (1236–40) is the only woman sovereign; Balban (zil-i-Ilahi, sijda–paibos, barids) destroyed the Forty he rose from. Alauddin's market control (four markets, shahna-i-mandi) financed the cash-paid standing army — dagh (horse-brand) and chehra (descriptive roll) are the audit pair; biswa is his land-measure. Token currency (c. 1330, no mint monopoly — forgery) and the Daulatabad transfer (1327, reversed) are Muhammad Tughlaq's; Khurasan and Qarachil are the failed expeditions; diwan-i-amir-kohi is the agricultural board. Firoz built four canals, the diwan-i-khairat charity and Kotla (Topra–Meerut Ashokan pillars shifted there); Timur sacked Delhi in 1398, collapsing the Tughlaqs into Sayyids. Sikandar Lodi founded Agra (1504) and the gaz-i-Sikandari; Ibrahim lost Panipat-I (1526) to Babur's invitation-by-Daulat-Khan. The first true arch is Balban's tomb; the first true dome is the Alai Darwaza (1311); the double dome debuts under the Lodis. Nizamuddin Auliya (d. 1325, \"Hunuz Dilli dur ast\") is Chishti; Bahauddin Zakariya (Multan) is Suhrawardi; Sharfuddin Yahya Maneri (Bihar) is Firdausi. Ibn Battuta served Muhammad Tughlaq (Rihla, 1334–42); Barani chronicled Firoz's reign; Amir Khusrau served seven sultans. Abdur Razzaq visited Devaraya II's Vijayanagara and Nikitin the Bahmanis — neither belongs to Delhi.
