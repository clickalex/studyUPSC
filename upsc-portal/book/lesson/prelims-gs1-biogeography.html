Biomes, diversity metrics, island theory, soil-vegetation links. Light-read area with trick MCQs.

1. Biomes & vegetation belts

  • Whittaker's frame: temperature x precipitation
  • biome. Sequence pole-to-equator: tundra
  • taiga (boreal coniferous)
  • temperate deciduous-grassland
  • Mediterranean scrub
  • desert
  • tropical rainforest (max NPP)
  • savanna.
  • India forest types (Champion-Seth 1968, 16 major groups): tropical evergreen (Western Ghats-NE-Andaman), tropical deciduous (most extensive — moist then dry), tropical thorn (NW), montane (oak-conifer above), littoral-swamp (mangroves), alpine. Forest Survey of India (ISFR biennial): forest+tree cover ~25% of geographic area (verify latest ISFR figure).

2. Biodiversity metrics & hotspots

  • Metrics: alpha (within habitat), beta (between-habitat change), gamma (landscape total). Endemism: species confined to an area (India: high in Himalaya-Western Ghats-Andaman). India's 4 hotspots (of 36 global): Himalaya, Indo-Burma, Western Ghats-Sri Lanka, Sundaland (Nicobars).
  • Species roles: keystone (ecosystem-defining: tigers, sea otters), flagship (iconic: panda), umbrella, indicator; IUCN categories: EX-EW-CR-EN-VU-NT-LC-DD-NE; Red List updates annual.
  • Island biogeography (MacArthur-Wilson 1967): species richness rises with area, falls with isolation; immigration-extinction balance; SIBA-conservation design basis.

3. Soil-vegetation geography

  • Soil classification (ICAR 8-fold): alluvial (largest; khadar-new vs bangar-old), black/regur (cotton; Deccan basalt, moisture-retentive, self-ploughing), red (ferruginous, weathered crystalline), laterite (intense leaching; brick-tea-cashew), arid (calcified; kankar layers), saline-usara (alkaline; irrigation mismanagement), peaty-organic (Kerala), forest-mountain soils.
  • Soil-vegetation coupling: laterite ↔ degraded deciduous
  • black ↔ dry savanna-ish zones
  • alluvium ↔ agriculture belt
  • erosion (sheet-rill-gully-ravine — Chambal badlands) + conservation (contour bunding, watershed, agroforestry).

⚡ MCQ hooks

  • India's 4 hotspots; Andaman-Nicobar chain sits under the Sundaland hotspot.
  • Champion-Seth classification 1968; tropical deciduous = most extensive type.

  • Black soil = regur = Deccan basalt; laterite = leaching (not lava).

4. Biomes: The Global Pattern of Life

A biome is a large-scale community of plants and animals determined chiefly by climate — specifically the combination of temperature and precipitation and their seasonality. Because climate is arranged latitudinally, biomes form broad zonal belts, modified by altitude, continentality and ocean currents. The parallel between latitudinal and altitudinal zonation — climbing a tropical mountain reproduces in a few kilometres the vegetation sequence found over thousands of kilometres of latitude — is a standard question.

  • Tropical rainforest (equatorial, Af). Between roughly 10° north and south
  • hot and wet throughout the year with no dry season
  • evergreen, multi-storeyed with emergent, canopy, understorey and forest floor layers
  • the world's greatest biodiversity
  • buttress roots, lianas, epiphytes and drip-tip leaves. The paradox to remember: luxuriant vegetation on poor lateritic soils, because nutrients are held in the biomass and rapidly recycled, so clearance leads to swift degradation. Amazon, Congo, Southeast Asia
  • hardwoods mahogany, ebony, rosewood.
  • Tropical deciduous / monsoon forest (Aw, Am). Marked dry season; trees shed leaves to conserve moisture; less dense, more commercially exploitable — teak, sal, sandalwood, bamboo. This is India's most extensive natural forest type.
  • Tropical savanna. Tall coarse grasses with scattered drought-resistant trees such as acacia and baobab; a distinct wet and dry season; maintained partly by fire and grazing, which is why some ecologists treat it as a fire-climax rather than a climatic climax. Home to the great African herbivore-carnivore assemblage.
  • Hot desert (BW). Located on the western margins of continents around 20–30° under the subtropical high, reinforced by cold offshore currents and, in some cases, rain shadow. Xerophytic vegetation — succulents, deep tap roots, reduced leaves, ephemerals. Sahara, Kalahari, Atacama, Thar, Great Australian.
  • Mediterranean (Cs). The only biome with a dry summer and wet winter, on western margins around 30–40°; sclerophyllous evergreen scrub with thick waxy leaves — chaparral in California, maquis in the Mediterranean, mallee in Australia, fynbos in South Africa. Fire-adapted. Agriculturally famous for citrus, olives and viticulture.
  • Temperate grassland. Continental interiors, treeless with deep fertile chernozem soils — Prairies, Pampas, Steppes, Velds, Downs, Canterbury. The world's granaries.
  • Temperate deciduous forest. Oak, beech, maple, elm; four distinct seasons; heavily cleared for settlement.
  • Taiga / boreal coniferous forest. The largest continuous land biome, a belt across Canada, Scandinavia and Siberia; low species diversity, conical evergreen conifers shedding snow, acidic podzol soils; the world's chief softwood and pulp source. Absent in the southern hemisphere for want of land at those latitudes.
  • Tundra. Permafrost, very short growing season, no trees
  • mosses, lichens and dwarf shrubs
  • migratory caribou and reindeer
  • extremely fragile with slow recovery, and a vast store of frozen carbon whose thawing releases methane.

5. Dispersal, Barriers and Biogeographic Realms

The distribution of organisms is explained by origin, dispersal and barriers. Dispersal occurs by wind, water, animals and human agency; barriers may be physical (oceans, mountains, deserts), climatic or biological (competition, predation). Endemism — species restricted to a defined area — is highest where isolation has been long and habitats diverse, hence on islands, in isolated mountain systems and in ancient landmasses. Madagascar, Australia, the Western Ghats and the Galapagos are the standard exemplars.

Continental drift supplies the deep-time explanation. The break-up of Pangaea into Laurasia and Gondwana separated lineages and produced the disjunct distributions that puzzled early naturalists — the southern beech Nothofagus and ratite birds (ostrich, emu, rhea, cassowary, kiwi) across South America, Africa, Australia and New Zealand are Gondwanan relics. India's northward drift from Gondwana and its collision with Asia explains the mixture of African, Malayan and Palaearctic elements in Indian biota, and the presence of Gondwanan relicts in the Western Ghats such as the caecilians and the purple frog.

Wallace's Line, between Bali and Lombok and between Borneo and Sulawesi, separates the Oriental and Australian faunal realms, marking the abrupt transition from placental mammals to marsupials across a deep-water strait that persisted even at glacial low sea levels. Weber's Line and Lydekker's Line bound the transitional zone called Wallacea. Alfred Russel Wallace is accordingly the father of zoogeography and independent co-discoverer of natural selection.

The six classical zoogeographic realms are the Palaearctic (Europe, North Africa, northern Asia), Nearctic (North America), Neotropical (South and Central America — the richest, with marsupials, edentates and hummingbirds), Ethiopian/Afrotropical (sub-Saharan Africa), Oriental/Indomalayan (South and Southeast Asia) and Australian (marsupials and monotremes), with Antarctic and Oceanic sometimes added. Island biogeography, formalised by MacArthur and Wilson, holds that species richness on an island reflects an equilibrium between immigration and extinction, so larger and less isolated islands hold more species — the theoretical basis for reserve design and the debate over SLOSS, a Single Large Or Several Small protected area.

6. India's Biogeographic Zones and Vegetation

The Rodgers and Panwar classification divides India into ten biogeographic zones: Trans-Himalaya (cold desert of Ladakh and Spiti, snow leopard and wild ass), Himalaya, Desert (Thar and Rann), Semi-Arid (Gujarat-Rajputana, the Asiatic lion's Gir), Western Ghats, Deccan Peninsula (the largest zone), Gangetic Plain, North-East India, Islands (Andaman, Nicobar and Lakshadweep) and Coasts. Do not confuse these ten biogeographic zones with Champion and Seth's sixteen forest types or with the biodiversity hotspots.

Champion and Seth's five major groups, in order of decreasing moisture and temperature: tropical, montane subtropical, montane temperate, sub-alpine and alpine — giving sixteen types, of which tropical moist deciduous and tropical dry deciduous dominate by area.

India's four biodiversity hotspots — a hotspot requiring at least 1,500 endemic vascular plant species and the loss of at least 70% of its original habitat — are the Himalaya, the Western Ghats and Sri Lanka, the Indo-Burma region and Sundaland, which reaches India through the Nicobar Islands. India occupies about 2.4% of the world's land area but holds roughly 8% of recorded species, and is one of the seventeen megadiverse countries.

Altitudinal zonation in the Himalaya, a reliable question: tropical moist deciduous and sal below about 1,000 m; subtropical pine (chir) from 1,000 to 2,000 m; temperate wet and moist forest of oak, rhododendron, deodar, spruce and fir from 2,000 to 3,000 m; sub-alpine birch and juniper from 3,000 to 3,800 m; alpine meadows or bugyals above the treeline; and permanent snow above the snowline, which lies lower in the eastern Himalaya and on northern slopes because of aspect and higher precipitation.

Special vegetation types: mangroves of the Sundarbans, Bhitarkanika and Pichavaram, with pneumatophores, vivipary and salt-secreting leaves, serving as cyclone buffers and nurseries; coral reefs of the Gulf of Mannar, Gulf of Kachchh, Lakshadweep and the Andamans; shola-grassland mosaics of the high Western Ghats; the Terai-Bhabar belt; and myristica swamps. Threats are habitat fragmentation, invasive alien species such as Lantana camara, Prosopis juliflora and water hyacinth, climate-driven upslope range shifts, and linear infrastructure severing corridors.

Trap watch. Rainforests have poor lateritic soils; fertility is in the biomass. The Mediterranean biome alone has dry summers and wet winters. Taiga is absent in the southern hemisphere. India has ten biogeographic zones (Rodgers and Panwar), sixteen forest types (Champion and Seth) and four biodiversity hotspots — three separate schemes. Wallace's Line separates the Oriental from the Australian realm; Wallacea lies between Weber's and Lydekker's Lines. Island biogeography balances immigration against extinction. The Deccan Peninsula is India's largest biogeographic zone. Chernozem belongs to temperate grassland, podzol to taiga.
