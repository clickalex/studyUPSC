Biodiversity of India: Hotspots & Protected Areas

Detailed Study

The full treatment of this lesson.

Hotspots, species-terms, IUCN categories, legal-institutional frame (2002 Act + 2023 amendment).

1. India's biodiversity profile

  • Megadiverse country (~17 of world's stated species on 2.4% land — figure bands vary; 4/36 global hotspots): Himalaya, Indo-Burma, Western Ghats-Sri Lanka, Sundaland (Nicobar group); high endemism in Ghats (amphibians-reptiles) + Himalaya; India: ~45,000+ plant, ~91,000+ animal species described (recall bands, not decimals).
  • Species-role terms: keystone (disproportionate trophic effect — sea otter-tiger), flagship (tiger-panda PR), umbrella (habitat-wide protection), indicator (amphibians-lichens for pollution), endemic (restricted range), invasive (Lantana-Water hyacinth-Prosopis).

2. IUCN framework

  • Red List categories: Extinct → Extinct in Wild → Critically Endangered → Endangered → Vulnerable → Near Threatened → Least Concern (+ Data Deficient, Not Evaluated); CR+EN+VU = 'threatened'; criteria: population decline-range-size-fragmentation; Red List ≠ Red Data Book (book = document record).
  • India examples band: GIB-vulture-gharial = CR lineage; tiger-Asiatic lion = EN; snow leopard = VU (2017 reassessment); recall category-movement questions (e.g., IUCN 2021-24: migratory monarch up-listed EN).

3. Legal & institutional frame

  • Biological Diversity Act 2002: NBA (Chennai) — access-benefit sharing; SBBs (states); BMCs (local; People's Biodiversity Registers); 2023 amendment: ex-situ exemption eased, AYUSH fast-track (registered AYUSH practitioners become notified users), foreign-company penalties decriminalised (adjudication officer), foreign-controlled Indian companies treated differently in benefit-sharing rules.
  • Access-benefit: Abs under Nagoya Protocol (2010, India party 2012); community protocol-BRS frame; MFP-tribal linkage (FRA 2006 minor forest produce rights — separate act, pair-ask).
  • Ex-situ: NBPGR gene banks, seed banks, cryopreservation, botanical-zoological gardens; in-situ: parks-sanctuaries-biosphere reserves (UNESCO MAB) — triad often asked.

MCQ hooks

  • India hotspots = 4 (Himalaya, Indo-Burma, Ghats-Sri Lanka, Sundaland); Nicobar = Sundaland limb.
  • Threatened = CR+EN+VU (NT is not threatened).
  • Keystone vs flagship vs umbrella — role-swap trap.
  • NBA = Chennai; ABS flow: BMC → SBB → NBA — ladder ask.
  • 2023 amendment: decriminalisation + AYUSH relaxation.
  • Nagoya = ABS protocol under CBD (2010).
  • Red List (IUCN) vs Red Data Book — document pair.

6. India's Biogeographic Zones and Forest Types

India occupies about 2.4% of the world's land area but holds roughly 8% of recorded species, making it one of the 17 megadiverse countries. The Wildlife Institute of India divides the country into ten biogeographic zones: Trans-Himalaya, Himalaya, Desert, Semi-Arid, Western Ghats, Deccan Peninsula, Gangetic Plain, North-East India, Islands and Coasts. This scheme is distinct from the four biodiversity hotspots (Himalaya, Western Ghats–Sri Lanka, Indo-Burma, Sundaland) — do not confuse the two classifications.

Champion and Seth's classification of Indian forests recognises sixteen major types, of which the examinable groups are:

  • Tropical evergreen and semi-evergreen — over 200 cm rainfall; Western Ghats, north-east, Andamans. Multi-storeyed, no definite shedding season, extremely high species richness. Species: rosewood, mahogany, ebony, aini.
  • Tropical deciduous (monsoon forests) — the most widespread in India, shedding leaves in the dry season. Moist deciduous (100–200 cm) yields teak, sal, sandalwood, shisham; dry deciduous (70–100 cm) yields tendu, palas, axlewood.
  • Tropical thorn and scrub — under 70 cm; acacia, babool, khair, kikar, euphorbia, with long roots and thick cuticles.
  • Montane forests — altitudinal zonation from wet temperate (Nilgiris, "sholas") through pine, deodar, oak, silver fir to alpine meadows and tundra.
  • Littoral and swamp / mangrove — tidal forests dominated by Rhizophora and Avicennia; the Sundari tree gives the Sundarbans its name.

The India State of Forest Report, published biennially by the Forest Survey of India, is the authoritative source. Key definitions: forest cover means all land over one hectare with canopy density above 10% regardless of ownership or legal status — which is why plantations and orchards count, a much-criticised feature; tree cover means patches under one hectare. Density classes: very dense (canopy ≥70%), moderately dense (40–70%) and open (10–40%); below 10% is scrub. Forest and tree cover together stand at roughly a quarter of the geographical area, against the National Forest Policy 1988 target of 33% (and 66% for hills). By area Madhya Pradesh has the largest forest cover; by percentage of geographical area the north-eastern states and Mizoram lead — a classic trap pairing.

7. Endemism, Flagship Species and Distribution

The Western Ghats and the north-east concentrate India's endemism. Western Ghats endemics: Lion-tailed Macaque, Nilgiri Tahr, Nilgiri Langur, Malabar Civet, Malabar Grey Hornbill, and the purple frog. The Hangul (Kashmir Stag) survives only in Dachigam; the Asiatic Lion only in the Gir landscape; the Great Indian Bustard chiefly in the Thar of Rajasthan and Kutch; the one-horned rhinoceros in Kaziranga, Manas, Orang and Pobitora in Assam and Jaldapara in Bengal; the Asiatic Wild Ass (Ghudkhur) in the Little Rann of Kutch; the Snow Leopard, Ibex and Bharal in the Trans-Himalaya; the Hoolock Gibbon, India's only ape, in the north-east; and the Ganges River Dolphin, India's National Aquatic Animal, in the Ganga-Brahmaputra-Meghna and Karnaphuli systems.

National symbols worth fixing: national animal Bengal Tiger, national bird Indian Peafowl, national aquatic animal Ganges River Dolphin, national heritage animal Indian Elephant, national tree Banyan, national flower Lotus, national fruit Mango.

Also learn the agrobiodiversity dimension, which mains questions favour: India is a Vavilov centre of origin for rice, sugarcane, several pulses and many spices, with thousands of traditional landraces now displaced by a handful of high-yielding varieties. Genetic erosion — the narrowing of the crop genetic base — creates vulnerability to pests and climate shocks, and is why the National Bureau of Plant Genetic Resources gene bank, the Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights Act 2001 (which uniquely recognises farmers as breeders and conservers), and the recognition of Agrobiodiversity Heritage Sites matter.

8. Conservation Designations and International Recognition

Beyond the statutory protected areas, several international designations recur in the paper and must be distinguished by their parent convention:

  • Ramsar sites — wetlands of international importance under the Ramsar Convention (1971, Iran), which is not a UN convention. Criteria include representativeness, supporting vulnerable species, and regularly hosting 20,000 waterbirds or 1% of a flyway population. India has a large and growing number of sites, including Chilika, Keoladeo, Wular, Loktak, Sambhar, Kolleru, the Sundarbans and the East Kolkata Wetlands (notable for waste-water aquaculture). The Montreux Record lists sites facing ecological change — Keoladeo and Loktak are India's entries, Chilika having been removed after restoration.
  • Biosphere Reserves — UNESCO's Man and the Biosphere programme, zoned into core, buffer and transition. Nilgiri was India's first (1986); others on the World Network include Gulf of Mannar, Sundarbans, Nanda Devi, Nokrek, Pachmarhi, Similipal, Achanakmar-Amarkantak, Great Nicobar, Agasthyamala, Khangchendzonga and Panna.
  • UNESCO World Heritage Sites (natural) — Kaziranga, Manas, Keoladeo, Sundarbans, Nanda Devi and Valley of Flowers, Western Ghats, Great Himalayan National Park, and Khangchendzonga (a mixed site).
  • Important Bird and Biodiversity Areas identified by BirdLife International, and Marine Protected Areas.

Wetlands deserve separate attention. Their services — groundwater recharge, flood buffering, sediment and nutrient retention, carbon sequestration and fisheries — make them among the most valuable ecosystems per hectare, yet they are legally the weakest, governed by the Wetlands (Conservation and Management) Rules, 2017, which decentralised authority to State Wetland Authorities and were criticised for removing the central regulatory body and omitting a national inventory-based prohibition list. The Amrit Dharohar initiative promotes Ramsar site conservation.

9. Threats, Legal Framework and Debates

Beyond the HIPPO drivers, several India-specific pressures should be named precisely: linear infrastructure (roads, railways, canals and transmission lines) fragmenting habitat and killing wildlife, which is why wildlife corridors, underpasses and overpasses and the eco-bridge concept feature in current affairs; human-wildlife conflict, especially with elephants and leopards, driving retaliatory killing; invasive alien species such as Lantana camara, Prosopis juliflora, water hyacinth and the African catfish; forest fires; and illegal wildlife trade in pangolin scales, tiger and leopard parts, star tortoises and red sanders, addressed by the Wildlife Crime Control Bureau and TRAFFIC.

The legal architecture: the Biological Diversity Act, 2002 with its three-tier NBA–SBB–BMC structure and People's Biodiversity Registers documenting local knowledge; the Wildlife Protection Act schedules; the Forest Conservation Act; and, internationally, the CBD with the Nagoya Protocol on access and benefit sharing and the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework, whose headline "30×30" target seeks 30% of land and sea under effective conservation by 2030. Biopiracy cases — neem, turmeric, basmati — prompted the Traditional Knowledge Digital Library.

A balanced answer should also register the conservation-versus-livelihood debate: fortress conservation displaces forest-dependent communities, whereas the Forest Rights Act and community conservation recognise them as stewards. Cite sacred groves (kavu in Kerala, devrai in Maharashtra, orans in Rajasthan, law kyntang in Meghalaya) as India's oldest in-situ conservation institution, and community-led successes such as the Bishnoi tradition and Maharashtra's community forest resource management.

Trap watch. Forest cover counts any land over 1 ha with ≥10% canopy irrespective of legal status, so plantations are included. Madhya Pradesh leads by area, Mizoram by percentage. The 33% target comes from the National Forest Policy 1988, not the Constitution. Ramsar is not a UN convention and predates Stockholm. Keoladeo and Loktak are on the Montreux Record. Biosphere reserves are non-statutory; only their cores are legally protected. India has ten biogeographic zones but four biodiversity hotspots. The Hoolock gibbon is India's only ape.

Biodiversity basics

  • Biodiversity (Walter Rosen 1985; E.O. Wilson popularised) — genetic, species, ecosystem levels; alpha (within habitat), beta (between habitats), gamma (landscape). Species–area relationship (Alexander von Humboldt; log S = log C + Z log A; Z 0.1–0.2 for small areas, 0.6–1.2 for continents). Latitudinal gradient — tropics richer (Amazon ~40,000 plant species). Rivet popper hypothesis (Paul Ehrlich). Evil quartet of extinction (habitat loss/fragmentation, over-exploitation, alien invasion, co-extinction). Sixth mass extinction (Anthropocene); IUCN: >47,000 species threatened (2025; 28% assessed); Living Planet Index 2024: −73% since 1970 (WWF).
  • India: 2.4% of world land, ~7–8% of recorded species (~1,04,000 fauna; ~55,000+ flora); 8% of global biodiversity; one of 17 megadiverse countries (LMMC group — India, Brazil, China, Indonesia, Mexico, South Africa etc.); 4 of 36 global hotspots (Myers 1988; criteria: ≥1,500 endemic vascular plants + ≥70% original habitat lost): Himalaya, Indo-Burma (NE India east of Brahmaputra + Andamans), Western Ghats–Sri Lanka, Sundaland (Nicobar Islands). Endemism: ~33% of flowering plants endemic; Western Ghats ~1,500 endemic plants, 60% of amphibians; Indian endemic fauna — lion-tailed macaque, Nilgiri tahr (TN state animal; Project Nilgiri Tahr 2023), Malabar civet, Nilgiri langur, Nicobar megapode, purple frog (Nasikabatrachus), Andaman teal, Kashmir stag/hangul, sangai (Manipur), pygmy hog (Assam; Manas — smallest wild pig), golden langur, Namdapha flying squirrel, Jerdon's courser (AP), forest owlet (Maharashtra/MP), Great Indian Bustard (~150; Rajasthan/Gujarat; SC 2024 revisited power-line order — expert committee), lesser florican, Bengal florican. Traps

  • India has 4 hotspots (not 3) — Nicobar Islands fall in Sundaland; Andamans in Indo-Burma; hotspot criteria = 1,500 endemic plants + 70% habitat loss; 36 hotspots globally (Myers 1988; North American Coastal Plain 36th 2016); 17 megadiverse countries.
  • National Park: no rights; boundaries altered only by state legislature resolution; Sanctuary: Chief Wildlife Warden may permit grazing; both notified by state govt (Centre can under s. 38); Conservation Reserve = government land; Community Reserve = private/community land; Tiger Reserve is notified under WPA (s. 38V) on NTCA recommendation; core inviolate; Biosphere Reserve is a UNESCO/MAB concept, not a legal category under WPA; Ramsar has no legal protection under WPA either — Wetlands (Conservation & Management) Rules 2017.
  • Largest NP Hemis; largest tiger reserve Nagarjunsagar-Srisailam; largest BR Gulf of Kachchh; first BR Nilgiri; first NP Corbett (1936); most tigers MP; most elephants Karnataka; most rhinos Kaziranga (highest density Pobitora); most Ramsar sites Tamil Nadu (20); most community reserves Meghalaya; highest forest % Lakshadweep/Mizoram; largest forest area MP; mangroves WB 42%.
  • ISFR 2023: forest cover 21.76%, forest + tree 25.17%; policy target 33% (NFP 1988) — India is far below; 'Forest cover' = ≥1 ha with canopy density >10% irrespective of ownership/legal status (includes orchards, plantations); VDC (very dense) >70%, MDF 40–70%, OF 10–40%.
  • Biological Diversity Act 2002 — NBA (Chennai), SBBs, BMCs; 2023 amendment exempted codified traditional knowledge & AYUSH practitioners from benefit sharing; decriminalised offences; PBRs prepared by BMCs (panchayat level); Nagoya Protocol (ABS) 2010, in force 2014; India party.
  • WPA 2022 amendment: four schedules (was six); CITES implementation; invasive alien species control; Schedule I & II fauna (no more Schedule V vermin list — s. 62 declaration remains); Van Adhiniyam 2023 renamed FCA; SC (2024) kept Godavarman dictionary definition alive.
  • Cheetah reintroduction — Kuno (MP) 2022 (Namibia) and 2023 (South Africa); Gandhi Sagar 2025; extinct in India 1952 (Koriya, Chhattisgarh); Gangetic dolphin national aquatic animal (2009); first population estimate 2025: 6,327; Snow leopard SPAI 2024: 718 (Ladakh most); Lion census 2025: 891; Tiger 2022: 3,682; Elephant 2025 DNA estimate: 22,446; GIB ~150 wild.
