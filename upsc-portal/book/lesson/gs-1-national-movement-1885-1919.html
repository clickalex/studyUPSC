1. Background — Why Congress Emerged (1885)

By the 1880s three converging trends made a national political body inevitable:

FactorExplanation
Modern intelligentsiaEnglish-educated middle class (moderate lawyers, journalists, doctors, teachers) — fluent in a common political vocabulary of liberty, rights and representation.
Economic critiqueDadabhai Naoroji's drain theory (Poverty and Un-British Rule in India, 1901); R. C. Dutt's Economic History of India — provided an economic case against colonialism.
Political exclusionIlbert Bill agitation (1883–84) — Europeans' privileged position in criminal trials exposed racial discrimination; educated Indians realised charity would not suffice.
Regional associationsPoona Sarvajanik Sabha (1870) — Ranade, Joshi, Telang; Indian Association (1876) — Surendranath Banerjee, Ananda Mohan Bose; Madras Mahajan Sabha (1884) — Viraraghavachari, Subrahmanya Aiyar; Bombay Presidency Association (1885) — Mehta, Wacha, Tyabji.

A. O. Hume, a retired ICS officer, saw an urgent need: a body to channel Indian grievances into constitutional pathways before discontent turned violent. He convened 72 delegates to the first session of the Indian National Congress (INC) at Bombay (Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College), 28 December 1885, with W. C. Bonnerjee presiding.

2. The Moderate Era (1885–1905)

Character

  • Methods: constitutional — prayers, petitions, speeches, and resolutions; no mass mobilisation; looked to Britain's liberal conscience.
  • Leader generation: Dadabhai Naoroji (the "Grand Old Man of India"), Pherozeshah Mehta (the "Father of Indian Nationalism" in a parliamentary mould), Gopal Krishna Gokhale, Surendranath Banerjee, M. G. Ranade.
  • Demands (in order of priority): expansion of legislative councils (Indian representation), civil-service examination in India (Naoroji's Drain + Morely Reforms later), judicial and military expenditure audited, extension of trial by jury, reduction of land revenue.

Achievements

  1. Created a national body — annual sessions rotated across Presidencies; common all-India political language.
  2. Parliamentary influence — Raikes Act (1892 based on Indian Councils Act 1892), an entrance for the few into legislatures; critique forced the government to promise the Indian Councils Act 1909.
  3. Economic nationalism — Naoroji's drain theory and R. C. Dutt's economic critique proved that British rule was systematically impoverishing India; even loyal reformers became its critics.
  4. Civil services — forced successive reductions of the ICS entrance age in England (still a bias, but a point on record).

Limitations

  • Elitist — delegates mostly upper-caste men, urban and English-educated; peasants and women not mobilised.
  • Methods were slow — three-year cycles of prayer and petition produced slow and grudging British concessions.
  • Believed in Britain's "providential mission" — still faithful to the Empire; moderate language was often apologetic.
  • Congress was officially neutral on "divisive" questions (separate electorates, famine relief) — left these to separate bodies.

Critical moderate line: Gokhale — "no nation without its people" — tried to make Congress a training ground for self-government.

3. The Extremist / Assertive Nationalist Era (1905–1919)

Why the shift after 1905?

  • Partition of Bengal (1905) — Lord Curzon's administrative division (effective 16 October 1905) was clearly intended to split the politically vocal Bengali Hindu and Bengali Muslim populations.
  • Curzon's broader authoritarianism — Official Secrets Act (1904), police repression, famine administration, and the Indian Universities Act (1904) tightening government control over universities.
  • The Russian Revolution of 1905 and Japan's victory over Russia (1905) — inspired Indians to believe an Asian people could defeat a European empire.
  • Swadeshi ideas — already maturing in the Arya Samaj, Bankim Chandra's Anandamath ("Vande Mataram") and Ramakrishna–Vivekananda neo-Vedanta.

The Partition & Swadeshi Movement (1905–08)

  • Boycott (Calcutta resolution 7 August 1905) — the first mass boycott of British goods; British cloth, salt, sugar imports boycotted; public bonfires of cloth.
  • Swadeshi — indigenous industry (Bengal Chemicals, Bengal Potteries); national education (National Council of Education, Bengal, 1906 — forerunner of Jadavpur University).
  • Methods new to Congress: passive resistance, non-payment of taxes (partial), picketing liquor shops, singing of "Vande Mataram" at meetings (Bankim Chandra's hymn from Anandamath, 1882; adopted at the 1905 session).
  • Partition annulled 1911 — Rahmat after mass protest; the capital shifted from Calcutta to Delhi.

The Surat Split (December 1907)

  • Venue: Surat Gujarati Sabha; presided over by Rash Behari Ghose.
  • Issue of contention: the extremist demand that Congress officially confirm the resolutions of the Calcutta session (1906) on Swadeshi, boycott, national education and swaraj; the moderates wanted to dilute them after the partition crisis waned.
  • Tilak was nominated and then blocked; shoes and chairs were thrown; the moderates under Bipin Chandra Pal left the hall, and Congress formally split — the moderate faction stayed with the official INC.
  • Consequence: the Extremists were expelled; Congress weakened during the crucial years 1908–14.

4. The Muslim League & the Khilafat Context (1906–1919)

  • Muslim League founded (30 December 1906, Dacca) — Aga Khan, Nawab Salimullah of Dacca, Mohsin-ul-Mulk; supported by loyalists after Curzon encouraged separate Muslim representation.
  • Lucknow Pact (1916) — Tilak and Muhammad Ali Jinnah (then Congress leader) agreed on a joint programme; Congress accepted separate electorates for Muslims — a tactical compromise with serious consequences.
  • 1919 — the Rowlatt Act agitation, the Jallianwala Bagh massacre (13 April 1919), and the Khilafat question (Turkish Sultan's fate after WWI) set the stage for a new phase. Gandhi — absent from Indian politics since 1915 — now took leadership.

5. Foreign Policy & International Environment

  • Russian Revolution 1905 — inspired the Indian extremist generation; many extremists (Aurobindo, Savarkar at London's India House) admired Russian methods of resistance.
  • Japan 1905 — Asian victory over European power shattered the myth of European invincibility.
  • Germany and the Ghadar Movement (1913) — Punjabi diaspora in North America, led by Lala Har Dayal, Sohan Singh Bhakna, and Barkatullah — aimed to incite mutiny during WWI; the Komagata Maru incident (1914) dramatically exposed colonial racism.
  • Home Rule Leagues (1916) — Tilak (Poona) and Annie Besant (Madras) — agitated for self-government within the Empire; Besant became first woman president of Congress (Calcutta, 1917).

7. Gandhian Dawn (1915–1919)

  • January 1915 — Gandhi returned from South Africa via Prabhashankar Pattani's ship, landing at Bombay (9 January).
  • Early Indian campaigns:
    • Champaran (1917) — indigo planters' tinkathia system in Bihar; Rajendra Prasad and J. B. Kripalani helped; first civil disobedience in India.
    • Ahmedabad Mill Strike (1918) — workers vs plague bonus cut; Gandhi's first fast (16 days).
    • Ahmedabad Kheda (1918) — solid tax-resistance campaign; Vallabhbhai Patel's debut.
  • Rowlatt Satyagraha (1919) — first nationwide civil disobedience; suppressed brutally in Punjab; culminated in Jallianwala Bagh (13 April 1919): General Dyer ordered fire on a New Year/Baisakhi crowd in an enclosed garden — official toll 379, Indian estimates over 1,000.
  • Hunter Commission (Oct 1919) — official inquiry whitewashed the massacre.
  • 1919 Government of India Act — introduced dyarchy in the provinces — the British answer to mass agitation: placate with limited provincial responsibility while tightening emergency powers.

Gandhi's 1915–19 interventions showed a new technique: non-violent mass satyagraha involving peasants and workers, not just urban elites — the bridge to the Non-Cooperation movement (1920).

10. The Moderate Achievement Debate: Mendicancy or Foundation?

Section 2 lists achievements and limitations; this section argues the verdict — because \"were the Moderates mendicants or nation-builders?\" is the period's master-question, and Mains grades the argument, never the slogan. The prosecution (contemporary Extremists + later impatience): the methods (prayer–petition–protest — the annual December ritual of resolutions Britain filed-and-forgot); the sociology (urban, upper-caste, English-educated men — peasants unmobilised, women undelegated, Muslims under-represented — the \"microscopic minority\" charge); the psychology (faith in Britain's \"providential mission\" — Naoroji's own early loyalism, the apologetic tone — mendicancy as method: begging the master for the master's reasons); the record (Councils Act 1892's crumbs — indirect election, official majority retained; ICS age-cut from 21 to 19 (1878–Ly decree reversed only in 1892 — the door narrowed, not opened); the simultaneous-examination demand unmet till the 1920s). The defence (Bipan Chandra's classic rehabilitation — India's Struggle: the Moderates as the necessary first stage — nation-in-the-making): the economic critique (Naoroji's drain — Poverty and Un-British Rule, 1901 — the moral indictment quantified: Home Charges, salary-remittances, the \"un-British\" frame turning liberalism against empire; R.C. Dutt's Economic History — the deindustrialisation documented — the critique that converted loyalists into nationalists, including the young Gandhi and Nehru); the political education (annual sessions rotating across presidencies — the all-India vocabulary forged: liberty, representation, swaraj-seeded — Gokhale's \"training ground for self-government\" — the Servants of India Society 1905 as the cadre-proof); the constitutional wedge (1892 Act's foothold → 1909's promise — Morley–Minto conceded because the critique embarrassed — the Extremists inherited the platform the Moderates built).

The synthesis (the examination-grade verdict): judge the Moderates by stage-necessity (no mass movement without prior ideological consensus — the drain theory had to be believed before Swadeshi could be felt — the Moderates manufactured the belief) and by counterfactual (without 1885–1905's press–platform–petition infrastructure — the Hindu, Kesari-to-come, the session-network — Tilak's 1905–08 explosion would have had no wires to travel). Concede the prosecution its sociology (elite, slow, loyalist-tinged — the Surat-1907 verdict of history's impatience) and the defence its function (foundation, not failure — the Congress they built survived the split, reunited 1916, and became Gandhi's vehicle). The verdict-line: the Moderates petitioned the Empire and educated the nation — failing at the first (by design-impossibility) and succeeding at the second (by historical necessity) — mendicants toward London, schoolmasters toward India.

11. Swadeshi Dissected: Boycott, National Education, Culture, and Why It Ebbed

Section 3 narrates Swadeshi (boycott 7 August 1905, indigenous industry, National Council of Education 1906, annulment 1911); this section dissects it — because \"assess Swadeshi\" demands anatomy plus autopsy. The four arms: boycott (British cloth–salt–sugar — public bonfires — the import-curves visibly dented 1905–08: Manchester's lobby complaining to Whitehall — economic pressure proven); swadeshi-enterprise (Bengal Chemicals — P.C. Ray's proof that science could be nationalist; Bengal Potteries, Mohini Mills, Swadeshi steam-navigation (Chidambaram Pillai's Tuticorin–Colombo line — the Tamil extension) — enterprise as resistance: capital recruited to the flag); national education (National Council of Education, 1906 — Jadavpur's forerunner — vernacular-medium, technical-plus-national curriculum — the boycott of Calcutta-University examinations, the student-strike as method: education seized from the coloniser's syllabus); culture-as-politics (Vande Mataram — Bankim's 1882 hymn, sung at Congress-1896, adopted-1905-session — the anthem before the anthem; Tagore's rakhi-and-arandhan (tying bonds across the partition-line, the non-cooking day of mourning — 16 October 1905 — grief choreographed); Abanindranath's Bharat Mata (1905 — the nation imaged: saffron ascetic-mother — the Bengal-School's political birth); Tilak's Ganapati (1893)–Shivaji (1895) festivals as the Maharashtra template — religion's calendar converted to nationalism's timetable).

The ebb — autopsy in four causes (§8's decline-question answered): State repression (1907–08 press laws, the Tilak-Mandaley transportation 1908 — six years for \"terrorism\" journalism — the leadership decapitated; Aurobindo's Alipore-trial-to-Pondicherry-1910 exit; the Criminal Law Amendment's meeting-bans — the open movement criminalised); the Surat split (December 1907 — Rash Behari Ghose's chair, the Calcutta-four (swadeshi–boycott–education–swaraj) as the casus, shoes-and-chairs, expulsion — the movement beheaded and divided at its peak); the social ceiling (students–middle-class–zamindars mobilised, peasants-and-Muslims thinly reached — the boycott's cloth-cost falling on the poor, the Hindu-idiom (Shivaji, Ganapati, Kali) alienating Muslim artisans — the Morley–Minto 1909 separate-electorates wedge driven into exactly this crack); the annulment's deflation (1911 — George V's Delhi-Durbar announcement — partition revoked, capital shifted to Delhi — the grievance removed while the method was exhausted — victory that dissolved the movement: Swadeshi won its demand and lost its momentum). The dissection's verdict: Swadeshi invented the modern repertoire (boycott, national-education, cultural-politics — Gandhi's 1920 kit prefigured) and discovered its limits (repression-plus-division-plus-ceiling — the lessons Non-Cooperation would study) — the rehearsal whose script the main performance followed.

12. Surat, Lucknow, Home Rule: The Politics of Split and Reunion

Sections 3–5 stage the split (1907), the pact (1916) and the Leagues (1916); this section theorises the arc — because 1907–1919 is the Congress's education in unity, and the examination tests the lesson. Surat dissected (December 1907 — Gujarati-Sabha venue, Rash Behari Ghose presiding): the surface issue (confirm-or-dilute the Calcutta-1906 four — swadeshi, boycott, national education, swaraj — Naoroji's 1906 \"Swaraj\" declaration as the radical high-water mark the Moderates feared to reaffirm); the structural issue (who is Congress — the constitutional lobby (Mehta–Gokhale: resolutions as petitions-to-London) versus the national movement (Tilak–Aurobindo–Pal: resolutions as pledges-to-the-people) — two theories of the organisation in one hall); the trigger (Tilak's nomination blocked, the procedural melee — shoes, chairs, the Bipin-Chandra-Pal-led Moderate walkout — expulsion voted); the consequence (1908–14 — the wilderness years: Moderates alone (Loyalist cooperation — Morley–Minto 1909 welcomed — councils entered, momentum lost), Extremists jailed-or-exiled (Tilak-Mandalay, Aurobindo-Pondicherry, Lajpat-Rai-deported-then-America, Pal-England) — the movement's energy diverted underground: Anushilan–Jugantar–India-House–Ghadar — the revolutionary current as the split's bastard child).

Lucknow reunited (1916 — A.C. Majumdar presiding): the reunion mechanics (Tilak-released-1914 — Mandalay-mellowed, war-loyalty pledged — readmitted; Besant's mediation — the Home-Rule agitation as the common platform; the war-context — Britain needing Indian men-and-money — the loyalist bargain available); the Lucknow Pact (Tilak–Jinnah (then \"ambassador of Hindu–Muslim unity\" — Gokhale's phrase, Sarojini's biography-title) — Congress accepts separate electorates for Muslims plus weightages — the tactical compromise: unity-now (joint demands: elected-majority councils, half-executives, the reform-memorandum) at the price of principle-later (the League's separatism legitimised by Congress's own signature — Jinnah's 1940 Pakistan prefigured in 1916's concession — the PYQ's \"three-cornered compromise\" (Congress–League–British-reforms) unpacked: each corner gaining (Congress-unity, League-recognition, British-time) and the nation mortgaging (the communal principle constitutionalised by nationalist hands)). Home Rule bridged (1916 — Tilak's Poona League (April — Marathi-Deccan, \"Swaraj is my birthright\" as the war-cry) plus Besant's Madras League (September — all-India, English-press — New India, Commonweal — the Theosophist's organisation: 200+ branches — the first woman to preside over Congress, Calcutta-1917 — the League agitation as mass-meetings-plus-pamphlets: self-government-within-Empire demanded during the Empire's war — the loyalist-radical paradox that made it unbanishable and irresistible — Montagu's August-1917 declaration (\"increasing association… progressive realisation of responsible government\") as its direct yield). The arc's thesis: Surat taught the cost of division (wilderness-plus-underground), Lucknow the price of unity (separate-electorates mortgaged), Home Rule the method of pressure (constitutional-agitation at wartime) — the Congress graduating, term by term, toward the Gandhian university.

13. The Ghadar–Revolutionary Current and the Gandhian Takeover Technique

Section 5's international environment (Ghadar 1913, Komagata Maru 1914) plus §7's Gandhian dawn (Champaran–Ahmedabad–Kheda, Rowlatt, Jallianwala, 1919 Act) frame the period's two roads — this section walks both to their junction. The revolutionary current: Anushilan–Jugantar (Bengal — Barindra–Aurobindo's Bhavani Mandir manual, the Muzaffarpur-1908 bombing (Khudiram–Prafulla — the first gallows-martyrs), Alipore-trial-1908–09 (Chittaranjan Das's defence — Aurobindo acquitted, the organisation shattered)); India House (London — Savarkar's War of Independence-1909-banned, Madanlal-Dhingra's Curzon-Wyllie-1909 assassination — the Empire struck in its capital); Ghadar (1913 — San Francisco Hindustan Ghadar — Har Dayal, Sohan Singh Bhakna, Barkatullah — Punjabi-diaspora mutiny-plan for wartime: 1914–15 returnees intercepted (Komagata-Maru-September-1914 Budge-Budge firing as the prelude — Gurdit Singh's charter exposing dominion-racism), the Lahore-conspiracy trials 1915–16 (49 transported-or-hanged — the mutiny strangled by intelligence: the Central-Intelligence-Department's penetration); the Singapore-mutiny-1915 (Malay-States-Guides — suppressed, 47 shot) — the current's verdict: heroism without organisation (individual-martyrdom maximised, mass-linkage absent — the peasant unreached, the soldier unconverted — courage that inspired (Bhagat Singh's canon) but couldn't liberate).

The Gandhian takeover technique (§7's three experiments as method-building): Champaran-1917 (tinkathia-indigo — Rajendra-Prasad–Kripalani enlisted — the inquiry-plus-disobedience template: investigate openly, defy the externment-order, suffer-and-negotiate — first civil disobedience, the planter-raj dented); Ahmedabad-mill-1918 (plague-bonus — the workers' cause — Gandhi's first fast — 3 days, the moral-wager: starve-till-arbitration — the fast-as-weapon invented); Kheda-1918 (crop-failure-revenue — Vallabhbhai's debut — the no-tax peasant campaign — the Sardar found); then Rowlatt-1919 (the Anarchical-and-Revolutionary-Crimes-Act — detention-without-trial — the wartime-Defence-of-India-Act made permanent — Gandhi's first all-India satyagraha call: hartal-plus-disobedience — the technique scaled: peasants-and-workers (Champaran–Kheda-learnt) plus urban-masses (the Rowlatt-hartal crowds) — the bridge §7 names to Non-Cooperation-1920). Jallianwala (13-April-1919 — Baisakhi crowd, Dyer's 1,650 rounds, 379-official/1,000+-Indian dead — the Hunter-Committee-October-1919 whitewash, Dyer-censured-not-punished, the Morning-Post-fund glorifying him — the Empire's moral bankruptcy exhibited) plus the 1919 Act's dyarchy (transferred-versus-reserved, the placate-and-tighten: provincial-responsibility dangled, emergency-powers kept) complete the junction: the revolutionaries proved Indians would die for freedom, Gandhi proved they could organise for it — martyrdom's inspiration fused to satyagraha's method — the 1920s inheriting both the courage and the technique.

Revision Digest

Condensed for the last mile before the exam.

One-liner

From the moderate petitions of 1885 to the boycott of 1905 and the reunion at Lucknow (1916), Indian nationalism evolved from prayer to mass action — and Gandhi's three campaigns (1917–18) readied the country for the 1920 mass movement.

Three phases (memorise the years)

  1. Moderates (1885–1905) — petitions, prayer, English liberalism. Leaders: Naoroji, Mehta, Gokhale, Banerjee, Ranade.
  2. Extremists / Assertive (1905–1919) — partition of Bengal (1905) → Swadeshi, boycott, national education; leaders: Tilak, Pal, Lajpat Rai, Aurobindo.
  3. Gandhian dawn (1915–19) — return from South Africa (Jan 1915); Champaran (1917), Ahmedabad (1918), Kheda (1918); Rowlatt Satyagraha (1919).

Most-asked answers (one-sentence versions)

  • Why Surat split? Over the moderate demand to water down the Calcutta resolutions; the extremists wanted affirmation.
  • Lucknow Pact significance? Moderate–Extremist reunion + Congress–League tactical cooperation.
  • Gandhi's "three" movements? Champaran (indigo peasants, 1917), Ahmedabad (mill workers' plague bonus, 1918), Kheda (tax remission, 1918).

  • First Bengali president — Dadabhai Naoroji (Calcutta 1886).
  • First Muslim president — Rahimtullah M. Sayani (Calcutta 1896); Vande Mataram sung for the first time.
  • Moderate achievements — Indian Councils Act 1892; economic critique (drain theory); proving that annual sessions create a national public.
  • Partition of Bengal — 16 October 1905 by Lord Curzon.
  • First mass boycott — 7 August 1905 Calcutta resolution; British cloth, salt, sugar boycotted.
  • National Council of Education, Bengal (1906) — forerunner of Jadavpur University.
  • The Calcutta session of 1906 — Dadabhai Naoroji presiding; declared the four-point programme: Swaraj, Swadeshi, Boycott, National Education.
  • Surat split — December 1907 — Rash Behari Ghose presiding; moderates walked out after Tilak was blocked.
  • Partition annulled 1911; capital shifted Calcutta → Delhi.
  • Ghadar Party (1913) — San Francisco; Lala Har Dayal; Komagata Maru (1914) incident — Japanese ship carrying Punjabis turned back from Vancouver; British India put them in prison on landing.
  • Home Rule Leagues (1916) — Tilak (Poona, April) and Annie Besant (Madras, September).
  • Lucknow Pact 1916 — Tilak + Jinnah; Moderate-Extremist reunion; Congress accepted separate electorates.
  • Besant, first woman president — Calcutta, 1917.
  • Champaran (1917) — tinkathia system; first civil disobedience in India; Rajendra Prasad and J. B. Kripalani present.
  • Ahmedabad (1918) — mill strike for plague bonus; Gandhi's first hunger fast (16 days).
  • Kheda (1918) — tax remission; Vallabhbhai Patel's debut.
  • Rowlatt Satyagraha (1919) — first nationwide protest; martial law in Punjab.
  • Jallianwala Bagh — 13 April 1919 — General Reginald Dyer; official toll 379; Indian estimates over 1,000; Hunter Commission exposed the cover-up.

UPSC Mains PYQs

Q2015

"The Surat Split of 1907 marks a turning point in the Indian National Movement." Discuss. (12.5 marks)

Model answer outline

Model answer skeleton

Introduction — The split — at the Surat session, December 1907, Rash Behari Ghose presiding — ended a decade of uneasy unity between the Moderate and Extremist wings.

Body

  1. Immediate context — the Calcutta session (1906, Naoroji presiding) had endorsed the four-point programme (swaraj, swadeshi, boycott, national education); the moderates wanted to dilute the resolutions after the partition crisis eased.
  2. The confrontation — Tilak was nominated president, then blocked; shoes and chairs thrown; the moderates left the hall; the Extremists — including Tilak, Pal, Lajpat Rai, Aurobindo — were expelled.
  3. Turning point in leadership — weakened Congress immediately before the revolutionary upsurge (1907–14); Bengal's secret societies (Anushilan Samiti, Jugantar) gained space.
  4. Turning point in ideology — moderates still dominated the official Congress, but Extremist methods — boycott and mass agitation — entered the national vocabulary permanently.
  5. Reunion and superseding — Lucknow session (1916) reunited the wings, but the Gandhian leadership from 1919 onward drew on both wings — mass action plus constitutional negotiation.

Conclusion — The Surat split turned out to be less a defeat than a sorting out; it destroyed the old moderate programme of prayer and petition permanently and matured the two essential ingredients (mass agitation and political negotiation) of the later Gandhian synthesis.

Q2018

Examine the role of Mahatma Gandhi in the Champaran Satyagraha (1917) as his first mass mobilisation in India. (10 marks)

Model answer outline

Model answer skeleton

Introduction — Champaran (Bihar) was Gandhi's first civil disobedience in India; indigo planters' tinkathia system (cultivation of indigo in 3/20 of holding, rent paid in kind).

Body

  1. Cause — European planters forced peasants to grow indigo at distress prices; the collapse of synthetic indigo market (German invention) made matters worse.
  2. Gandhi's method — arrived April 1917; defied the Magistrate's Section 144 order to leave; toured villages; collected 8,000+ statements; set up schools and medical camps; worked with Rajendra Prasad, J. B. Kripalani, Mazharul Haq.
  3. Government response — appointed the Champaran Agrarian Enquiry Committee (Gandhi a member); the report accepted peasants' grievances; tinkathia abolished by the Champaran Agrarian Act (1918).
  4. Significance — first time a mass of Indian peasants saw a leader confront official coercion non-violently; the model of investigation + mobilisation + negotiation.

Conclusion — Champaran showed that Gandhi's South-African satyagraha technique could be adapted to India and to the peasantry — the paradigm for his later national campaigns.
