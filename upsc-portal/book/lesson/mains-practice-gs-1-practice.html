Detailed Study

The full treatment of this lesson.

How to convert the GS-1 question bank (see pyqs/) into marks: syllabus mapping, time protocol, rubric, weekly cycle.

1. What GS-1 Demands

GS-1 rewards substantive memory organised analytically: culture (art, architecture, personalities), modern history (1857–1947), post-independence consolidation, world history (revolutions, redrawing boundaries), society (urbanisation, globalisation, communalism, women), geography (physical, Indian, world) and geophysical phenomena (earthquakes, cyclones, tsunamis). The examiner's mix is roughly: recall-heavy (culture/history) + analytical (society) + applied-conceptual (geography).

2. The Time Protocol

  • 10 markers (150 words): 7 minutes — 30 sec decode + 30 sec micro-outline + 5:30 write + 30 sec underline.
  • 15 markers (250 words): 11 minutes — with a diagram/map slot wherever a spatial or temporal spread exists.
  • Full mock (20 questions, 3 hours): 20-question arithmetic means no answer exceeds budget — practise abandoning an answer at the bell.

3. Answer Anatomy for GS-1

  • Introduction (2-3 lines): define the core term or set the period/context — dates and dynasties earn their keep here.
  • Body: 2-4 labelled dimensions with examples as evidence; history answers move chronologically, society answers move thematically, geography answers move spatially.
  • Diagram/map: at least in every geography answer and wherever history offers a timeline — a clean 30-second sketch differentiates.
  • Conclusion (2 lines): significance or legacy — never end mid-list.

4. Self-evaluation Rubric (score /10 per answer)

CriterionMarks
Question decoded — directive verb honoured (discuss ≠ describe)2
Substance — facts, dates, schools of thought, examples accurate3
Structure — labelled dimensions, logical flow, visual used3
Closure — significance stated within word limit2

5. The Weekly Cycle

  • Mon: culture — 2 answers + timeline revision (dance, architecture schools).
  • Tue: modern history — 2 answers + one 1757–1947 segment re-read.
  • Wed: society — 2 answers with current data (NFHS, census-proxy, NCRB).
  • Thu: geography — 2 answers, each with a map/annotated sketch.
  • Fri: world history/post-independence — 2 answers.
  • Sat: half mock (10 Q, 90 min) + same-day evaluation.
  • Sun: error-log review; rewrite the two worst answers.

6. The Error Log

Four columns: question, what I wrote, what a 8+/10 wrote, the fix (fact gap / structure slip / time breach). Review weekly; the log becomes your personal question bank by December.

7. Pointers

  • Culture answers: name texts/texts' authors, dynasties, regions — vagueness is death.
  • Society answers: pair a concept (modernisation, patriarchy) with one datum and one example.
  • Geography answers: sketch first (10 sec), write around it.

8. The Directive Verb in a Memory Paper

GS-1 directives look identical to those in GS-2 and GS-3 — discuss, examine, critically analyse, evaluate — but they behave differently because the paper tests a different faculty. In GS-2 the directive asks for judgment about institutions, and in GS-3 it asks for prescriptions about problems; in GS-1 it asks how memory is to be organised. A candidate who has memorised the facts but organised them wrongly for the verb will score less than a candidate who knows half as much but answers the verb exactly. The reason is mechanical: the examiner's model answer for a GS-1 question is a checklist of expected points, and the verb decides which checklist is applied. Answering \"describe\" when the question asked \"discuss\" means matching the wrong checklist even when every fact you write is true.

The verbs cluster into three families in this paper. The descriptive family — describe, elucidate, explain, illustrate — wants ordered recall with a frame. \"Describe the salient features of the Nagara style\" does not want a judgment about whether Nagara is superior to Dravida; it wants the five canonical elements (shikhara, garbhagriha, mandapa, amalaka, antarala) named, located in two or three dated examples, and distinguished from what the style is not. The failure mode is editorialising: candidates who \"critically examine\" a descriptive question burn a third of their word limit on objections that appear nowhere in the marking scheme. The relational family — discuss, examine, analyse, account for — wants facts arranged into an argument about causes, comparisons or consequences. \"Discuss the causes of the decline of the Mughal Empire\" is not an invitation to list Aurangzeb's mistakes; it is an invitation to weigh structural explanations (the jagir crisis, the Deccan ulcer, the Maratha-Sikh-Jat resistance, the agrarian crisis thesis associated with Irfan Habib, the \"great firm\" thesis) against personality explanations, and to take a position on which weight is heavier. The evaluative family — critically examine, critically analyse, evaluate, assess, comment, \"to what extent\" — wants a verdict, and in GS-1 the verdict is usually historiographical or interpretive rather than prescriptive. \"To what extent was the Revolt of 1857 the first war of independence\" wants the nationalist, colonial and subaltern positions laid out and then judged; an answer that lists causes without naming a single interpretation has answered a different question.

Two refinements separate trained candidates from the rest. The first is that GS-1 questions are frequently two-clause: a subject clause and a scoped clause — \"…with special reference to the role of women\", \"…Also suggest what lessons it holds for the present\". The word limit is a budget for both clauses, and the marks split roughly in proportion to clause length. A 15-mark question asking you to discuss the Non-Cooperation Movement and assess its significance for mass politics is in effect a 9-mark narrative and a 6-mark assessment; candidates who spend 220 words on the narrative forfeit the second half. The second refinement is that the same verb means different things in different GS-1 segments. A \"critical examination\" of a historical process is a weighing of interpretations (which school of historians is right); of a social phenomenon it is a weighing of evidence against a concept (does the data support the modernisation thesis); and of a geographical process it is a weighing of competing causal factors (tectonic versus climatic drivers of a landform). Learn the verb once and the segment three times.

9. Six Question Families and the Skeletons That Score

Nearly every GS-1 question since the syllabus stabilised in 2013 falls into one of six families, and each family has a skeleton stable enough to be learned cold. The skeleton is what lets you begin writing within ninety seconds: you are not inventing structure under time pressure, you are filling slots.

The first family is culture and art forms: architecture schools, painting, dance, music, theatre, literature, UNESCO inscriptions. The skeleton is definition → period and patronage → canonical features in threes or fives → two dated examples with locations → what distinguishes it from its neighbour. The last slot is the scoring one, because culture questions are almost always implicitly comparative: Nagara wants its Dravida contrast, Kathak its Kathakali contrast, Hindustani its Carnatic contrast. Dates and places are the currency — \"the Lingaraja temple at Bhubaneswar, c. 11th century, mature Kalinga Nagara\" earns what \"many temples in Odisha\" cannot. The second family is modern history and the freedom struggle, and its skeleton is context → sequence with turning points dated → actors and their motives → historiographical judgment. The recurring error is pure narrative: a chronicle of 1857 or of the Civil Disobedience Movement that never says whose interpretation it endorses. Name the debate — Cambridge school versus nationalist versus subaltern, or the \"safety-valve\" controversy over the Congress's founding — and take a side, even a qualified one.

The third family is post-independence consolidation and world history: linguistic reorganisation, the Emergency, land reforms, the Cold War, decolonisation, the redrawing of boundaries. The skeleton is initial condition → the decision or process → intended versus actual outcomes → legacy for the present. These questions reward the candidate who connects the past to the syllabus's present: the States Reorganisation Act of 1956 answered through the lens of the 2014 bifurcation of Andhra Pradesh, or Suez 1956 answered through the vocabulary of contemporary West Asian chokepoints. The fourth family is society — urbanisation, globalisation, communalism, regionalism, women, poverty, population — and its skeleton is concept → one honest datum → mechanism → distributional note → an evaluative close. This is the family where a single number does disproportionate work: NFHS-5 (2019–21) reporting a total fertility rate of 2.0 and, for the first time, 1,020 women per 1,000 men; Census 2011 as the last completed enumeration, with urbanisation at 31.2 per cent. The distributional note — who gains, who loses, which region or gender or caste bears the cost — is the step most often skipped and most heavily rewarded.

The fifth family is physical geography and geophysical phenomena: geomorphology, climatology, oceanography, earthquakes, cyclones, tsunamis, volcanism. The skeleton is process → mechanism in sequence → Indian instances with locations → human interface (hazard, mitigation). Geography answers are won in the first ten seconds, when the candidate decides on the visual: a cross-section for landforms, a labelled map for distributions, a sequence diagram for processes. A cyclone question without the Bay of Bengal–Arabian Sea asymmetry (roughly a 4:1 frequency ratio, with Odisha–Andhra–West Bengal as the landfall coast) is an answer that described the mechanism but missed the Indian geography, which is what the paper exists to test. The sixth family is Indian and world economic geography: resources, industries, agriculture, transport, settlements. The skeleton is distribution → reason for the distribution → change over time → a forward judgment. \"Reason for the distribution\" is the causal core — why the cotton textile industry concentrated in Mumbai and Ahmedabad (humidity, port, capital, labour), why IT clustered in Bengaluru and Hyderabad — and \"change over time\" is where contemporary knowledge enters: the mineral-auction regime, the freight-corridor redrawing, the renewable-energy map.

10. The Fact Bank as a Machine, and the Timeline Discipline

The authority bank that Sections 2 and 5 of the system describe only becomes usable in GS-1 when it is built for retrieval under time pressure rather than for completeness. Give every entry five fields and no more: the anchor (the date, site, school, case or datum); its type; the exact form (Kanishka's accession c. 78 CE, not \"around the first century\"; the Regulating Act 1773, not \"the first parliamentary control\"); the question family it serves; and a one-line deployment — the sentence in which you will actually use it. The one-line deployment is the discipline that separates learning from hoarding: a date you cannot place inside an argument is not yet usable, and the bank should be pruned by use, not grown by collection. Five hundred entries you can deploy beat two thousand you can only recognise.

Three sub-banks deserve separate notebooks because the syllabus names their contents. The timeline bank is a single continuous line from the Harappan mature phase (c. 2600–1900 BCE) through the Vedic corpus, the Mahajanapadas, the Mauryas (Ashoka c. 268–232 BCE, Kalinga c. 261 BCE), the Guptas (Chandragupta II c. 380–415 CE), the Sultanate (1206–1526), the Mughals (1526–1857 in name, effective to 1707), the Company (1757–1857), the Crown (1858–1947) and the Republic — with the freedom-struggle spine (1885, 1905, 1919, 1920, 1930, 1942, 1947) learnable to the month. The culture bank is organised as patron–school–signature triads: dynasty, style, and the one monument, text or artist that proves you know it — the Cholas with Rajaraja I and the Brihadeshwara at Thanjavur (c. 1010 CE), the Vijayanagara rayas with Hampi, Krishnadevaraya with the Amuktamalyada. The data bank is the smallest and the most neglected: a dozen numbers refreshed yearly — Census 2011 totals, NFHS-5 headline indicators, the sex ratio and literacy figures, forest-cover band from the ISFR, the urbanisation share — each with its source and year, because an unsourced number in a GS-1 society answer reads as invention.

The error log from Section 6 earns its keep when it is read quantitatively. After twenty logged answers a pattern always emerges, and in GS-1 it is almost always one of three: fact gaps (the answer was well-structured but thin — the fix is the bank, not more writing), verb slips (the answer described when it should have weighed — the fix is re-reading the directive before outlining), or visual omissions (geography answers without maps, history answers without timelines — the fix is a pre-committed \"sketch first\" rule). Most candidates discover they are not short of knowledge but of position: their recurring defect is the missing historiographical judgment or the missing distributional note. Rewriting the week's two weakest answers, which the Sunday protocol already prescribes, is the only practice activity with a measured effect on score; drafting new answers mostly rehearses existing habits. Use the previous decade's papers as a frequency map rather than a bank to be \"finished\": cluster ten years of GS-1 questions into the six families and count, then learn first the anchors that the frequency map, not the textbook, says are heavily taxed.

11. Marking Psychology and the Failure Modes That Cost Two Marks Each

An evaluator reads a few hundred scripts in a few weeks and spends on the order of six to eight minutes on each answer. That constraint has design consequences that are not cosmetic. Structure is a scoring variable: the examiner is checking whether the directive was honoured, whether dated and located evidence was deployed, and whether a position or significance was stated — all three of which should be visible in the first four lines. Short paragraphs with one idea each, underlined anchors, a labelled visual where the family demands one, adherence to the word limit within roughly ten per cent, and a two-line conclusion stating significance or legacy are the conditions under which those judgments can be made quickly. An answer that requires re-reading is scored from memory of its opening, and an answer that ends mid-list has announced that it ran out of plan.

The failure modes, in descending order of damage, are stable across cohorts. Vagueness in evidence is the first and the GS-1-specific killer: \"many temples were built\", \"several leaders participated\", \"in ancient times\" — each such phrase forfeits the mark that a named anchor would have earned, because in a memory paper precision is the competence being tested. Verb blindness is the second: the well-informed narrative that never weighs, never judges, never compares. Chronology drift is the third and the most punishing — placing the Khiljis before the Slave dynasty, dating the Non-Cooperation Movement to 1919, attributing the Vernacular Press Act to the wrong viceroy — because a misdated anchor signals that the candidate is guessing at the language of the paper, and one visible error discounts every other claim in the answer. Visual omission is fourth: geography without maps, history without timelines, culture without a single named site. Presentism is fifth — judging the past exclusively by present values without acknowledging the historiographical debate — and unallocated two-part answers, where the second clause starves, is sixth.

Calibration follows from the same logic. The gap between a 4 and a 6 out of 10 is not additional content; it is one correctly named and dated anchor per dimension and a stated position where the verb demands one. The gap between a 6 and an 8 is comparison — a second school, a second interpretation, a second region — and specificity of evidence: sites with locations, texts with authors, data with sources. Above 8 the answer additionally holds its own nuance: it states the limiting condition under which its judgment would change. Practise against that ladder, not against a word count, and let the error log tell you which rung you are actually standing on.

Trap watch. The Regulating Act is 1773 (Warren Hastings, Supreme Court at Calcutta) and Pitt's India Act is 1784 (dual control, Board of Control) — the two are routinely swapped. The Vernacular Press Act is 1878 (Lytton, repealed 1882 by Ripon); the Arms Act is the same viceroyalty but a different instrument. The Ilbert Bill controversy is 1883–84 (Ripon); the Congress is founded 1885. Non-Cooperation is 1920–22 (suspended after Chauri Chaura, February 1922); Civil Disobedience is 1930–34 (Dandi March, March–April 1930); Quit India is 1942. The Cabinet Mission is 1946, Mountbatten Plan June 1947, Independence August 1947. Kanishka c. 78 CE founds the Saka era; the Gupta era begins 319–20 CE; Harsha rules c. 606–647 CE. Ashoka's Kalinga war is c. 261 BCE and the Rock Edict XIII account of it — Major Rock Edicts are fourteen in number. The 42nd Amendment (1976) moved education and forests to the Concurrent List; the 73rd and 74th (1992) are Panchayats and Municipalities. NFHS-5 covers 2019–21 with a total fertility rate of 2.0 and 1,020 women per 1,000 men; the Census was last conducted in 2011, so \"Census 2021\" figures in any answer are projections, not enumerations. Nagara is the northern shikhara idiom, Dravida the southern vimana-gopuram idiom, Vesara the Deccan synthesis — Hampi is Vijayanagara-Dravida, not Vesara. Kathak is the North Indian narrative dance (Lucknow–Jaipur gharanas); Kathakali is the Kerala dance-drama with chutti makeup — the names differ by two letters and the traditions by a subcontinent. Hindustani music divides into gharanas (Gwalior, Kirana, Jaipur-Atrauli); Carnatic music organises around the Trinity (Tyagaraja, Muthuswami Dikshitar, Syama Sastri). Bay of Bengal cyclones outnumber Arabian Sea ones roughly four to one; the southwest monsoon reaches Kerala around 1 June and retreats from the northwest from September.

30 questions

30 original questions in the UPSC pattern (10-markers ≈ 150 words, 15-markers ≈ 250 words). A. Indian Heritage & Culture

Q110 marks

Discuss how the Chola temple architecture reflects the political and economic power of the Chola state.

  • Intro: Brihadeeswara (1010 CE) as 'imperial temple' — Rajaraja I's inscription listing endowments.
  • Body: vimana height as royal statement; temple as landlord, banker and employer (devadana grants, 400 dancers, 600 staff); bronze casting (Nataraja) linked to guild patronage; temple-town urbanisation (Thanjavur, Gangaikondacholapuram); inscriptions as archives of the state.
  • Conclusion: the temple as a 'state within the state' that outlived the dynasty — UNESCO 'Great Living Chola Temples'.
Q210 marks

'Bhakti and Sufi movements were not merely religious but also social revolutions.' Examine.

  • Body: vernacular languages (Kabir's dohas, Tukaram's abhangs, Baba Farid's Punjabi); rejection of caste (Ravidas, Chokhamela); women's voices (Mirabai, Akka Mahadevi, Lal Ded); Sufi khanqahs as inclusive spaces; langar and Sikhism's institutional egalitarianism; limits — did not dismantle caste structurally.
  • Conclusion: laid a composite culture that later reformers (Phule, Ambedkar) drew upon.
Q315 marks

Compare the Nagara and Dravida styles of temple architecture with examples, and explain the emergence of the Vesara style.

  • Body: table — shikhara vs vimana, absence vs presence of gopuram and enclosure walls, single vs multiple shrines, latina/phamsana/valabhi sub-types (Khajuraho, Konark, Modhera) vs Pallava–Chola–Vijayanagara–Nayaka evolution (Mamallapuram to Madurai); Vesara — Chalukyan experiments at Aihole/Pattadakal, Hoysala star-shaped plans (Belur, Halebidu, Somnathpur).
  • Conclusion: Deccan as a laboratory of synthesis; regional variants (Kalinga, Maru-Gurjara) show that the binary is a simplification.
Q410 marks

Evaluate the significance of the Maratha Military Landscapes of India inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2025.

  • Body: 12 forts across Maharashtra and Tamil Nadu (Raigad, Shivneri, Pratapgad, Gingee etc.); typology — hill, hill-forest, hill-plateau, coastal, island forts; Shivaji's guerrilla strategy embodied in landscape; India's 44th site; issues — conservation funding, encroachment, tourism pressure, ASI–State coordination.
Q510 marks

Indian classical dance forms are living traditions rather than museum pieces. Discuss with reference to their evolution in the twentieth century.

  • Body: revival from devadasi stigma (Rukmini Devi Arundale, Kalakshetra 1936); Sattriya recognised 2000; Kathak's court-to-proscenium journey; contemporary choreography (Chandralekha, Astad Deboo); diaspora transmission; institutional support (SNA, CCRT); tensions over 'purity' vs innovation.

B. Modern Indian History

Q615 marks

'The Revolt of 1857 was the culmination of a century of popular resistance rather than a sudden sepoy mutiny.' Critically examine.

  • Body: pre-1857 civil rebellions (Sanyasi–Fakir, Paika 1817, Kol 1831, Santhal 1855) and mutinies (Vellore 1806, Barrackpore 1824); shared grievances — revenue settlements, annexations (Doctrine of Lapse, Awadh 1856), dispossession of taluqdars, missionary anxieties; but 1857 differed — scale, sepoy–peasant–elite alliance, symbolic Mughal restoration; limitations — regional confinement, absence of a forward-looking programme; historiographical debate (Sen, Majumdar, Stokes, Mukherjee).
  • Conclusion: continuity of grievance, discontinuity of scale; watershed in colonial policy (Queen's Proclamation, army reorganisation).
Q710 marks

Assess the contribution of the Moderates to the national movement despite the charge of 'political mendicancy'.

  • Body: economic critique (Naoroji's drain theory, R.C. Dutt), constitutional agitation (Councils Acts 1892, 1909), creating a pan-Indian political culture and press, training ground for Extremists; limits — narrow social base, faith in British justice; Bipan Chandra's defence.
Q815 marks

How did Gandhi transform the Congress from an elite debating body into a mass movement between 1917 and 1922? What were the limits of this transformation?

  • Body: Champaran, Kheda, Ahmedabad as laboratories; Nagpur Constitution 1920 (four-anna membership, linguistic PCCs, Working Committee); satyagraha as a technique accessible to peasants, women, Muslims (Khilafat); constructive programme; limits — Chauri Chaura withdrawal, communal fault lines post-Khilafat, class ceiling (no-rent campaigns discouraged), Ambedkar's critique.
Q910 marks

Examine the role of women in the Civil Disobedience Movement and how it differed from their participation in Non-Cooperation.

  • Body: salt satyagraha picketing (Sarojini Naidu at Dharasana, Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay), Rashtriya Stree Sangha, Nari Satyagraha Samiti, Matangini Hazra, Rani Gaidinliu; contrast — 1921 mostly symbolic/domestic, 1930 public and organisational; consequences — Karachi Resolution 1931 on equality, franchise under 1935 Act; limits — upper-caste urban leadership.
Q1015 marks

'Partition was not inevitable until very late.' Discuss the developments between 1940 and 1947 that made it so.

  • Body: Lahore Resolution 1940 ambiguity; Cripps 1942 provincial opt-out; Quit India vacuum used by the League; 1945–46 elections (League's sweep of Muslim seats); Cabinet Mission grouping and its collapse (Nehru's July 1946 press conference); Direct Action Day and Noakhali–Bihar violence; interim government deadlock; Attlee's 20 February 1947 statement; Mountbatten's June 3 Plan; Congress acceptance rationale.
  • Conclusion: structural (separate electorates since 1909) plus contingent factors; Jalal's 'bargaining counter' thesis versus mainstream view.
Q1110 marks

Discuss the contribution of revolutionary nationalism to India's freedom struggle beyond individual acts of violence.

  • Body: HRA/HSRA's socialist turn (Bhagat Singh's 'Why I am an Atheist', Assembly bomb as propaganda), Chittagong armoury raid, Ghadar's diaspora mobilisation, INA's impact on the 1946 naval mutiny and the British decision to leave; ideological legacy (secularism, anti-imperialism) vs strategic limits.
Q1210 marks

Evaluate the integration of princely states as an achievement of statecraft.

  • Body: Instrument of Accession (three subjects), Patel–Menon strategy (privy purses, carrot-and-stick), Junagadh plebiscite, Hyderabad Police Action, Kashmir's accession and its legacy; States Reorganisation 1956 completing the process; 26th Amendment 1971 abolishing privy purses.

C. World History

Q1315 marks

'The Industrial Revolution was as much a social and political revolution as a technological one.' Examine with reference to Britain.

  • Body: technology (steam, textiles, iron) → factory system, urbanisation (Manchester), new classes (bourgeoisie, proletariat), Reform Acts 1832/1867, Chartism, Factory Acts, trade unions, ideologies (liberalism, socialism, Marx 1848), imperial demand for markets and raw materials (India's deindustrialisation), demographic transition.
Q1410 marks

How far was the Treaty of Versailles responsible for the Second World War?

  • Body: war guilt clause, reparations, territorial losses (Alsace, Polish corridor), disarmament humiliation feeding revanchism; counter — Weimar's economic crises (1923, 1929), appeasement, League's weakness, Japanese and Italian expansionism, ideological drivers of Nazism; historiographical balance (Taylor vs orthodox).
Q1515 marks

Compare the processes of decolonisation in Asia and Africa after 1945.

  • Body: Asia earlier and often negotiated (India 1947, Indonesia 1949 after struggle, Indochina violent); Africa 1957–75 wave (Ghana, 'Year of Africa' 1960), settler colonies violent (Algeria, Kenya, Rhodesia, Portuguese Africa); Cold War overlay; role of UN and Bandung; legacies — arbitrary borders, neo-colonial economies, Pan-Africanism vs Non-Alignment.
Q1610 marks

Discuss the causes and consequences of the redrawal of national boundaries after the First World War in Europe and West Asia.

  • Body: collapse of four empires; Wilsonian self-determination selectively applied; new states (Poland, Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia, Baltic states); Sykes–Picot and mandates (Iraq, Syria, Palestine — Balfour Declaration); minority problems (Sudetenland), Kurdish statelessness; seeds of 1939 and of West Asian conflicts.
Q1710 marks

Analyse the factors behind the disintegration of the Soviet Union in 1991.

  • Body: economic stagnation, Afghanistan, arms-race burden, glasnost unleashing nationalities, perestroika's half-measures, 1989 East European revolutions, August coup, Yeltsin and the Belovezha Accords; consequences — unipolar moment, NATO expansion debates, India's post-1991 realignment.

D. Indian Society

Q1815 marks

'Caste in India is changing but not disappearing.' Discuss in the light of contemporary developments.

  • Body: Srinivas's sanskritisation and dominant caste; caste as identity and political resource (Mandal, sub-classification judgment 2024, Bihar caste survey 2023, Census 2027 caste enumeration); persistence in marriage (endogamy above 90%), atrocities (NCRB), urban informal networks; dilution in occupation and commensality; caste in diaspora and digital spaces.
  • Conclusion: from a system of hierarchy to a system of competing identities (Béteille, Jodhka).
Q1910 marks

Examine the paradox of falling female labour force participation despite rising female education in India, and the recent reversal.

  • Body: U-shaped hypothesis, income effect, mechanisation of agriculture, safety and mobility constraints, care burden (Time Use Survey 2024); PLFS shows FLFPR rising from 23% (2017–18) to 41.7% (2023–24) driven by rural self-employment — quality concerns; policy levers — crèches, labour codes, urban jobs, transport.
Q2015 marks

Urbanisation in India is 'messy and hidden'. Elaborate, and suggest governance reforms.

  • Body: census towns (3,894 in 2011), peri-urban sprawl, informal settlements, weak ULBs (74th Amendment's unfinished agenda, 15th FC on property tax), climate vulnerability (Bengaluru/Chennai floods); reforms — Urban Challenge Fund (Budget 2025, ₹1 lakh crore), metropolitan planning committees, municipal bonds, city-level data, land pooling, transit-oriented development.
Q2110 marks

Discuss the role of social media in shaping communalism and secularism in contemporary India.
