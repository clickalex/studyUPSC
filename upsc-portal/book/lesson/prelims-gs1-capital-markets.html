Detailed Study

The full treatment of this lesson.

Primary-secondary mechanics, debt-derivatives, participants, settlement reforms. SEBI-term precision.

1. Primary market

  • Instruments: IPO-FPO-rights-bonus-private placement-QIP (qualified institutional placement); pricing: fixed vs book building (price band, cut-off); ASBA (application supported by blocked amount) + UPI-IPO for retail; anchor investors (50% QIB share pre-open); minimum public shareholding 25%; SME platforms (NSE Emerge-BSE SME); greenshoe option; delisting norms.
  • Regulation: SEBI ICDR regs; RHP-DRH; merchant bankers, registrars, credit-rating agencies; IPO disclosures via OFSDP; T+3 listing timeline (from T+6).

2. Secondary market & products

  • Exchanges: NSE-BSE (+MSE legacy; Metropolitan defunct), clearing corps; T+1 rolling settlement (Jan 2023; optional opt-in then all — India first major market); T+0 beta (2024 limited pilot — same-day settlement).
  • Debt: G-secs (RBI Retail Direct 2021, NDS-OM), SDLs, corporate bonds (SEBI; electronic book platform), voluntary retention route (VRR/FPI debt), fully-accessible securities (FAS); yield-curve drivers. Derivatives: equity F&O (index-heavy), currency-commodity derivatives (commodity derivatives unified under SEBI 2015 merger of FMC); position limits, expiry-day rationalisation (2024 curb on weekly index options).
  • Participants: FPIs (two categories), DIIs (mutual funds-insurance-pension: NPS-APY funds), QIBs, retail surge post-COVID (demat accounts ~15 crore band), AIFs, algo rules (2025 retail-algo framework), Research-Analyst-IA norms.

3. Reforms & mutual funds

  • SEBI (1992 act; powers post-SAHARA-SC orders; SAT appeal): F&O curbs 2024, small/midcap froth checks, nomination-e-will pushes; index-derivatives notional cap; risk-based supervision; ESG: BRSR-core disclosures, ESG-debt (sovereign green bonds, first tranches 2022-23).
  • Mutual funds: NAV-TRI (2018 total-return index benchmarking), expense slabs, SID transparency, MF utility-MF Central (2022 consolidated statement); SIP culture; passive growth (index funds-ETFs; CPSE-Bharat-22 ETF legacy).

MCQ hooks

  • QIP = preferential issue to QIBs (listed companies); ASBA blocks funds until allotment.
  • T+1 settlement 2023 (T+0 pilot 2024) — India first large market.
  • Commodity derivatives moved to SEBI in 2015 (FMC merger).
  • Minimum public shareholding = 25%.
  • RBI Retail Direct (2021) = retail G-sec access; VRR = FPI debt window.
  • BRSR = SEBI ESG disclosure; sovereign green bonds 2023.

6. Money Market versus Capital Market

The financial market divides by maturity. The money market deals in short-term instruments of up to one year, is regulated by the RBI, and exists to manage liquidity. The capital market deals in medium and long-term funds beyond one year, is regulated by SEBI, and exists to channel savings into investment. Getting the regulator right is half the marks in this topic.

Money market instruments:

  • Treasury Bills — issued by the Government of India through the RBI in 91, 182 and 364-day tenors. They are zero-coupon, issued at a discount and redeemed at face value; the difference is the return. There are no 14-day T-bills currently.
  • Cash Management Bills — ultra-short maturity, under 91 days, for temporary mismatches.
  • Certificate of Deposit — issued by banks; Commercial Paper — an unsecured promissory note issued by corporates and financial institutions with high credit rating.
  • Call money (overnight), notice money (2–14 days) and term money — the inter-bank market where the weighted average call rate is the RBI's operating target.
  • Repo and reverse repo, and Tri-Party Repo (TREPS) and market repo, which now dominate turnover.

7. Primary and Secondary Markets

The primary market is where securities are created and issuers raise fresh capital; the secondary market is where existing securities are traded between investors, giving liquidity and price discovery but bringing no new money to the company. This distinction is tested repeatedly.

Primary-issue vocabulary: IPO (a company's first public offer) and FPO (a subsequent one); Offer for Sale, where existing shareholders sell and the company receives nothing; rights issue to existing shareholders at a discount; bonus issue, a free capitalisation of reserves that changes nothing economically; private placement and Qualified Institutional Placement; preferential allotment; and green shoe option, an over-allotment mechanism to stabilise price. In book building, the price is discovered within a band through bids, as opposed to a fixed-price issue. Anchor investors are institutional investors allotted ahead of the issue. Red herring prospectus is the offer document that omits final price and issue size.

Instruments. Equity shares confer ownership, voting rights and a residual claim; preference shares carry a fixed dividend and priority in repayment but generally no voting rights; debentures and bonds are debt, carrying a fixed coupon, with holders as creditors ranking ahead of shareholders. Convertible instruments transform into equity. Government bonds are G-Secs or dated securities; State Development Loans are their state counterpart; masala bonds are rupee-denominated bonds issued abroad, transferring currency risk to the investor; ADRs and GDRs are depository receipts allowing Indian companies to raise foreign equity; municipal bonds fund urban local bodies; green bonds finance climate projects, and India has issued sovereign green bonds; social impact bonds and SGrBs are the newer instruments.

Bond pricing logic is a favourite conceptual question: bond prices and yields move inversely. When market interest rates rise, existing bonds paying lower coupons become less attractive, so their price falls until their yield to maturity matches the new rate. Longer-duration bonds are more sensitive. An inverted yield curve — short-term yields exceeding long-term — is a classic recession signal.

8. Institutions, Intermediaries and Derivatives

SEBI, statutory since 1992, has a threefold mandate: protect investor interests, develop the securities market, and regulate it. It exercises quasi-legislative (framing regulations), quasi-executive (investigation, search and seizure) and quasi-judicial (adjudication and penalties) powers; appeals lie to the Securities Appellate Tribunal and thence to the Supreme Court. Its notable regulations cover insider trading, prohibition of fraudulent and unfair trade practices, listing obligations and disclosure requirements, and the takeover code.

Exchanges and infrastructure: the BSE (1875, Asia's oldest, benchmark Sensex of 30 stocks) and the NSE (benchmark Nifty 50); depositories NSDL and CDSL, which hold securities in dematerialised form; clearing corporations that act as central counterparty and guarantee settlement; and the settlement cycle, which India has moved to T+1, among the fastest globally. Intermediaries include brokers, merchant bankers, registrars, custodians and credit rating agencies.

Collective investment: mutual funds pool investor money under an Asset Management Company, valued by Net Asset Value; ETFs track an index and trade like shares; SIPs average cost over time; REITs and InvITs allow retail participation in real estate and infrastructure; AIFs cover private equity, venture capital and hedge funds. FPIs (portfolio, liquid, "hot money") must be distinguished from FDI (lasting management interest, stable) — the distinction and its implications for external vulnerability are standard mains content. Participatory notes are offshore derivative instruments issued to investors not registered in India, historically a transparency concern.

Derivatives derive value from an underlying asset: forwards (customised, OTC), futures (standardised, exchange-traded), options (a right without obligation — a call to buy, a put to sell) and swaps. They serve hedging, speculation and arbitrage. Also know short selling, margin trading, circuit breakers, bull and bear markets, market capitalisation categories, and algorithmic trading.

9. Functions, Reforms and Risks

The capital market performs capital formation, liquidity, price discovery, risk transfer and corporate governance discipline through the threat of takeover and the scrutiny of disclosure. For an economy attempting to finance infrastructure without straining bank balance sheets already burdened by non-performing assets, deepening the corporate bond market is the standard policy prescription; India's bond market remains shallow, dominated by AAA private placements, with weak secondary liquidity and a small municipal segment.

Reform milestones to cite: dematerialisation and screen-based trading, which ended the open-outcry and badla systems; the shift to T+1; the introduction of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, improving creditor recovery and thus bond-market confidence; GIFT City and the IFSCA as a unified regulator for international financial services; and the Account Aggregator framework. Historical scandals — Harshad Mehta (1992), which precipitated SEBI's statutory empowerment, Ketan Parekh, the NSEL and Satyam episodes — are useful illustrations of regulatory learning.

Risks to name: excessive financialisation and disconnection from the real economy; herd behaviour and asset bubbles; volatility from FPI reversals, aggravated by global monetary tightening; retail investors entering derivatives with poor risk understanding, which SEBI has flagged; and inadequate financial literacy. The investor protection apparatus — grievance redressal through SCORES, the Investor Protection and Education Fund, and mandatory KYC — is the counterweight.

Trap watch. Treasury Bills are issued by the Government through the RBI, never by the RBI on its own account, and are zero-coupon. Commercial paper is corporate, certificates of deposit are bank-issued. Money market is RBI-regulated, capital market SEBI-regulated. Only the primary market brings fresh capital to the issuer; in an Offer for Sale the company receives nothing. Bond prices move inversely to yields. FPI is portfolio and volatile, FDI is durable. Options confer a right, futures an obligation. Masala bonds are rupee-denominated but issued overseas.
