Detailed Study

The full treatment of this lesson.

Class-X level maths at speed: percent-ratio core, arithmetic set, DI reading discipline.

1. Percent-ratio core

  • Commutative percent: x% of y = y% of x (instant 18% of 50 = 9); fraction-percent anchors: 1/8 = 12.5%, 1/6 ≈ 16.67%, 1/7 ≈ 14.28%, 1/12 ≈ 8.33%.
  • Successive change: a + b + ab/100 (two 10% rises = 21%); percentage-change = (new − old)/old × 100 with old as base — base-swap is the classic trap.
  • Income-expenditure ratios: assume LCM values; part-whole from ratios (3:5 → 3/8, 5/8).

2. Arithmetic set

  • Profit-loss: markup on CP → discount on MP; SP/CP ratio method ((100+P)/100); successive discounts add via a+b+ab/100 (not simple sum).
  • SI linear
  • CI compounding
  • 2-year CI − SI = P(r/100)²
  • halves-doubling (rule of 72 ≈ 72/r years).
  • TSD: distance constant → speed × time trade (speed ×1.25 → time ÷1.25); average speed for equal distances = 2ab/(a+b) (never (a+b)/2); trains: relative speed on opposite directions.
  • Time-work: LCM-of-days method (work = units)
  • pipes: fill − empty
  • efficiency ∝ 1/time
  • men-days: M₁D₁H₁/W₁ = M₂D₂H₂/W₂.
  • Averages-mixtures: alligation (cheaper:dearer = d−m : m−c); replacement formula for repeated dilution.

3. Data interpretation discipline

  • Read title-units-footnotes first; per-capita vs total traps; percentage-point vs percent change distinction.
  • Approximate aggressively — options are far apart; compute only what the question asks; DI often needs no exact arithmetic (rank-compare questions).
  • Charts: bar = compare, line = trend, pie = share (angles: share × 3.6°), table = precision lookups.

4. Probability & counting

  • P = favourable/total
  • complement: P(at least one) = 1 − P(none)
  • independent: multiply
  • mutually exclusive: add.
  • Permutations with repetition (letters of a word), combinations = selection (nCr), circular arrangements (n−1)!; dice-card standard setups memorised.

⚡ MCQ hooks

  • x% of y = y% of x — instant mental math.
  • Successive changes: a+b+ab/100 — discounts trap (not simple sum).
  • Equal-distance average speed = 2ab/(a+b) — never arithmetic mean.
  • CI−SI (2 yr) = P(r/100)² — formula ask.
  • Alligation ratio = (d−m):(m−c) — mixture ask.
  • P(at least one) = 1 − P(none) — complement first.
  • Percentage-point ≠ percent change — DI trap.

5. Arithmetic Foundations: Numbers, Ratios and Percentages

The CSAT quantitative component is pitched at roughly Class X level and rewards fluency in a small set of arithmetic ideas rather than advanced mathematics. Since the paper is qualifying at 33% with one-third negative marking, the objective is to secure the mechanical, high-certainty questions quickly.

Number system. Divisibility rules should be automatic: by 2 if the last digit is even; by 3 if the digit sum is divisible by 3; by 4 if the last two digits form a multiple of 4; by 5 if it ends in 0 or 5; by 6 if divisible by both 2 and 3; by 8 if the last three digits are a multiple of 8; by 9 if the digit sum is divisible by 9; by 11 if the alternating digit sum is divisible by 11. HCF and LCM are the workhorses — the product of two numbers equals the product of their HCF and LCM. Use HCF for problems about cutting into the largest equal pieces or distributing into the greatest equal groups, and LCM for repeating events such as bells ringing together, runners meeting on a circular track, or the smallest number leaving a given remainder. Remainder theorems, unit-digit cyclicity (the unit digit of powers repeats in cycles of at most four), and the count of trailing zeros in a factorial (determined by the number of factors of 5) recur regularly.

Percentages underpin most of the paper, so the fraction equivalents should be memorised: 1/2 = 50%, 1/3 = 33⅓%, 1/4 = 25%, 1/5 = 20%, 1/6 = 16⅔%, 1/7 ≈ 14²/₇%, 1/8 = 12½%, 1/9 = 11⅑%, 1/11 = 9¹/₁₁%, 1/12 = 8⅓%. Converting a percentage into a fraction turns a multiplication into a cancellation and saves substantial time.

Two percentage traps deserve explicit attention. First, successive percentage change is not additive: a rise of a% followed by b% gives a net change of a + b + ab/100. Hence a 20% increase followed by a 20% decrease yields a net 4% loss, not zero. Second, the base matters: if A is 25% more than B, then B is 20% less than A, because the bases differ. The general relation is that if A is x% more than B, B is 100x/(100 + x) per cent less than A. Distinguish also a change of five percentage points from a five per cent change.

Ratio and proportion. Ratios compare quantities of the same kind; keep the order strictly as stated. To combine A:B and B:C, scale to a common B. In problems about mixing, the alligation rule is far quicker than equations: the ratio of the quantities of two ingredients equals the ratio of the differences of the mean from each of the two values, taken crosswise. Alligation applies to mixtures, average speeds, average marks, and any weighted average — recognising that a weighted-average question is an alligation question is one of the biggest time savings available.

6. The Standard Word-Problem Families

Averages. The average is the total divided by the count. Most questions are best handled by the total rather than the average itself: if the average of n items changes by d when one item is replaced, the change in the total is n×d, which gives the replacement directly. For the average of consecutive numbers, the answer is simply the middle term. A weighted average always lies between the two component averages and closer to the one with greater weight — a useful sanity check.

Time, speed and distance. Speed = distance ÷ time; conversions run 1 km/h = 5/18 m/s and 1 m/s = 18/5 km/h. When the distance is constant, time is inversely proportional to speed, so a ratio of speeds a:b gives times in the ratio b:a — this single observation solves a large share of questions without algebra. Average speed for equal distances at speeds u and v is the harmonic mean 2uv/(u+v), never the arithmetic mean; for equal times, it is the arithmetic mean. In relative speed, add speeds for opposite directions and subtract for the same direction. For trains, a train crossing a pole covers its own length, while crossing a platform or another train covers the sum of lengths. In boats and streams, downstream speed is boat plus current and upstream is boat minus current, so the boat's speed in still water is the average of the two and the current's speed is half their difference.

Time and work. Treat work as a unit and rates as reciprocals: if A finishes in a days, A's rate is 1/a per day, and combined rates add. The efficient technique is to take the LCM of the individual times as the total work, converting fractions into whole units — if A takes 12 days and B 18, set the work at 36 units, giving rates of 3 and 2 units per day, so together 5 units per day and 7.2 days in total. Efficiency is inversely proportional to time taken. Pipes and cisterns are identical in structure, with an outlet pipe simply carrying a negative rate. The chain rule handles men-days-hours problems: M₁D₁H₁/W₁ = M₂D₂H₂/W₂.

Profit, loss and interest. Profit percentage is always calculated on cost price unless stated otherwise, while discount is always on the marked price — confusing the two bases is the standard error. Selling two articles at the same price with equal percentage profit and loss always results in a net loss of x²/100 per cent. Simple interest is PRT/100 and grows linearly; compound interest is P(1 + R/100)ⁿ − P and grows geometrically. For two years, the difference between compound and simple interest is PR²/10000, and for three years PR²(300 + R)/10⁶ — memorising the two-year formula answers a recurring question instantly. Note also that compound interest at r% for n years is structurally identical to successive percentage increase, and that population growth, depreciation and appreciation are the same formula in different clothing.

Permutations, combinations and probability appear in small numbers but are highly formulaic. Use permutations when order matters and combinations when it does not; the arrangements of n objects with repetitions are n!/(p!q!…); circular arrangements of n objects number (n−1)!. Probability is favourable outcomes over total outcomes, with the addition rule for mutually exclusive events and multiplication for independent ones; the complement rule — computing 1 minus the probability of the event not happening — is usually the faster route for "at least one" questions.

7. Calculation Speed, Estimation and Examination Craft

No calculator is permitted, so calculation technique is itself a scoring skill. The essential preparation is knowing tables to 20, squares to 30, cubes to 15, and the common square roots. Learn a handful of multiplication shortcuts: multiplying by 5 is halving then multiplying by 10; by 25 is dividing by 4 and multiplying by 100; by 9 is multiplying by 10 and subtracting the number; the square of a number ending in 5 is the leading digits times their successor, followed by 25, so 45² = 4×5 followed by 25, giving 2025. Use the algebraic identities (a+b)² , (a−b)² and (a+b)(a−b) to handle awkward products — 98 × 102 is (100−2)(100+2) = 9996.

Estimation is usually superior to exact computation. Inspect the options first: if they are far apart, round aggressively and eliminate. Check the unit digit of the answer, which often eliminates two or three options at no cost. Verify orders of magnitude and apply plausibility tests — an average must lie between the extremes, a probability must lie between 0 and 1, a weighted average must lie nearer the heavier weight. In many questions back-substitution of the options is faster than forming and solving an equation, and this should be a first resort rather than a last one.

Examination craft. Budget roughly 20 to 25 minutes of the 120 for the quantitative questions, leaving the bulk for comprehension and reasoning. Make two passes: in the first, take only the questions whose method you recognise within ten seconds; in the second, return to the rest. Because negative marking is one-third of a mark, a genuinely blind guess is negative in expectation, but a guess after eliminating two options is positive in expectation and should be taken. Watch the units in the question — kilometres against metres, hours against minutes, per cent against percentage points — because a large fraction of errors at this level are not conceptual but clerical.

Practice design. Solve previous year CSAT papers from 2011 onward under timed conditions; the 2011 to 2014 papers are noticeably harder and make excellent training. Re-solve every incorrect question a week later from scratch. Maintain an error log classified by cause — wrong base, unit conversion, misread question, arithmetic slip, formula misapplied — and you will find that a small number of recurring causes account for most losses. Ten to fifteen minutes of daily mental arithmetic over several months does more for the score than any quantity of new theory, because at this level the constraint is speed and accuracy under pressure, not knowledge.

Trap watch. Successive changes of +20% and −20% give a net 4% loss, not zero. Profit is on cost price, discount on marked price. Average speed over equal distances is the harmonic mean, not the arithmetic mean. Selling two items at the same price with equal profit and loss percentages always yields a loss of x²/100 per cent. Time is inversely proportional to speed and to efficiency. In "A is what per cent more than B", the base is B. Percentage points are not per cent. The complement rule is the fast route for "at least one" probability. CSAT is qualifying at 33%, so certainty beats coverage.

A price rises 20% then falls 20%. Net change:

  1. (a)0%
  2. (b)4% fall

CP ₹250, marked ₹400, discount 20%. Profit %:

  1. (a)28%
  2. (b)25%
  3. (c)20%
  4. (d)32%
Show answer
Correct: (a)(SP 320; 70/250 = 28%)
Q3

  1. (a)50
  2. (b)48
  3. (c)45
  4. (d)52
