6. Settlement Geography: Types, Patterns and Hierarchy

A settlement is an organised colony of human beings together with the structures they occupy. The primary division is rural versus urban, and the criteria differ across countries — in India the Census defines a statutory town as any place with a municipality, corporation, cantonment board or notified town area committee, and a census town as a place satisfying three conditions simultaneously: a minimum population of 5,000, a density of at least 400 persons per square kilometre, and at least 75% of the male working population engaged in non-agricultural pursuits. The rapid growth of census towns — places that have urbanised in character without any change in administrative status — is a major reason India's urbanisation is understated and why such settlements lack urban governance and finance. This is often called "in-situ" or hidden urbanisation.

Rural settlement patterns, determined by terrain, water availability, security and social structure:

  • Compact or nucleated — houses clustered together; typical of fertile alluvial plains with assured water and, historically, of areas needing collective defence. The Indo-Gangetic plain is the classic case.
  • Dispersed or scattered — isolated farmsteads; characteristic of hills, forests, and areas of extensive farming or high water-table uniformity, as in Meghalaya, Kerala and parts of the Himalaya.
  • Semi-clustered or fragmented — often the result of social segregation, where a dominant group occupies the core and others live in peripheral hamlets; common in Gujarat, Rajasthan and parts of the Deccan.
  • Hamleted — a settlement fragmented into several physically separate units bearing local names such as para, pana, palli or tola; common in the middle and lower Ganga plain, Chhattisgarh and the lower Himalaya.

Shape-based types recur in prelims: linear (along a road, river, canal or levee), rectangular (on plains with a grid of routes), circular or semi-circular (around a lake, pond or tank), star-shaped (at the convergence of several roads), T-, Y- and cross-shaped (at road junctions and confluences), double (either side of a river with a bridge) and terraced on hill slopes.

Urban classification by function — administrative (New Delhi, Chandigarh, Gandhinagar), industrial (Jamshedpur, Bhilai), transport and port (Kandla, Kochi, Mughalsarai), commercial (Kolkata, Kanpur), mining (Raniganj, Digboi, Singrauli), garrison (Ambala, Jalandhar), educational (Roorkee, Pilani), religious (Varanasi, Amritsar, Tirupati) and tourist (Shimla, Nainital) — with the caveat that all large cities are multifunctional. By population the Census hierarchy runs from Class I towns (100,000 and above) downward; a metropolis exceeds one million, a megacity ten million, and a conurbation, Patrick Geddes's term, is the coalescence of several towns into a continuous built-up mass. A million-plus urban agglomeration comprises a core town plus its contiguous outgrowths.

Christaller's central place theory supplies the theoretical frame: settlements form a hierarchy in which higher-order places offer higher-order goods and services with a larger range (maximum distance a consumer will travel) and a larger threshold (minimum population needed to sustain the service), and on an isotropic plain their market areas tessellate into hexagons. Losch refined it for manufacturing; Burgess's concentric zone, Hoyt's sector and Harris and Ullman's multiple nuclei models describe internal urban structure and are worth naming with their core insight — respectively, growth outward in rings, growth along transport sectors, and growth around several specialised nuclei.

7. Urbanisation in India: Process, Problems and Policy

India's urban population is roughly a third of the total and projected to approach half by 2050 — a slower rate than East Asia but on an enormous base, so the absolute increment is the largest in the world. The distinguishing features are top-heaviness, with a disproportionate share of the urban population in Class I cities and million-plus agglomerations; strong regional variation, with Tamil Nadu, Maharashtra, Gujarat and Kerala far more urbanised than Bihar, Odisha and Assam; and the salience of census towns noted above.

Drivers: rural push from land fragmentation, agricultural distress and underemployment; urban pull from wages and services; natural increase within cities; and reclassification and boundary expansion. Where push dominates over pull, the result is over-urbanisation and the growth of the informal sector without a corresponding rise in productivity.

Problems, each with its policy response:

  • Housing and slums — a large share of the urban population in informal settlements with insecure tenure. Response: PMAY-Urban with its in-situ redevelopment, credit-linked subsidy, affordable housing in partnership and beneficiary-led verticals, and the Affordable Rental Housing Complexes for migrants.
  • Water, sanitation and solid waste — intermittent supply, high non-revenue water, untreated sewage and unscientific landfilling. Response: AMRUT and AMRUT 2.0, Swachh Bharat Mission-Urban with source segregation and remediation of legacy dumpsites, and the Solid Waste Management Rules, 2016.
  • Transport and congestion — the modal shift to private vehicles, low road space, and the resulting congestion and air pollution. Response: metro rail expansion, the National Urban Transport Policy with its principle of moving people, not vehicles, bus rapid transit, non-motorised transport and transit-oriented development concentrating density around transit corridors.
  • Air pollution and the urban heat island — the latter caused by heat-absorbing surfaces, waste heat, loss of vegetation and canyon geometry trapping radiation, mitigated by urban greening, cool roofs and water bodies; the National Clean Air Programme is the pollution instrument.
  • Urban flooding — from encroachment on wetlands and drainage channels, impervious surfaces raising runoff and reducing infiltration, and inadequate stormwater drains. Chennai 2015, Mumbai 2005 and Bengaluru are the standard cases; sponge-city principles and the restoration of natural drainage are the answers.
  • Municipal finance and governance — the deepest structural problem. Despite the 74th Amendment and the Twelfth Schedule's eighteen functions, urban local bodies lack devolved funds, functions and functionaries; property tax collection is poor; parastatals and development authorities dilute accountability; and there is no directly empowered mayor in most states. Instruments: State Finance Commissions, municipal bonds, value capture financing and the Fifteenth Finance Commission's performance-linked urban grants.

Missions to cite accurately: the Smart Cities Mission (100 cities, area-based development plus pan-city ICT solutions, implemented through Special Purpose Vehicles — the critique being the bypassing of elected municipal bodies), HRIDAY for heritage cities, DAY-NULM for urban livelihoods, and PM SVANidhi for street vendors, alongside the Street Vendors Act, 2014 with its Town Vending Committees.

8. Transport Geography and Communication Networks

Transport is evaluated on cost structure and suitability rather than on speed alone. Roads offer door-to-door flexibility and are cheapest for short hauls but expensive per tonne-kilometre over distance. Railways have high fixed and low variable costs, making them efficient for bulk commodities over long distances. Waterways — inland and ocean — are the cheapest per tonne-kilometre and the most fuel-efficient and least polluting, but slowest and constrained by geography; they carry about 95% of India's trade by volume. Pipelines are ideal for liquids and gases, with very high initial cost but negligible operating cost and no return-haul problem. Air transport is fastest and costliest, justified only for high-value, low-bulk or perishable goods and for passengers. Efficient systems are therefore multimodal, and the containerisation revolution — standardised intermodal boxes — is what made global value chains economically possible.

Global networks worth knowing: the Trans-Siberian Railway, the longest in the world, linking St Petersburg to Vladivostok; the Trans-Canadian and Union Pacific; the Trans-Australian; the Orient Express; the proposed Trans-Asian Railway; the Pan-American Highway; and the great sea lanes of the North Atlantic, the Mediterranean-Indian Ocean route through Suez, and the Cape route. In India, note the Golden Quadrilateral, the North-South and East-West corridors, the Dedicated Freight Corridors, National Waterway 1 on the Ganga, and the UDAN regional air connectivity scheme.

Communication has collapsed the friction of distance — the phenomenon of time-space convergence. Submarine optical fibre cables carry the overwhelming majority of international data traffic, making them strategic infrastructure; satellite communication serves remote and maritime areas; and BharatNet aims at broadband to every gram panchayat. The corresponding concern is the digital divide across rural-urban, gender and income lines, and the concentration of data infrastructure in a few metropolitan hubs.

Trap watch. A census town requires all three conditions together — 5,000 population, 400 persons per sq km, and 75% male non-agricultural workforce. A metropolis is a million-plus city; a megacity exceeds ten million. Christaller's range is the maximum distance travelled, threshold the minimum population needed. Hamleted settlements are fragmented units of one village, not separate villages. Smart Cities Mission works through SPVs, which is the source of the accountability critique. Pipelines have high fixed and low operating costs. Waterways are cheapest per tonne-kilometre; railways suit bulk over long distance.
