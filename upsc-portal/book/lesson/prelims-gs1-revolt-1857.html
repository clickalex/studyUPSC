Revolt of 1857 & Aftermath

In this lessonDetailed StudyRevision DigestKey Points & MindmapDiagrams, Maps & FlowchartsPrevious Year Questions

Detailed Study

The full treatment of this lesson.

First major challenge to Company rule: causes, spread, leaders, suppression, and the 1858 settlement.

1. Causes

  • Political: Lapse annexations (Jhansi, Nagpur, Satara), Awadh 1856, Mughal-pension fear (Dalhousie's succession plan), disrespect to Nawabs/rajas.
  • Economic: land-tax squeeze, deindustrialisation (duty-biased trade), drain theory seeds (Dadabhai later articulates), zamindari distress, taluqdari grievance in Awadh.
  • Socio-religious: missionary push, Religious Disabilities Act 1850 (convert-inheritance), rail-telegraph suspicion, sea-voyage loss-of-caste fear.
  • Military: sepoy-european ratio, pay-rank gaps, General Service Enlistment 1856 (cross-the-sea clause), Enfield rifle greased cartridge (cow-pig fat rumour) — Barrackpore Mangal Pandey 29 March 1857 (hanged 8 April).
  • Historiography tags: 'Sepoy Mutiny' (British school) vs 'First War of Independence' (V.D. Savarkar 1909) vs 'popular-military mix' (modern view — S.N. Sen, R.C. Majumdar).

3. Suppression & aftermath

  • Campbell (Cawnpore Dec 1857, Lucknow Mar 1858, Oudh pacification), Havelock-Outram reliefs, Nicholson-Lawrence Delhi, Rose central India, Cooper's Allahabad summary justice; proclamation of peace Nov 1858 (Campbell-Canning).
  • Government of India Act 1858: Company ends; Secretary of State + Council (15); Viceroy replaces GG (Canning first); Queen Victoria Proclamation 1 Nov 1858 (Allahabad durbar — 'Magna Carta of Indian people' tag); army reorganisation (higher European ratio, mixed castes, artillery European-only); princely states protected (paramountcy doctrine starts); policy of non-annexation.

MCQ hooks

  • Match centres-leaders table verbatim (5 main pairs).
  • 1858 Act twins: Secretary of State (London) + Viceroy (Canning) — Company abolished.
  • Proclamation date 1 Nov 1858, venue Allahabad.
  • Bahadur Shah II exiled to Rangoon (died 1862).
  • Awadh annexation 1856 (misgovernment plea) ≠ Jhansi 1853 (lapse).

7. The Historiography at Full Stretch: Mutiny, War, Reaction, Subaltern

Section 1 tables five readings; this section argues them — because the \"nature of 1857\" question is Mains' most-recurring modern-history 15-marker, and it is graded on historiographical staging, not verdict-delivery. The imperial school (Kaye–Malleson, Seeley — \"a mere sepoy mutiny,\" later the \"Muslim conspiracy\" and \"Hindu–Muslim intrigue\" variants): evidence cited (the cartridge trigger, the Bengal-Army specificity, loyal Punjab–Madras–Bombay, princely neutrality-or-aid — Hyderabad, Gwalior's Scindia, Patiala), motive imputed (restorationist — Nana Sahib's pension, the Rani's throne, Zafar's shadow-crown — grievances, not nationalism). The nationalist school (Savarkar, The Indian War of Independence, 1909 — written in London, banned before print — \"planned, national, liberationist\": the chapati-and-lotus circulation as organisation, Hindu–Muslim unity as programme, 31 May 1857 as the fixed date): evidence stretched (the \"plan\" largely inferred), significance political (the bible of revolutionary nationalism — Bhagat Singh's generation raised on it). The scholarly sceptics (R.C. Majumdar — mutiny-into-peasant-revolt, neither national nor first-war; S.N. Sen's official centenary history — not national, but leaders-not-all-reactionary — the state's balanced brief; the Marxists — feudal reaction, the ancien régime fighting modernity — Kosambi–Desai lineage: taluqdars and princes defending privilege, peasants following patrons).

The subaltern revision (Ranajit Guha's Elementary Aspects of Peasant Insurgency — 1857 as peasant-domain autonomy: the ulgulan grammar — inversion (attacking symbols — jails, telegraphs, bungalows), the rumor-as-mobilisation (the bone-dust flour, the greased cartridge — \"the panic that was also a programme\"), Eric Stokes's The Peasant and the Raj — the revolt's localism: Awadh's taluqdar-peasant alliance (the 21,000-of-23,000 estates lost — §2's datum — as the material base), the \"primary resistance\" thesis; Rudrangshu Mukherjee's Awadh micro-history — the four phases of Lucknow: mutiny → popular siege → British re-conquest → guerrilla tail) recovers what both imperialists and nationalists miss: the people's revolt inside the sepoys' mutiny — the cultivators, artisans and demobilised retainers who fought without pensions to restore. The examination-grade synthesis concedes each school its evidence (imperialists right on geography's limits and leadership's rivalries; nationalists right on Hindu–Muslim cooperation and anti-British breadth; Marxists right on feudal leadership's class character; subalternists right on peasant autonomy) and lands the candidate's verdict: a sepoy mutiny that ignited a regionally-concentrated popular uprising — national in sentiment where it burned, never national in organisation — whose defeat taught the next generation that sentiment needs organisation, which is what the Congress (1885) supplied.

8. Awadh: The Epicentre Examined

Sections 2–3 distribute causes and centres nationally; this section concentrates on Awadh — because the revolt's popular character is proved or disproved in Awadh, and the candidate who knows Awadh knows 1857's argument. The annexation (February 1856 — Dalhousie's \"misgovernment\" charge, Outram's report as pretext — Wajid Ali Shah deposed to Calcutta's Matia Burj, his army disbanded, his courtiers pensioned-or-ruined) detonated every social layer simultaneously: the taluqdars (disarmed, dispossessed — the Summary Settlement 1856 stripping 21,000 of 23,000 estates — §2's datum — the class that led the revolt because it lost everything); the sepoys (Awadh supplied ~40,000 of the Bengal Army — high-caste peasant-soldiers whose families the settlement ruined — the mutiny's grievance made personal: serve the Company, lose the estate); the peasantry (revenue demands doubled in places, moneylenders empowered by new courts, the begar-and-tax squeeze); the artisans and retainers (Lucknow's court economy — chikankari, zardozi, music, cuisine — collapsed with the court); and the religious establishment (Shia–Sunni ulema, temple networks — the cartridge rumour landing on soil already sown by the Religious Disabilities Act 1856 and missionary visibility). No other province combined all five detonations — which is why Awadh burned hottest and longest.

The Lucknow siege (May 1857–March 1858 — Henry Lawrence killed July 1857; the Residency's 87-day first siege; Havelock–Outram's September relief-in-name (reinforcement, not relief); Campbell's November evacuation to Cawnpore; the final March-1858 recapture — street-by-street, the Kaisarbagh stormed) and the Birjis Qadr–Hazrat Mahal government (the boy-king crowned, the Begum's administration — revenue collected, troops paid, proclamations issued — the famous counter-proclamation answering Canning's: point-by-point refutation of British promises — land, religion, service — the revolt's most articulate state-paper) prove organisation beyond mutiny. The rural guerrilla tail (summer 1858–59 — taluqdar-peasant bands, the Nepal-terai holdouts, Nana–Tantia–Azimullah's fugitive arc ending at Tantia's April-1859 betrayal-and-hanging) proves depth beyond cities. Canning's Oudh proclamation (March 1858 — confiscation of all taluqdari estates save five loyalists — \"Clemency Canning\" mocked by London for leniency while confiscating a province — then the November-1858 Queen's reversal: estates restored on submission — the carrot-after-stick that ended the guerrilla war) proves the British understood exactly what they faced: a propertied rebellion requiring a property settlement. Awadh's lesson for the answer: where dispossession was total (taluqdars), mobilisation was total — 1857's popular depth tracks its material grievance precisely.

9. Leaders, Proclamations, and Participation: Who Fought, Who Didn't, Why

Section 3's centre-table names leaders and suppressors; this section asks the participation question — the \"why\" behind the \"who\" — because Mains grades explanation, Prelims grades names, and both need the pairing. The leaders, each with motive-plus-evidence: Bahadur Shah Zafar (82, reluctant — proclaimed 12 May 1857 — the symbol the sepoys needed and the poet-king who signed proclamations he didn't write — tried at the Red Fort 1858, exiled to Rangoon, dead 1862 — the Mughal line's full stop); Nana Sahib (Dhondu Pant — the denied pension — Bithur's grievance, Cawnpore's June-1857 rising, the Satichaura–Bibighar massacres staining the cause — fled to Nepal, fate unknown); Tantia Tope (Ramchandra Pandurang — the revolt's operational brain — Cawnpore, Kalpi, the Gwalior dash, the 1858–59 guerrilla campaign across Central India — betrayed by Man Singh, hanged 18 April 1859); Rani Lakshmibai (the adopted Damodar's throne denied 1853 — \"main apni Jhansi nahin dungi\" — Jhansi's June-1857 rising, the Kalpi–Gwalior campaign alongside Tantia, dead at Gwalior 18 June 1858 — Hugh Rose's tribute: \"the most dangerous of Indian leaders\" — the revolt's military legend); Begum Hazrat Mahal (Awadh's regent — the counter-proclamation's authorial voice — refused Canning's pension, died in Kathmandu exile 1879 — the revolt's unbroken will); Kunwar Singh (Arrah's octogenarian — 80, commanding boots-and-all — the Eyre-column's defeat, the Ganga-crossing legend — dead days after victory, April 1858 — Bihar's lion); Khan Bahadur Khan (Bareilly's Rohilla governor — administration, coinage, order — hanged 1860); Bakht Khan (Delhi's sepoy-general — the Bareilly artilleryman who organised the capital's defence — the professional soldier as revolutionary). The proclamations (the Delhi Fath-e-Islam-style appeals; the Azamgarh proclamation — merchants, artisans, sepoys addressed class-by-class — the revolt's sociology in its own words; Hazrat Mahal's counter-proclamation — the state-paper) prove programme-awareness: zamindars promised estates, traders promised customs-relief, sepoys promised pay-and-faith — the coalition bid in writing.

Who didn't fight — and why (§4's failure-analysis deepened): the princes (Scindia's Gwalior troops held by the Maharaja — his neutrality \"saved India for the British,\" Canning's famous admission; Hyderabad's Salar Jung suppressed the city rising; Patiala–Jind–Nabha guarded the Punjab roads; Nepal's Jung Bahadur lent Gurkhas — the breakwater-in-advance: self-preservation plus British-proven invincibility); Punjab (disarmed 1849, prospering under Lawrence's roads-canals, Sikh memory of Mughal–Maratha enemies making the Delhi restoration unappealing — the \"martial races\" loyalty purchased by preference); Madras–Bombay armies (lower-caste, better-paid, no cartridge issue — presidencies calm); Bengal–south India (no sepoy concentration, the Permanent Settlement's zamindars quiescent, the intelligentsia hostile — the Derozians and Hindu Patriot's Harish Mukherjee condemning — modern educated Indians seeing feudal restoration, not freedom); big merchants–moneylenders (the Company's courts protected their bonds — their capital voted British). The participation synthesis: 1857 mobilised those the Company dispossessed (taluqdars, sepoys, artisans, deposed courts) and was boycotted by those it enriched or protected (princes, Punjab, presidencies, intelligentsia, capital) — a rebellion of the dispossessed, defeated by the coalition of the contented plus the telegraph.

10. Consequences Unpacked: Crown, Army, Princes, and the Racial State

Section 5 lists eight consequences; this section unpacks them into the post-1857 order — because \"1857 changed everything\" is only true if the candidate can say what, how, and for whom. The Crown takeover (Government of India Act, August 1858 — Company and Board of Control abolished; Secretary of State for India with a 15-member Council — \"despotism tempered by distance\"; Governor-General becomes Viceroy — Canning first — the Crown ruling through proclamation-prestige); the Queen's Proclamation (1 November 1858, Allahabad durbar — read by Canning: religious non-interference pledged, Doctrine of Lapse renounced, \"our subjects, of whatever race or creed\" promised equal opportunity — the Magna Carta of Indian loyalism, honoured in text, graded in practice: the ICS age-limits and venue (London-only exams) keeping the promise theoretical — Surendranath Banerjea's 1877 disqualification-campaign as the promise-tested). Army reorganisation (the Peel Commission — Europeans-to-Indians ratio fixed (~1:2 Bengal, 1:3 Madras–Bombay — up from 1:5+); artillery-and-tanks European-only; regiments mixed by caste–region–religion (\"divide and counterpoise\" — the Sikh–Gurkha–Pathan \"martial races\" doctrine formalised — recruitment manuals ranking communities' soldierliness — the ethnographic state enlisted); no single-caste regiments, no all-Indian brigades — the army re-engineered against united mutiny).

Princes as breakwater (sunnuds of adoption granted — annexation renounced, loyalty purchased: 562 states guaranteed, gun-salutes ranked, the Chamber of Princes 1921 distant-descendant — the conservative ballast against nationalism: \"breakwater against the waves\" in Canning's phrase); divide and rule formalised (communal enumeration in censuses from 1871 — categories hardened by counting; Muslim \"favouritism\" after the 1870s — the Aligarh strategy — versus Hindu-majority suspicion — the wedge institutionalised toward separate electorates 1909); the racial state (the pre-1857 \"white Mughal\" intimacy buried — clubs, cantonments, hill-stations as colour-bars; the Ilbert Bill storm 1883–84 as the racial state's temper-tantrum — white planters vetoing Indian judges trying Europeans — the betrayal that birthed organised nationalism: the Indian Association 1876, the National Conference 1883, the Congress 1885 — 1857's defeat as nationalism's tutor); finance (the war's cost (~£40 million) loaded onto Indian revenues — income tax 1860 (Wilson), the public-debt balloon, the Home Charges swollen — Indians taxed for the war fought against them). The consequences' synthesis: Crown prestige atop, martial-race army inside, princely breakwater around, racial distance throughout, and the bill presented to India — the post-1857 order was counter-insurgency frozen into constitution, stable for sixty years precisely because it had learnt 1857's lessons better than the rebels' heirs had — until the heirs organised.

Trap watch. Barrackpore (Mangal Pandey, 34th NI, 29 March 1857) precedes Meerut (10 May) precedes Delhi proclaimed (12 May, Zafar) — the founding triple in order. Greased cartridges (Enfield) are the trigger, never the cause — causes run Doctrine of Lapse (Satara 1848 → Jhansi 1853 → Nagpur 1854) + Awadh annexation (Feb 1856, the biggest grievance) + settlements-and-ruin + socio-religious interference + military grievances (General Service 1856, batta loss, subedar ceiling, 85% Indian Bengal Army). Awadh's 21,000-of-23,000 estates lost is the material base of the popular revolt. Centres-leaders-suppressors: Delhi (Zafar–Nicholson), Cawnpore (Nana/Tantia–Campbell), Lucknow (Hazrat Mahal–Lawrence/Campbell), Jhansi–Gwalior (Lakshmibai–Rose), Bareilly (Khan Bahadur–Campbell), Arrah (Kunwar Singh–Eyre). Savarkar (1909, banned) claims the First War; Majumdar (mutiny-into-revolt) and Sen (not-national-but-not-reactionary) qualify; subalterns (Guha, Stokes) recover peasant autonomy — stage all five, synthesise, never merely verdict. Failure: geography (loyal Punjab–presidencies–south), no common leadership, intelligentsia opposed, feudal particularism, telegraph-and-resources, Crimean-diverted troops. Consequences: 1858 Act (Crown, Secretary of State, Viceroy) + Queen's Proclamation (1 Nov 1858, Allahabad) + Peel army ratios + martial races + princely sunnuds + divide-and-rule + racial state → Congress 1885 as 1857's organised heir.

Revision Digest

Condensed for the last mile before the exam.

Trigger & spread

  • Immediate spark: greased cartridges of the Enfield P-53 rifle; Mangal Pandey, 34th BNI, Barrackpore, 29 March 1857.
  • Outbreak 10 May 1857 Meerut (3rd Light Cavalry) → Delhi 11 May → Bahadur Shah Zafar proclaimed emperor.
  • Not a mutiny alone: peasants, taluqdars of Awadh, artisans; ~half of Bengal Army's sepoys were from Awadh/NWP.

  • Zafar tried and exiled to Rangoon (died 1862); Nana Saheb disappeared to Nepal; Tantia Tope betrayed by Man Singh, executed April 1859.

Who stayed aloof

  • Scindia (Gwalior), Holkar, Nizam, Rajput rulers, Punjab (Sikh rulers), Nepal (Jung Bahadur helped British), most of South, educated middle class, Bengal Army's Bombay/Madras counterparts largely loyal.

Causes checklist

  • Political: Doctrine of Lapse, annexation of Awadh 1856, disrespect to Mughal (Canning's 1856 decision that Zafar's successor would leave the Red Fort).
  • Economic: heavy revenue, ruin of handicrafts, Inam Commission (Bombay, 20,000 estates confiscated).
  • Socio-religious: Sati abolition 1829, Widow Remarriage 1856, Religious Disabilities Act 1850, missionary activity.
  • Military: General Service Enlistment Act 1856 (overseas service), foreign-service allowance (bhatta) cut, low pay, promotions blocked.

Aftermath — institutional redesign

  • Government of India Act 1858 — Company abolished; Secretary of State for India (Stanley first) with 15-member India Council; Governor-General became Viceroy (Canning).
  • Queen's Proclamation 1 Nov 1858 (Allahabad) — "Magna Carta of Indian people": no more annexation, religious neutrality, equal treatment in services.
  • Army reorganisation (Peel Commission): European:Indian ratio 1:2 Bengal, 1:3 Bombay/Madras; artillery in European hands; "martial races" doctrine.
  • Indian Councils Act 1861 — Indians nominated to Legislative Council (portfolio system, Canning).

Interpretations (names only, for match questions)

  • V.D. Savarkar — "First War of Independence" (1909). R.C. Majumdar — "neither first, nor national, nor a war of independence". S.N. Sen — began as fight for religion, ended as war of independence. Benjamin Disraeli — "national rising". Eric Stokes — peasant/agrarian dimension. Lawrence & Seeley — sepoy mutiny.

Traps

  • Nana Saheb's cause: pension denied by Dalhousie (he was Baji Rao II's adopted son).
  • Rani Lakshmibai died at Kotah-ki-Serai near Gwalior, not at Jhansi.
  • The Revolt was suppressed by mid-1858; Tantia Tope captured 1859.
