GS Paper II — Practice System

Governance, constitution, polity, social justice and international relations — converting the pyqs/ question bank into marks.

1. What GS-2 Demands

GS-2 is the judgment paper: it asks you to take positions on constitutional design ("critically examine"), institutional performance ("assess"), and diplomacy ("discuss the significance"). It rewards authority-citation (Articles, cases, committees, Acts, indices) more than any other paper — every answer should carry at least one named anchor.

2. The Authority Bank (build before practising)

  • Constitutional: Articles (14, 21, 32, 226, 243, 356, 368…), landmark cases (Kesavananda, Maneka, Puttaswamy, SR Bommai, Vishaka).
  • Institutional: CAG, ECI, UPSC, Finance Commission, NHRC, NCW — one line each on mandate + one recent controversy.
  • Committees/Commissions: Punchhi, Sarkaria, ARC (2nd), Rajamannar — for federalism and governance answers.
  • Acts/policies: RTI, RPA 1951, DPDP 2023, NFSA, MGNREGA — with year.
  • Indices/data: World Governance Indicators, press-freedom index, NFHS-5, ASER — for social-justice answers.
  • IR anchors: neighbourhood-first doctrine lines, BIMSTEC/SCO/QUAD mandates, UN reform positions, treaty names.

3. Answer Anatomy for GS-2

  • Intro (2-3 lines): constitutional/statutory context — "Article X provides…" or case citation.
  • Body: mandate
  • performance (both records)
  • issues (structural, political)
  • comparisons (committees, best practices, other democracies).
  • Conclusion: reform direction with a committee recommendation or a balanced way-forward. GS-2 conclusions may propose — this is the proposing paper.

4. Judgment Phrases That Signal Maturity

  • "The provision is constitutionally sound; its implementation suffers from…" (separates design from delivery).
  • "Three objections are usually raised; the first and third survive scrutiny…"
  • "The committee's remedy addresses the symptom; the deeper leak is…"

5. The Weekly Cycle

  • Mon: polity/constitution (2 answers)
  • Tue: governance & social justice (2)
  • Wed: IR (2)
  • Thu: comparison-of-democracies + RPA/election answers (2)
  • Fri: full GS-2 sectional (10 Q)
  • Sat: evaluation + authority-bank update
  • Sun: rewrite two weakest.

6. Rubric /10

CriterionMarks
Position & decoding — stance or assessment visible early2
Authority — Article/case/committee/index correctly deployed3
Balanced analysis — both records, structural causes3
Proposed reform — specific, feasible, sourced2

7. Pointers

  • IR answers: pair bilateral substance with structural context (region, doctrine, economics); avoid news-recap tone.
  • Social justice: institutional mechanism + beneficiary outcome + one datum, every time.
  • Never moralise where you can analyse; never analyse where the question says "suggest."

8. The Directive Verb Is Half the Question

GS-2 questions are almost always two-part in substance even when they look single: a subject clause and a directive. Candidates who lose marks systematically have usually answered the subject and ignored the directive, because the directive is what determines how much of the paper is analysis and how much is description. The reason this matters more in GS-2 than in any other paper is that GS-2 is the only General Studies paper in which the syllabus itself asks for judgment — "critically examine", "assess", "evaluate" — and in which an answer that merely describes a correct institution cannot score above the middle band.

A directive is an instruction about the shape of the argument. Discuss asks you to set out the competing positions and arrive at a view; it is the most permissive verb and the one most candidates fail by turning it into a list. Examine asks you to look closely at the provision or institution and state what it does and does not achieve. Critically examine adds the obligation to test a proposition: state the case for it, the case against it, and which survives — and, crucially, to say which survives. Analyse means break the subject into its parts and show how they relate, so a causal chain is expected. Critically analyse means do that and then judge the parts. Evaluate asks for a verdict against stated criteria — performance against mandate, means against ends, cost against benefit. Comment requires your own position on a given statement, usually in two or three moves. Elucidate and explain demand clarity, not criticism, and are the traps: candidates who "critically examine" an elucidation question waste a third of their word limit on objections that earn nothing. Substantiate and illustrate are evidence verbs — they want authorities and instances, and the more the better. Assess is evaluative but quantitative in temper: it wants the record in both directions, ideally with one datum.

Two mechanical rules follow from this. First, when a question carries a second clause — "…Also suggest reforms" or "…with special reference to federalism" — the word limit is a budget for both clauses, and the marks are usually split in proportion to the clause length. A 15-mark question asking you to critically examine a doctrine and suggest a way forward is, in effect, a 9-mark analysis and a 6-mark reform section, and candidates who spend 220 of 250 words on the analysis forfeit the second half. Second, GS-2's verbs behave differently from the same verbs in GS-1 and GS-3 even though they are spelled identically: a "critical examination" in GS-1 of a historical process is a weighing of interpretations, in GS-3 it is a weighing of evidence and data, and in GS-2 it is a weighing of design against delivery. That distinction — is the defect in the constitutional scheme or in the institution's functioning? — is the single analytical move that separates a good GS-2 answer from an average one.

9. Four Question Families and the Skeletons That Score

Nearly every GS-2 question since 2013 falls into one of four families, and each family has a skeleton stable enough to be learned in advance. Learning them is what allows a candidate to begin writing within ninety seconds of reading the question.

  • The first family is constitutional design: basic structure, federalism, separation of powers, the conflict between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles, judicial review, constitutional amendment. The skeleton is provision
  • doctrinal evolution in two or three named cases
  • the structural tension
  • a normative judgment
  • reform anchored in a commission. The recurring error is writing history instead of argument — a chronicle of Kesavananda (1973), Minerva Mills (1980) and the NJAC judgment (2015) that never says whether the doctrine has strengthened or weakened parliamentary democracy, which is what the question asked.

The second family is institutional performance: the Governor, the Election Commission, the UPSC, the CAG, the NHRC, tribunals, Parliament's productivity, judicial pendency, the anti-defection law. The skeleton is mandate → record in both directions → structural cause → comparison → reform with a source. The structural cause is the step most often skipped and most heavily rewarded: an institution's failure is rarely about individual integrity and nearly always about appointment rules, tenure security, jurisdiction, resources or the absence of an enforcement mechanism. The Governor's discretionary space, for example, is a problem of an unspecified time-limit in Article 200 and an unenforceable convention, not of particular governors — which is why the Supreme Court's April 2025 judgment in the Tamil Nadu reference introduced the idea of deemed assent, and why the November 2025 advisory opinion declined to lay down fixed timelines while refusing to legitimise a pocket veto.

  • The third family is governance delivery and social justice: RTI and its exemptions, the DPDP Act 2023 and the privacy–transparency tension, welfare delivery and DBT, health and education outcomes, caste and reservation, women, children, tribal rights. The skeleton is mechanism
  • beneficiary outcome
  • one datum
  • bottleneck
  • fix. This is the family where a single number does disproportionate work, because it converts a normative claim into an assessed one: NFHS-5 (2019–21) reporting a total fertility rate of 2.0 and, for the first time, 1,020 women per 1,000 men
  • ASER on learning levels
  • SECC 2011 on deprivation
  • NITI Aayog's Multidimensional Poverty Index
  • NCRB on crimes against Scheduled Castes and women. The bottleneck is likewise structural — last-mile capacity, vacancy rates, data quality, judicial delay, the absence of a social audit — and naming it is what distinguishes an assessment from a complaint.

The fourth family is international relations, and it is the one where tone does the most damage. The skeleton is issue → bilateral substance → structural context → regional or global frame → India's interest stated explicitly. "Structural context" means geography, trade, defence cooperation, energy, water, connectivity and diaspora — the variables that persist regardless of which government is in office. The regional frame is where the doctrine vocabulary belongs: Neighbourhood First (2014), Act East (2014), SAGAR (2015), the Panchamrit commitments and the LiFE mission (2021), the International Solar Alliance (2015) and the Coalition for Disaster Resilient Infrastructure (2019) as Indian-initiated multilateralism, the Global Biofuels Alliance (2023), IMEC (2023). The economic frame is a small list of instruments with dates — the India–UAE CEPA (2022), the India–Australia ECTA (2022), the India–EFTA TEPA signed in March 2024, the India–UK FTA concluded in 2025 — plus the African Union's admission to the G20 during India's 2023 presidency. An IR answer built on this vocabulary reads as analysis; the same answer built on last week's headlines reads as recap, and examiners mark the difference.

  • A fifth, smaller demand deserves its own card file because the syllabus names it: comparison of the Indian constitutional scheme with other major democracies. The productive pairs are few and reusable — the Rajya Sabha against the US Senate (population-based versus equal state representation, both six-year, only one confirming appointments)
  • the collegium against the UK's Judicial Appointments Commission created by the Constitutional Reform Act 2005
  • India's basic structure against Germany's eternity clause in Article 79(3)
  • the absence of any Indian equivalent to Canada's notwithstanding clause
  • the UK Supreme Court's 2019 prorogation judgment (Miller II) against Article 85
  • the French Constitutional Council's prior review against Indian judicial review
  • Israel's 2023 reasonableness amendment and its January 2024 invalidation against India's own amendment litigation
  • and South Africa's socio-economic rights jurisprudence against the National Food Security Act 2013.

10. The Authority Bank as a Machine, and the Answer Log That Measures It

The authority bank described in Section 2 only becomes useful when it is built as a retrieval machine rather than a collection. Give every entry six fields and no more: the anchor (the Article, case, committee, Act or datum); its type; the exact form (Article 226, not "the writ jurisdiction"; S.R. Bommai, 1994, not "the federalism case"); the year; a one-line use stating what the anchor proves; and the question family it serves. The one-line use is the discipline that separates learning from hoarding: an anchor you cannot deploy inside a sentence is not yet usable, and the bank should be pruned rather than grown. Four hundred well-worn entries beat two thousand collected ones.

Sources should be ranked, and the ranking is itself a scoring strategy. At the top sit the Constitution and the bare Acts, because a misquoted Article is more damaging than a missing one. Next come Supreme Court judgments, then commission and committee reports — Sarkaria (1988) and Punchhi (2010) for Centre–State relations, the Rajamannar Committee (1971) for Tamil Nadu's dissent, the Second ARC's twelve reports, the Law Commission's 170th (1999) and 255th (2015) on electoral reform, the Dinesh Goswami (1990) and Indrajit Gupta (1998) committees, the Kovind Committee on simultaneous elections, and the Sixteenth Finance Commission under Arvind Panagariya, which retained the 41 per cent vertical devolution share for 2025–30. Then Standing Committee reports, then official statistics, then reputable secondary sources (PRS India for legislative tracking, the MEA's press briefings and treaty texts for IR, EPW for argument), and only last the newspaper. The order matters because the authority you cite tells the examiner where your preparation lives.

The answer log is the instrument that turns practice into improvement, and it should be maintained in parallel with the bank. Eight fields suffice: the question, the directive, the family, the skeleton used, the anchors deployed, a self-assessed mark out of 10, the dominant defect, and the fix. The last two are the point of the exercise. A log read after twenty answers produces a diagnosis with statistical weight — most candidates discover that they are not short of knowledge but of position, and that their recurring defect is a missing structural cause or an unnamed committee. Rewriting the week's weakest answer, which Section 5 already prescribes, is the only practice activity with a measured effect on score; drafting new answers mostly rehearses existing habits.

Finally, use the previous decade's papers as a frequency map rather than as a question bank to be "finished". Cluster the last ten years of GS-2 questions into the four families and count. Federalism and the Governor, local government and the 73rd and 74th Amendments, the Representation of the People Act and electoral reform, basic structure and judicial appointments, right to privacy and data protection, welfare delivery and social audit, the neighbourhood and the Indian Ocean recur with enough regularity to tell you which anchors to learn first. Preparation that follows the frequency map looks efficient to an examiner even when it is modest; preparation that follows a textbook looks thorough and scores less.

11. Marking Psychology and the Failure Modes That Cost Two Marks Each

An evaluator handles a few hundred scripts in a few weeks and spends on the order of six to eight minutes on each answer. That fact has design consequences which are not cosmetic. Structure is a scoring variable: the examiner is reading for whether the answer addressed the directive, whether a named authority was correctly deployed, and whether a position was taken — all three of which should be visible in the first four lines. Legibility, adherence to the word limit within roughly ten per cent, underlining of the anchor, short paragraphs each with one idea, and a two-line conclusion are not presentation flourishes; they are the conditions under which those three judgments can be made quickly. An answer that requires re-reading is scored from memory of its opening.

The failure modes, in descending order of damage, are consistent across cohorts. Absence of position is the first: a correct, well-sourced description that never says whether the doctrine strengthened democracy or whether the institution performed its mandate cannot exceed the middle band in a paper that asks for judgment. Generic authority is the second: "several committees have recommended" or "the Supreme Court has held" costs about as much as saying nothing, because the mark is awarded for the specific anchor. Misquotation is the third and the most punishing — writing Article 356 where the question concerns Article 365, attributing basic structure to Golaknath, or misstating the holding in Puttaswamy — because it signals that the candidate is guessing at the language of the paper. News recap in IR is fourth, and it is diagnosed by a simple test: if the answer would read the same way in six months, it is analysis; if it would not, it is journalism. Moralising where analysis is asked, and analysing where "suggest" is asked, is fifth, and it is a directive-reading failure. Unallocated word limits in two-part questions is sixth.

Calibration follows from the same logic. The gap between a 4 and a 6 out of 10 is not additional content; it is a stated judgment, one correctly named anchor, and a structural rather than personal cause. The gap between a 6 and an 8 is comparison (a committee, another democracy, or a best practice) and reform specificity — a recommendation with a source, an implementing agency and a mechanism. Above 8 the answer additionally holds its own nuance: it states the limiting condition under which its judgment would change. Practise against that ladder, not against a word count.

Trap watch. "Critically examine" requires a verdict, not a parallel list of pros and cons; "elucidate" requires clarity and forbids gratuitous criticism; "comment" requires your own position on a given statement; "assess" wants the record in both directions, ideally with a datum. Article 32 is the Supreme Court's writ jurisdiction and Article 226 the High Courts'; Article 136 is special leave to appeal; Article 142 is complete justice; Article 143 is advisory jurisdiction; Article 200 is the Governor's assent and Article 201 reservation to the President; Article 356 is President's Rule and Article 365 the sanction for it. Sarkaria reported in 1988 and Punchhi in 2010 — both Centre–State, but with different terms of reference, and Rajamannar (1971) was Tamil Nadu's own commission. NFHS-5 covers 2019–21 and reports a total fertility rate of 2.0 with 1,020 women per 1,000 men; NFHS-4 is 2015–16; the decennial Census has not been conducted since 2011, so most population arguments rest on projections. Kesavananda Bharati (1973) established the basic structure doctrine, Minerva Mills (1980) applied it against the 42nd Amendment, S.R. Bommai (1994) applied it to Article 356 and to secularism, and the NJAC judgment (2015) applied it to judicial appointments — the four are routinely conflated. The 106th Amendment (2023) provides one-third reservation for women but takes effect only after a census and a fresh delimitation; the 105th (2021) restored the States' power to identify SEBCs; the 104th (2020) extended SC and ST seat reservation and ended Anglo-Indian nomination; the 102nd (2018) gave the NCBC constitutional status. The electoral bonds scheme was struck down in February 2024 in Association for Democratic Reforms on disclosure grounds, not because donations were unlawful. Sub-classification within Scheduled Castes was permitted by State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh (2024), overruling E.V. Chinnaiah (2004). The India–UK FTA was concluded in 2025, the India–EFTA TEPA signed in 2024 and the India–UAE CEPA in 2022; the African Union joined the G20 in September 2023. India's notice on the Indus Waters Treaty was served in January 2023 under Article XII(3) — a notice to modify, not a denunciation.
