GS Paper IV — Practice System

Theory + case studies — the practice protocol that turns the pyqs/ bank (theory Qs and cases with model approaches) into 110+.

1. The Two Genres, One Clock

GS-4's Part A (theory — thirteen 10-mark units, 130 marks) and Part B (six case studies of 20 marks, 120 marks) share three hours. Budget: theory 5-6 minutes each; case studies 16-18 minutes each. Theory answers are short essays; case answers are decision memos. Practising them identically is the most common preparation error.

2. Theory Answers — the Compact Essay

  • Define the core concept in line one (quote a thinker only if exact).
  • Two-to-three analytical dimensions with one micro-example each (administrative, never trivia).
  • A counter-nuance or boundary condition ("but empathy without regulation becomes capture…").
  • Close with officer-relevance in one line. 150-200 words; never pad.

3. Case Answers — the Decision Memo

Use the 7-step pipeline (see case-studies detailed notes): facts → dilemma → stakeholders → options (≥3) → evaluation → decision + implementation → safeguards. Two timing internals: 3 minutes to annotate the case paper (underline actors, verbs, constraints), and the final 2 minutes reserved for safeguards — the most-skipped, most-rewarded band.

4. The Example Armoury

  • Rule + situation pairs: conduct rules ↔ gift cases; RTI ↔ transparency dilemmas; disaster manuals ↔ triage cases.
  • Officer stories (public-domain): Seshan's neutrality, Sreedharan's delivery ethos, forest-officer empathy cases — one line each, never cinematic.
  • Concept hooks: Gandhi's talisman, Rawls' difference principle, Kautilyan audit — mapped to question triggers.

5. Weekly Cycle

  • Mon: 2 theory answers · Tue: 1 case (full memo) · Wed: 2 theory · Thu: 1 case · Fri: sectional (3 theory + 2 cases, 100 min) · Sat: peer-evaluate against rubrics · Sun: rewrite worst answer; update example armoury.

6. Rubrics

Theory /10Case /20
Definition clarity — 2Structure (all 7 steps visible) — 4
Analytical depth + nuance — 4Stakeholder & option honesty — 4
Example fit — 2Evaluation rigour (tests applied) — 4
Officer-relevance close — 2Decision + implementation detail — 5

7. Pointers

  • Write decisions in first person ("I will…") — the paper tests you, not the government.
  • Legal floor first, compassion on top: orders lawful, humane, and recorded.
  • Never solve cases with resignation, leaks or vigilantism as first moves; exhaust the ladder.

8. The Paper as It Actually Stands: Structure, Genres and the Arithmetic of Time

The first correction most candidates need is to the shape of the paper rather than to their content. GS-4 carries 250 marks in three hours, and those marks have been distributed the same way for a decade: Section A holds 130 marks in thirteen 10-mark units and Section B holds 120 marks in six case studies of 20 marks each. What changes is the packaging of Section A — in 2024 the thirteen units appeared as thirteen separate questions, while in 2026 the same thirteen units were distributed as sub-parts across six questions — but the arithmetic the candidate must respect is identical. Section B is six cases, not four, and that single fact determines the entire time plan.

The plan is worth writing out because it is the most useful preparation fact in this lesson. Three hours is 180 minutes. Reading and annotating the question paper takes 5 to 6; a closing buffer for review, sequencing errors and one over-long answer takes 5 to 6. Six case studies at 16 minutes each consume 96. That leaves roughly 72 to 74 minutes for thirteen theory units — five and a half minutes per unit, or about 150 words written at speed. Any candidate who writes 250 words for a 10-mark theory question has borrowed three minutes from a case study, and case studies are where the marks are denser and the competition thinner. The converse error is equally common: candidates who enjoy the theory section arrive at case five and six with eight minutes each and produce the two weakest answers of the paper. Time discipline, not ethics knowledge, is the binding constraint on GS-4 scores.

Section A itself divides into a small number of genres, each with a different demand and therefore a different skeleton. Learning the genre map is what allows a candidate to recognise, within ten seconds of reading, which of five pre-rehearsed structures to deploy.

Two habits follow. First, the syllabus's own list of qualities — integrity, impartiality, non-partisanship, objectivity, dedication to public service, empathy, tolerance and compassion — should be prepared as thirteen pre-written cores of about eighty words each, so that any question built on them can be answered from a stored stem and extended with a fresh example. Second, quote questions should never begin with the biography of the speaker. Examiners do not reward attribution trivia; they reward what you do with the idea, and the first line should be the unpacking.

9. How Case Studies Are Actually Scored

A 20-mark case study is not an essay with a story attached; it is a decision memorandum assessed against a rubric that rewards visible structure. Six elements carry the marks: the facts restated with discrimination (which facts are legally relevant and which are noise); the ethical issues named rather than felt; the stakeholders mapped, including those with no voice in the narrative; the options honestly evaluated, with at least three and with the attractive-but-wrong option explicitly rejected on stated grounds; the decision taken in the first person with implementation detail; and the safeguards — the mechanism that stops the problem recurring. Candidates routinely write four of the six and lose the last two, which are the cheapest marks in the paper because almost nobody writes them.

What separates the top band is not sophistication but administrative realism. The paper tests whether you can act inside a system: every move must be lawful, feasible with the resources you actually have, and recorded. That produces the escalation ladder, which should be applied in order — verify the facts; counsel informally and privately; issue a written instruction with a deadline; invoke an existing statutory mechanism (a vigilance enquiry, a referral to the Central Vigilance Commission or the State Lokayukta, a complaint to the police where an offence is disclosed, a social audit under the MGNREGA Act's Section 17, the grievance machinery under CPGRAMS); record the decision in writing and inform the superior; then institutionalise a safeguard. An answer that begins with resignation, a leak to the media or a personal act of charity has abandoned the ladder and, in the examiner's reading, has failed the test of whether the candidate can hold the post. An answer that ends by referring everything upward, without a written record, fails it differently.

The mechanics of sub-questions are where marks are lost silently. A 20-mark case usually carries three parts whose marks are printed — commonly 5+5+10, 6+6+8 or 4+6+10 — and the parts are assessed separately. Answer them in the order asked, label each part, and allocate words in proportion to marks; an unlabelled answer forces the examiner to search for the parts and rewards nothing that must be searched for. Two devices recur and both test discrimination rather than knowledge: the "additional information" twist introduced in the third part, which is there to see whether your earlier decision survives a new fact, and the "who is most/least responsible" ranking, which must be preceded by the criterion you are ranking on. When a case asks you to justify a choice among beneficiaries, the criterion is where the marks are — a Rawlsian worst-off test, a rule-based eligibility test or a capability test — and a ranking without a stated criterion is arbitrary however humane it sounds.

10. Building the Ethics Input System: Thinkers, Instruments and Examples

GS-4 knowledge falls on three shelves, and candidates who study only the first shelf plateau at the same score every attempt. The first is normative frameworks: deontology (Kant's imperative and the rule that persons are ends, not means), consequentialism (Bentham's calculus, Mill's qualitative revision and his harm principle), virtue ethics (Aristotle's golden mean and phronesis, practical wisdom in the exercise of discretion), justice as fairness (Rawls' veil of ignorance and the difference principle), the capability approach (Sen's distinction between niti as institutional correctness and nyaya as realised justice, and Nussbaum's list of capabilities), discourse ethics (Habermas), the ethics of care, and Max Weber's distinction between the ethic of conviction and the ethic of responsibility — the most useful single idea in the paper, because it names the exact tension an officer feels between holding a principle and answering for consequences. Bernard Williams' integrity objection to utilitarianism (the Jim-and-the-Indians problem) is the standard criticism to deploy against pure consequentialist reasoning in triage and allocation cases.

The second shelf is Indian sources, which the syllabus names explicitly and which most candidates under-use. The Buddha's karuna and the Kalama Sutta's insistence on tested inquiry; Mahavira's anekantavada, which supplies the intellectual warrant for balanced multi-perspective analysis; the Gita's nishkama karma and sthitaprajna for detachment and equanimity; Kautilya's Arthashastra, whose observation that a revenue officer cannot handle money without tasting some of it, "as honey on the tongue cannot be tasted without swallowing some", is the oldest argument in Indian administrative thought for audit, rotation and detection rather than for exhortation; Vivekananda's daridra narayana and his idea of man-making education; Aurobindo's integral ethics; Gandhi's talisman and his doctrine of trusteeship, and the seven social sins; Tagore's humanism; Vinoba Bhave's sarvodaya; and Ambedkar's constitutional morality, drawn from his 4 November 1948 speech to the Constituent Assembly, which is the single most examinable Indian idea in the paper because it names the conflict between public morality as prevailing sentiment and morality as constitutional commitment.

The third shelf is instruments, and this is where GS-4 overlaps with governance and is most often blank. The CCS (Conduct) Rules 1964 — Rule 3 on integrity, Rule 9 on criticism of government, Rule 13 on gifts — and the All India Services (Conduct) Rules 1968; the Prevention of Corruption Act 1988 as amended in 2018, which criminalised the giving of bribes and narrowed the definition of criminal misconduct; the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act 2013, with the first Lokpal appointed only in 2019; the Whistle Blowers Protection Act 2014, which has not been brought into force — a fact worth stating explicitly in an answer, since citing it as a working remedy is a visible error; the RTI Act 2005, including Section 4(1)(b) on suo motu disclosure and Section 8's exemptions; the DPDP Act 2023, whose exemptions for State functions sit uneasily with RTI; the procurement discipline in the General Financial Rules 2017 and on the GeM portal; the Central Vigilance Commission, created in 1964 on the Santhanam Committee's recommendation and made statutory by the CVC Act 2003, supervising the CBI (which is itself not statutory but a creature of the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act 1946) since the Vineet Narain judgment of 1997; the Nolan Committee's seven principles of public life; the Second ARC's Fourth Report, Ethics in Governance; the citizens' charter initiative of 1997 and its Sevottam refinement; and, for private-sector ethics, Section 135 of the Companies Act 2013 with its thresholds and the two-per-cent-of-profits rule.

The example armoury is the fourth element and it has a strict form: rule + situation + outcome, drawn from the public domain, stated in one line. "Conduct Rule 13 caps the value of a gift a servant may accept, so the officer who declines a sponsor's watch is not being rude but being lawful." Cinematic detail and unverifiable anecdotes cost marks because they read as invention; a named rule with a named consequence reads as competence.

11. Draft, Diagnose, Rewrite: the Practice Cycle That Moves the Score

Practice should be phased rather than uniform, because the three phases train different defects. In the first six weeks write untimed, one genre per session, with the framework shelves open; the object is to produce correct structure, and speed at this stage only entrenches bad habits. From week seven to week sixteen write timed units — five and a half minutes for a 10-marker, sixteen for a case — and maintain the answer log; every Sunday, rewrite the week's worst answer rather than drafting a new one. In the final eight weeks write full papers on alternate weeks, with two papers back-to-back to reproduce fatigue, and spend the intervening days only on rewriting and on log review.

Diagnosis must be typed, because the four defect classes have different remedies and misdiagnosis is why practice plateaus. A structural defect — the safeguards missing, the parts unlabelled, the decision absent — is fixed by a checklist applied before writing. A knowledge defect — no framework or no instrument available for the situation — is fixed by adding a shelf entry, not by writing more answers. A judgment defect — a decision that is unlawful, infeasible, or that outsources the problem — is fixed by reading decisions of officers in the public domain and by testing each option against three questions: could I sign this order, could I defend it before a court or an audit, and could I implement it with the resources I have. A presentation defect — over-length, illegibility, unallocated word limits — is fixed by timed practice and by writing in short paragraphs with one idea each.

Peer review works only with a protocol. Exchange answers with the Section 6 rubric in hand and instruct the reviewer to hunt for three specific absences: the missing safeguards, the unnamed authority (a rule, a section, a committee, a case), and the absent first person. Reviewers who comment on style are useless; reviewers who count structure are not. The same armoury then carries into the personality test, which is why the Detailed Application Form should be cross-read against your ethics examples before the interview: an answer in the interview that contradicts a position taken in the paper is the one failure mode no amount of writing practice can repair afterwards.

Trap watch. Section B is six case studies of 20 marks (120 marks), not four, and Section A is thirteen 10-mark units totalling 130 — the arithmetic, not the content, is what most candidates get wrong. Writing 250 words for a 150-word theory question is not rewarded; the word limit is a time signal. Resignation, media leaks and vigilantism are not administrative solutions, and neither is referring everything upward without a written record: the escalation ladder must be exhausted and documented. The Whistle Blowers Protection Act 2014 has never been brought into force, so citing it as a working remedy is a factual error examiners notice; the Lokpal was appointed only in 2019 though the Act is of 2013. Empathy is not sympathy and neither is compassion; integrity is not honesty; probity is procedural integrity that survives an audit. Gandhi's talisman is a decision test, not a value in itself, and should be used once per paper at most. Weber's distinction between the ethic of conviction and the ethic of responsibility comes from Politics as a Vocation (1919); Kohlberg's stages concern moral reasoning, while the ABC model — affect, behaviour, cognition — concerns attitude structure, and the two are habitually conflated. Mayer and Salovey coined "emotional intelligence" in 1990 and Goleman's five components are the exam-friendly version; Thorndike's "social intelligence" dates to 1920. The CVC is statutory under the CVC Act 2003 but was created in 1964 on the Santhanam Committee's recommendation, while the CBI is not statutory at all — it derives from the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act 1946 and came under CVC supervision through Vineet Narain (1997). Finally, in a case study, answer in the first person and state who does what, by when, under which authority.
