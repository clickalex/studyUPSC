Constitutional Development & Government of India Acts (1773–1947) — Detailed Notes

Syllabus slot: GS 1 (Mains) · Modern Indian History · Polity & Constitution Also relevant for: Prelims GS Paper I — History & Indian Culture; GS Paper II — Polity (historical background)

1. Why Study This?

The 174 years between Plassey (1757) and Independence (1947) produced a continuous line of constitutional reworking by the British that became the institutional scaffolding of independent India. Many features of the Constitution of India (1950) — federal division of powers, the office of Governor, the Public Service Commission, the Supreme Court's structural ancestry, the three legislative lists, the pattern of parliamentary procedure — were inherited, adapted or consciously rejected from the Government of India Acts of 1919 and 1935.

2. Company Period Regulations (1773–1858)

2.1. 1 Regulating Act, 1773

  • First deliberate parliamentary intervention in the Company's affairs.
  • Elevated the Governor of Bengal to Governor-General of Bengal — Warren Hastings (1773–85); empowered a Council of Four to control him.
  • Established a Supreme Court at Calcutta (1774) — Sir Elijah Impey the first Chief Justice — with British subjects within its jurisdiction but ambiguous reach over Indian subjects (the Nand Kumar affair (1775) revealed its political use).
  • Required Company Directors to lay correspondence before the Crown; subordinated the Governors of Bombay and Madras to Bengal in time of war.
  • Weakness: no clear separation of functions; the Governor-General could be overruled by his Council; Court jurisdiction ambiguous.

2.2. 2 Pitt's India Act, 1784

  • Distinguished the Company's commercial from political functions.
  • Created a London Board of Control (crown agency) alongside the Court of Directors — "dual control."
  • Governor-General's Council reduced to three; given greater superintending power over Bombay and Madras.
  • Effect: the crown now supervised the Indian empire; the Company's territorial holdings in the east were legally subjected to the British Crown.

2.3. 3 Charter Acts (1793–1853)

ActKey provisions
Charter Act 1793Renewed the Company's charter for 20 years; empowered the Governor-General to override the Council in exceptional cases
Charter Act 1813 (under Lord Liverpool)Ended the Company's trade monopoly in India (except tea trade with China); asserted the Crown's sovereignty over British Indian territories; directed ₹ 1 lakh annually for education — the first official step toward Western education in India
Charter Act 1833 (the climax of the reforms)Ended the Company as a commercial body (it became purely administrative); made the Governor-General of Bengal the Governor-General of India (first: Lord William Bentinck); Indian laws to be codified via a Law Commission (first under Macaulay 1834 — drafted the IPC)
Charter Act 1853Separated legislative and executive functions of the Governor-General's Council — for the first time a legislative wing distinct from the executive; introduced open competition for the civil services (the "London office" agreement); authorised recruitment to the ICS by examination (first Indian admitted — Satyendranath Tagore, 1864)

3. Crown Takes Over (1858–1909)

3.1. 1 Government of India Act, 1858

  • Abolished Company rule; the Crown governed through a Secretary of State for India in London (a member of the British Cabinet) aided by a 15-member Council of India; and through the Viceroy (formerly Governor-General) in India — first Viceroy: Lord Canning.
  • Lord Canning read the Queen's Proclamation (1 November 1858, Allahabad) — promised non-interference in religion, ended the Doctrine of Lapse, opened public service to Indians "of whatever race or creed" (the promise was hollow in substance — ICS examinations continued only in London).
  • Indian Councils Act 1861 — restored legislative powers to Bombay and Madras
  • introduced a small legislative element into the Viceroy's Council — non-official Indians (the Maharaja of Patiala, Raja of Banaras 1862) nominated for the first time
  • began the institutional lineage of Indian legislatures
  • the "portfolio system" institutionalised.
  • Subsequent Councils Acts — 1892 (indirect election through recommended members) further enlarged the councils.

3.2. 2 The Moderates' foothold — Indian Councils Act 1892

  • Increased the number of non-official members in the central and provincial councils.
  • Introduced the principle (though not the word) of election — some members were nominated on recommendation by municipal bodies, chambers of commerce, zamindars, universities.
  • First opportunity for the educated Indian middle class to debate the budget and ask supplementary questions — the platform from which Naoroji, Gokhale and Mehta made their parliamentary critique.

4. Minto–Morley Reforms (Government of India Act 1909)

Often called the "Morley–Minto Reforms" — Lord Morley (Secretary of State) and Lord Minto (Viceroy).

Key provisions

  1. Enlarged the legislative councils — Central Legislative Council up to 60 members; provincial councils enlarged up to 50 (larger presidencies). Majorities of non-officials in the provinces, but not in the centre.
  2. **First recognition of the right to *vote*** — a narrow, property- and class-based franchise; communal electorate machinery begins.
  3. Separate electorates for Muslims — the infamous Muslim League demand of 1906 (Simla Deputation) incorporated; Muslims voted separately in Muslim constituencies — the communal poison that eventually led to Pakistan; the "mother of separate electorates."
  4. Preserved for the centre a majority of official members — the executive remained unaccountable; the new legislatures could discuss but not control.

Evaluation

  • The reforms conceded the form of parliamentary democracy without its substance — representation without power — and they legally confirmed the separate political existence of Muslim India.

5. Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms (Government of India Act 1919)

Background

  • WWI's rhetoric of self-determination; Edwin Montagu's August Declaration (20 August 1917) — "progressive realisation of responsible government in India as an integral part of the British Empire" — the definitive policy statement.
  • Congress–League Locknow Pact pressure (1916); Home Rule agitation; the Montagu–Chelmsford Report (1918).

Key provisions

  1. Dyarchy in the provinces — provincial subjects divided into "Reserved" (finance, law & order — administered by the Governor with his Executive Council) and "Transferred" (education, agriculture, health — administered by the Governor with ministers responsible to the legislative council). The classic "two-in-one" arrangement that collapsed in practice.
  2. Extended franchise — property, tax and education qualifications — approximately one-third of the adult population.
  3. Legislative Council at the Centre — bicameral: Council of State and Legislative Assembly; majority of elected members in the Assembly.
  4. Three of the six members of the Viceroy's Executive Council had to be Indian.
  5. New offices — Public Service Commission (set up 1926); a separate budget for India; separation of provincial budgets from the central budget.
  6. Statutory Commission after ten years — delivered as the Simon Commission (1927–30).

Why it failed

  • Dyarchy frustrated ministers of Transferred subjects — they had responsibility without adequate finance or autonomy.
  • The Governor's casting vote, special responsibilities and capacity to certify legislation made mockery of accountability.
  • The Separate Electorates expanded (Moplahs and Sikhs in Punjab) — deepened communal segmentation.
  • Rowlatt Act, Jallianwala Bagh and Non-Cooperation overtook the reforms before they could work.

6. Government of India Act 1935 — The Last Colonial Blueprint

Background

  • Simon Commission (1928), Nehru Report (1928), three Round Table Conferences (1930–32), Ramsay MacDonald's Communal Award (1932) and the joint committee's White Paper (1933).

Key provisions (memorise the architecture)

  1. All-India Federation — provinces and princely states in a federation with a Federal Court; never came into force because the princes never fulfilled the accession thresholds.
  2. Provincial autonomy — dyarchy transferred from provinces to the centre; provincial ministers fully responsible to elected legislatures; acted on after the elections of 1937.
  3. Three lists — Federal, Provincial, Concurrent; residuary powers with the Governor-General.
  4. Dyarchy at the centre (defence, external affairs, ecclesiastical affairs reserved; the rest transferred) — never operative because the federation never formed.
  5. Federal Court (1937) and Reserve Bank of India (1935) created.
  6. New provinces — Sindh (1936) separated from Bombay
  7. Orissa (1936) separated from Bihar–and-Orissa
  8. Burma separated from India (1937)
  9. Aden made a Crown colony (previously a residency of Bombay).
  10. Expanded franchise — to approximately one-sixth of the adult population.
  11. Public Service Commissions — federal and provincial.
  12. The 13 "safeguards" and special responsibilities of the Governor-General and the Governors — Nehru called the Act "a new charter of slavery."

What independent India borrowed

The Constituent Assembly's advisers decided early that the 1935 Act furnished a convenient working base; roughly 250 provisions were taken with modifications. Major inheritances:

  • The three-list system — Seventh Schedule.
  • The office of Governor and the Governor's discretionary powers pattern.
  • The Emergency provisions (Articles 352–360) in broad structural outline.
  • Public Service Commissions (Articles 315–323).
  • The supreme-court structural outline (Articles 124–147) as the successor of the Federal Court.
  • Administrative relations between the Union and the States (Articles 256–263).

Historical irony (mark the point): India rejected colonial rule but conserved much of the colonial constitutional blueprint because it was the working blueprint available in 1947 — and because Congress ministries had already governed under its "provincial autonomy" section in 1937–39.

7. The Final Acts (1946–47)

Cabinet Mission Plan (June 1946)

  • Rejected a sovereign Pakistan and a unitary India equally; proposed a Constituent Assembly elected by the new provincial legislatures; a three-tier Union (common subjects: foreign affairs, defence, communications).
  • The breakdown of the Plan led to Direct Action Day (16 August 1946), the Interim Government (September 1946) and the logic of partition.

Indian Independence Act, 1947

  • Received royal assent 18 July 1947; created two independent dominions — India and Pakistan from 15 August 1947.
  • Each dominion's Constituent Assembly given full sovereign authority to frame its constitution; the British Parliament's suzerainty over princely states lapsed — princes became technically sovereign until accession.
  • Governor-General shared by both dominions until their constituent assemblies chose otherwise; Mountbatten for India, Jinnah for Pakistan.
  • The offices of Secretary of State and the Council of India abolished; British troops to be withdrawn.
  • The GOI Act 1935 continued as the operative constitutional law of each dominion until its constituent assembly produced a new constitution.

10. The three through-lines — centralisation, representation, and communalism

  • The trunk's twelve turning points (1773–1947) read as a chronicle
  • the Mains upgrade is to read them as three pendulums swinging simultaneously. Pendulum one — centralisation vs devolution. The Regulating Act (1773) subordinated Bombay/Madras to Bengal only in war
  • Pitt's (1784) dual control centralised London oversight
  • the 1833 Charter made Bentinck the first Governor-General of India with all-India legislative power (centralisation's peak)
  • 1861 restored legislative powers to Bombay/Madras
  • 1919 separated provincial budgets and devolved transferred subjects
  • 1935 granted provincial autonomy while reserving the centre — and 1950 froze the settlement as the Seventh Schedule with residuary powers at the Union. The line: every devolutionary Act was a confession that the previous centralisation had become ungovernable. Pendulum two — representation without power. 1861 nominated Indians (Patiala, Banaras 1862)
  • 1892 smuggled in indirect election and budget debate (Naoroji–Gokhale–Mehta's platform)
  • 1909 enlarged councils but kept an official majority at the centre
  • 1919 gave elected majorities and dyarchy with finance reserved
  • 1935 gave provincial responsibility hedged by 13 safeguards — Nehru's "new charter of slavery." The system's formula never changed, only its camouflage: concede the form of legislatures, retain the substance of the executive.

Pendulum three — from united electorate to communal fragmentation. 1909's separate Muslim electorates (Simla Deputation 1906, the "mother of separate electorates") → 1919's extension (Sikhs in Punjab and others) → Communal Award (1932) → the partition logic of 1946–47. The Mains point is structural, not moral: separate electorates made religious identity the currency of representation, so politicians invested in religious identity — the electorate manufactured the communalism it claimed to reflect. Pair this with the August Declaration (20 August 1917 — "progressive realisation of responsible government") to show the contradiction at the heart of late-colonial constitutionalism: responsibility promised to a polity the franchise was simultaneously fragmenting.

11. The 1935-to-1950 ledger — what was borrowed and what was refused

The trunk lists the 1935 inheritances (~250 provisions: three lists → Seventh Schedule; Governor's office and discretionary pattern; Emergency outline → Arts 352–360; PSCs → Arts 315–323; Federal Court 1937 → Supreme Court Arts 124–147; Union–State administrative relations → Arts 256–263) and names the irony — Congress ministries had governed under provincial autonomy in 1937–39, so the blueprint was familiar. The Mains task is to argue the ledger both ways. The borrowing case: a partitioned subcontinent needed a working state on 15 August 1947, and the 1935 Act — which continued as each dominion's operative constitution under the Independence Act (royal assent 18 July 1947) — was the only tested machinery; federalism, an all-India judiciary, and professional bureaucracy could not be invented between 1946 and 1950. The refusal case is equally constitutional: universal adult franchise replaced the one-sixth franchise; fundamental rights replaced safeguards against Indians with rights of Indians; responsible government at the centre replaced dyarchy-that-never-was (defence/external/ecclesiastical reserved, federation stillborn as princes never met accession thresholds); and separate electorates were buried (the Assembly's communal decision being reservation's time-bound, joint-electorate opposite).

Two 1935 features deserve argued treatment because PYQs revisit them. Provincial autonomy (acted 1937): Congress ministries proved Indians could govern — agrarian relief, education expansion, civil-liberties releases — yet resigned in 1939 over the war issue, teaching the Assembly both the viability and the fragility of responsible government under a paramount executive; hence 1950's insistence on a fully responsible Union executive. The safeguards regime (Governor-General's and Governors' special responsibilities): the Assembly kept the instruments (emergency powers, Governor's discretion) while inverting their accountability — from an appointed Viceroy answerable to Whitehall to elected executives answerable to legislatures. Final ledger line: 1950 borrowed 1935's skeleton and transplanted a democratic heart — the continuities are administrative, the ruptures are political, and confusing the two is the standard wrong answer.

12. Answer frames — compare, explain, and judge

Three master-frames cover nearly every question this lesson generates. Frame A — compare 1909/1919/1935 on four axes: (i) franchise (narrow property → ~one-third → ~one-sixth of adults, note the 1935 contraction relative to rhetoric); (ii) legislative power (discuss-but-not-control → elected Assembly majorities with certified legislation → provincial responsibility with safeguards); (iii) executive accountability (unaccountable → dyarchy's responsibility-without-finance → autonomy-with-override); (iv) communal architecture (Muslim separate electorates → extended → Award-entrenched). The verdict sentence: 1909 communalised representation, 1919 provincialised it, 1935 federalised it — each Act solved the previous Act's crisis by enlarging the arena of Indian participation while fortifying the citadel of British power. Frame B — why dyarchy failed (1919 provinces; 1935 centre stillborn): transferred ministers held education/agriculture/health while finance stayed reserved — responsibility without resources; Governors wielded casting votes, certification, and special responsibilities; Rowlatt–Jallianwala–Non-Cooperation destroyed the political oxygen before the machinery could prove itself.

Frame C — the final-acts judgment (1946–47). The Cabinet Mission Plan (June 1946) rejected both sovereign Pakistan and unitary India for a three-tier Union (foreign affairs/defence/communications common) with a Constituent Assembly elected by provincial legislatures; its breakdown → Direct Action Day (16 August 1946) → Interim Government (September 1946) → the Independence Act's two dominions, lapsed paramountcy (princes technically sovereign till accession), Mountbatten/Jinnah as Governors-General, and abolition of the Secretary of State and Council of India. The judgment line: the Mission failed not on constitutional design but on the trust deficit separate electorates had compounded since 1909 — by 1946 no procedural ingenuity could substitute for a shared electorate that had never been allowed to exist. Cite Austin's Cornerstone for the borrowing thesis and Bandyopadhyay for the communal-electoral mechanism.
