Making of the Constitution and Salient Features — Detailed Notes

Dates, committees, sources, and features. Prelims favourites: 26 Nov vs 26 Jan, Drafting Committee members, borrowed features map.

1. Constituent Assembly

  • Cabinet Mission scheme: provincial 292 + princely-state 93 + chief-commissioners 4 = 389 seats (reduced to 299 post-partition). Indirectly elected by provincial assemblies (1946); Muslim League boycotts until 1947.
  • Officers: Sachchidananda Sinha (temporary chairman, 9 Dec 1946), Rajendra Prasad (President, 11 Dec 1946), H.C. Mukherjee vice-president, B.N. Rau constitutional adviser.
  • Objectives Resolution moved 13 Dec 1946 (Nehru), adopted 22 Jan 1947 — seed of the Preamble.
  • Drafting Committee (29 Aug 1947, 7 members): Ambedkar (chair), N. Gopalaswamy Ayyangar, Alladi Krishnaswamy Ayyar, K.M. Munshi, Syed Mohammad Saadullah, N. Madhava Rao (replaced B.L. Mitter), T.T. Krishnamachari (replaced D.P. Khaitan).
  • Timing: 2 years 11 months 18 days; 11 sessions; ~₹64 lakh cost; first session 9 Dec 1946; adopted 26 Nov 1949 (Constitution Day / Samvidhan Divas); some provisions (citizenship, elections, provisional parliament) effective then; fully in force 26 Jan 1950 (date chosen to honour Purna Swaraj Day 1930). Last CA session 24 Jan 1950 (sang national anthem, elected Prasad President).

3. Salient features

  • Lengthiest written constitution; blend of rigidity-flexibility (Art 368: simple majority some parts, special majority most, special + ½ ratification federal bits); parliamentary form; fusion of executive-legislature; federal in form, unitary in spirit.
  • Integrated judiciary + single citizenship; universal adult franchise; secular state ('secular' worded in Preamble by 42nd); independent bodies (Election Commission, CAG, UPSC).
  • Preamble (Berubari: not part; Kesavananda: part of Constitution; still not enforceable); 42nd added Socialist-Secular-Integrity.
  • Critiques often quoted: 'paradise for lawyers' tag, K. Hanumanthaiah's 'borrowed bag of bricks' line, defended by K.M. Munshi's blend argument.

MCQ hooks

  • 26 Nov 1949 = adopted; 26 Jan 1950 = commenced; 24 Jan 1950 = last CA sitting.
  • Drafting Committee = 7 members, 29 Aug 1947; B.N. Rau = adviser (not committee member).
  • Objectives Resolution: moved 13 Dec 1946, adopted 22 Jan 1947.
  • DPSP = Ireland; FDs = USSR; concurrent list = Australia; procedure established by law = Japan.
  • CA originally 389 seats (292 provincial); after partition 299.

4. The Assembly as Politics: Representation, Partition, and the Legitimacy Debate

The Constituent Assembly's composition is the most contested fact about it, and the contest is examinable. Elected indirectly in 1946 by provincial assemblies themselves elected on a restricted franchise (roughly 28 per cent of adults, weighted by property, tax and education qualifications), the Assembly was — critics charged then and charge now — an elite body: overwhelmingly male, disproportionately lawyer-Brahmin-upper-caste, Congress-dominated (208 of 299 post-partition seats), with the Muslim League's boycott removing most Muslim representation until partition settled the question by amputation. The Cabinet Mission scheme had allocated 389 seats (292 provincial, 93 princely-state nominees, 4 chief commissioners' provinces); partition cut it to 299, and the princely-state seats were filled by nomination rather than election. Against this, the defenders' case — made most forcefully by Granville Austin — runs on acclamation and absorption: the Congress's mass-movement legitimacy transferred to its Assembly majority; dissenters were deliberately co-opted (Ambedkar, elected from Bengal with Scheduled Caste Federation backing and later Bombay, chairing the Drafting Committee; non-Congress experts like B.N. Rau as constitutional adviser, Alladi Krishnaswamy Ayyar and K.M. Munshi inside the tent); and the Assembly's proceedings — published, press-covered, amendment-moved and division-called — performed representative deliberation more genuinely than its electoral base promised.

The legitimacy debate's productive form is comparative: no post-colonial constitution of the era was made more democratically (Pakistan's assembly shared the same restricted origins; most African and Asian constitutions of the 1950s–60s were executive-drafted or colonially granted), and India's Assembly is nearly unique in debating itself into legitimacy — 11 sessions over 2 years, 11 months, 18 days, at a cost of about ₹64 lakh, with the Objectives Resolution (moved by Nehru, 13 December 1946; adopted 22 January 1947) functioning as the normative charter the Preamble would later condense. The key committees map the division of labour: the Union Powers and Union Constitution Committees (Nehru chairing both), the Provincial Constitution Committee (Patel), the Advisory Committee on Fundamental Rights, Minorities and Tribal Areas (Patel, with the J.J. Singh-led minorities sub-committee whose separate-electorates demand the Assembly famously rejected in favour of joint electorates plus reservation), and the Drafting Committee (29 August 1947 — Ambedkar chairing Ayyangar, Alladi, Munshi, Saadullah, Madhava Rao, T.T. Krishnamachari). Partition runs through the chronology as the structuring trauma: the Assembly met first on 9 December 1946 under temporary chairman Sachchidananda Sinha, elected Rajendra Prasad President on 11 December — and by August 1947 was simultaneously writing a constitution and managing the subcontinent's division, which is why the emergency provisions, preventive detention and the strong centre passed with far less resistance than the federalists had planned.

The dating triad that every MCQ probes — adopted 26 November 1949, commenced 26 January 1950, last sitting 24 January 1950 — encodes the politics: 26 January honoured the Purna Swaraj declaration of 1930 (the Congress's independence day since Lahore), while citizenship, elections and provisional-Parliament provisions took immediate effect on adoption so the Republic could function during the interregnum. The national anthem sung on 24 January, Prasad elected President the same day, the Constitution signed in two copies (Hindi by Prem Behari Narain Raizada's calligraphy, English illuminated by Nandalal Bose's Santiniketan artists) — the ceremonial close matters because it answers the legitimacy question performatively: a restricted-franchise body exiting through unanimous national ritual.

5. Borrowed or Built? The Sources Debate and the 1935 Skeleton

The \"borrowed bag of bricks\" charge (K. Hanumanthaiah in the Assembly debates) against the \"beautiful patchwork\" defence (K.M. Munshi's reply — the patchwork is Indian even if the cloth is imported) is the liveliest framing question in the lesson, and the evidence vindicates a precise middle: the skeleton is the Government of India Act, 1935; the soul is the freedom movement; the joints are comparative borrowings. The 1935 Act contributed the federal scheme, the Governor's office, the judiciary's structure, the public-service commissions and the emergency provisions — some 250 articles carried over in substance — which is why the Constitution reads administratively continuous with late-colonial governance: the framers, inheriting Partition's chaos, prioritised a state that could function on day one over one that theorised elegantly. The freedom movement contributed the Preamble's resolve (\"We, the People\" — popular sovereignty asserted against imperial grant), Fundamental Rights (the Karachi Resolution, 1931, as the direct ancestor), universal adult franchise (against every contemporary precedent — Britain needed centuries, America excluded Black voters in practice till 1965), and the Directive Principles' social-revolution mandate (the \"socialist\" pattern the Congress had promised since the 1930s).

The comparative borrowings then sort by function rather than by flag. Westminster mechanics came from Britain (parliamentary responsibility, cabinet government, bicameralism, single citizenship, rule of law, the Speaker's office, legislative procedure) — chosen, as Ambedkar argued, because Indians already knew the system (two decades of provincial legislatures under 1919 and 1935) and because parliamentary responsibility (daily accountability through questions and no-confidence) suited an illiterate-but-politicised electorate better than presidential stability. Rights architecture came from America (justiciable Fundamental Rights, judicial review, an independent Supreme Court, impeachment, the Vice-President as Rajya Sabha chairman) — but with the deliberate substitution of Japan's \"procedure established by law\" for America's \"due process,\" a choice Maneka Gandhi (1978) would judicially reverse. Directive idealism came from Ireland (DPSP, the nominated-Rajya Sabha device); federal centralism from Canada (\"Federation with a strong Centre,\" residuary powers with the Union, gubernatorial appointment, advisory jurisdiction); fiscal–legislative devices from Australia (Concurrent List, Article 301 trade freedom, joint sittings); amendment mechanics from South Africa (with the basic-structure limit added by Kesavananda, not borrowed from anywhere); emergency suspension from Weimar Germany; republican ideals from France; and duties plus Preamble-justice from the USSR (Fundamental Duties via the Swaran Singh Committee, 42nd Amendment, 1976). The synthesis for answers is Austin's: the framers operated a seamless web — each borrowing re-woven to Indian ends (joint electorates, asymmetric federalism, group-differentiated rights) until the garment was unmistakably indigenous.

The \"paradise for lawyers\" critique (Jennings's tag, gleefully quoted) completes the debate: the Constitution's length (395 articles, 8 schedules at commencement — the world's longest) reflects not verbosity but distrust converted into detail — administrative provisions (services, commissions, elections, official language), transitional settled-everything drafting, and rights-with-printed-exceptions, all designed so the Republic's operators would find answers in the text rather than in conventions they had never practised. Length was a strategy for a polity without shared constitutional habits.

6. Salient Features as Design Choices: Federalism, Secularism, and the Living Document

The salient features must be learned as choices with reasons, not as a list — because GS-2 examines the reasoning. Parliamentary federalism is the master choice: federal in form (dual polity, division of powers, written supremacy, independent judiciary) but unitary in spirit (single citizenship, integrated judiciary, All-India Services, emergency conversion, Parliament's Article 3 power to redraw State boundaries unilaterally, the Governor as Union agent). Wheare's \"quasi-federal\" label captures the tilt; the framers' reason was Partition — a centrifugal subcontinent needed a centre that could hold, and B.R. Ambedkar said so explicitly (\"the Draft Constitution is federal as much as it establishes a dual polity… but the Union is not a league\"). The tilt's later history — Sarkaria (1988) and Punchhi (2010) on Centre–State friction, the GST Council (101st Amendment, 2016) as fiscal-federal innovation, Article 131 suits multiplying in the 2020s — is the living commentary the feature demands.

Secularism is the second master choice, and India's version is distinctive enough to need its type-name: not Western wall-of-separation but principled distance (Bhargava's formulation) — the State keeps equal distance from all religions while intervening to reform them (temple-entry laws, the Hindu Code Bills, the 2019 triple-talaq legislation, the Uttarakhand UCC of 2025). The word \"secular\" entered the Preamble only via the 42nd Amendment (1976) alongside \"socialist\" and \"integrity,\" but the substance (Articles 14–16 equality, 25–28 religious freedom with reform latitude, 29–30 minority rights, 44's UCC directive, 51A(e)'s harmony duty) predates the word — which is why Bommai (1994) could hold secularism basic-structure without depending on the 42nd. Judicial supremacy within parliamentary sovereignty is the third: the framers rejected both British parliamentary absolutism and American-style supremacy controversies by writing review into the text (Articles 13, 32, 141, 226) while giving Parliament the last word through amendment — a balance Kesavananda then re-balanced toward the Court. The remaining features slot as corollaries: rigidity–flexibility blend (Article 368's three tracks — simple majority for federal-mutable matters like State formation, special majority for most, special-plus-ratification for entrenched federal provisions); universal franchise (Article 326 — the framers' bravest gamble, enfranchising 173 million adults, 85 per cent illiterate, at a stroke); and independent fourth-branch institutions (Election Commission, CAG, UPSC, Finance Commission — the guardians of the procedures everyone else operates).

The Preamble concentrates all of this into eighty-odd words, and its jurisprudence is the lesson's capstone. Berubari (1960) held it not part of the Constitution; Kesavananda (1973) reversed that (it is part, amendable within basic structure — the 42nd Amendment promptly testing that holding); enforceability remains nil (no writ lies on the Preamble alone), but interpretive weight is immense — \"We, the People,\" the Justice–Liberty–Equality–Fraternity quartet, and the 42nd Amendment's Socialist–Secular–Integrity insertions supply the Court's favourite lenses for reading ambiguous provisions. The living-document doctrine (Maneka, Puttaswamy, Navtej) is the Preamble's method extended to the whole text: the framers wrote abstract nouns precisely so future generations could pour new content into old vessels.

7. Amendment Mechanics and the Basic-Structure Settlement

Article 368's three tracks are prelims-mechanical but mains-consequential, because which track an amendment takes determines who can block it. The simple-majority track (technically outside 368 — ordinary legislative process) covers State formation and boundary alteration (Articles 2–4), citizenship details, quorum and privilege rules, Fifth–Sixth Schedule administration, and salary restructurings: Parliament alone decides, which is why the States Reorganisation map could be redrawn repeatedly without constitutional crisis. The special-majority track (majority of total membership plus two-thirds of those present and voting, in each House separately — no joint sitting under 368) covers Fundamental Rights, DPSP, and most institutional provisions: the Opposition's blocking power peaks here, which is why controversial amendments (42nd, 44th, 99th/NJAC) cluster in this track's political history. The special-plus-ratification track adds half the State legislatures' consent for entrenched federal matters (Presidential election, Union–State executive and legislative relations, the Lists, High Courts, Article 368 itself): the federal veto, used rarely but decisively (the GST's 101st Amendment clearing sixteen ratifications in weeks is the modern type-case).

The tracks' history is the basic-structure story in miniature. Shankari Prasad (1951) and Sajjan Singh (1965) held amendments outside Article 13's \"law\" (Parliament omnipotent); Golaknath (1967) reversed that (FRs unamendable); the 24th Amendment (1971) asserted plenary amending power (new clause to Article 13 and 368 — \"Parliament may amend any provision\"); and Kesavananda (1973), thirteen judges, 7:6, split the difference that survives: amend anything, destroy nothing basic — with the basic features identified case-by-case since (supremacy of the Constitution, republican–democratic form, secularism, separation of powers, judicial review, federalism, FR–DPSP harmony, free elections, dignity, and since 2015, judicial primacy in appointments). The 42nd Amendment's clauses 4–5 (barring review of amendments) died in Minerva Mills (1980) — limited amending power is itself basic. The current frontier is which additions qualify: the Court has declined to freeze the list deliberately, keeping basic structure an open canon — flexibility's revenge on rigidity, and the reason every major amendment now ships with litigation as standard equipment. A century-old text amended 106 times and counting, yet never replaced: that durability is the final salient feature.
