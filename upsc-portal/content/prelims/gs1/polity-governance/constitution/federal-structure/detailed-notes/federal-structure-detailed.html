Federal Structure and Centre-State Relations — Detailed Notes

Quasi-federal design: three lists, administrative compass, fiscal flows, emergency centralisation, plus commissions (Sarkaria, Punchhi).

1. Legislative relations (245-255)

  • Seventh Schedule: Union ~97 (defence, foreign, banking, currency, posts, insurance), State ~66 (police, public order, health, agriculture, land), Concurrent ~52 (criminal law, education, forests, marriage, electricity). Residuary → Parliament (248; Entry 97).
  • Parliament can legislate on State List when: RS 2/3 resolution (249), national emergency (250), states consent (252), treaties (253), President's rule (356).
  • 254 repugnancy: Union law prevails; state law with presidential assent prevails in that state (Parliament may override later).
  • 42nd moved five subjects to Concurrent (education, forests, weights-measures, wildlife protection, administration of justice).

2. Administrative relations (256-263)

  • 256-257 directions power (compliance; communications), 258 entrustment, 262 inter-state water disputes (River Waters Disputes Act 1956; tribunals: Krishna, Cauvery, Ravi-Beas), 263 Inter-State Council (Sarkaria recommendation, established 1990), Zonal Councils (States Reorganisation Act 1956 — statutory, 5 + NE), full faith and credit (261).
  • All-India Services (312): IAS, IPS, Indian Forest Service (only three; new one needs RS resolution).

3. Financial relations (268-293)

  • Levy-collection splits: 268 (stamp duties — states collect), 269 (inter-state trade taxes — pooled), 270 divisible pool (post-GST includes CGST share), 271 surcharges excluded from pool. 280 Finance Commission (quinquennial; chairman + 4 members; tax devolution, grants-in-aid 275, local-body fund measures). 282 discretionary grants. Borrowing: Union 292, states 293 (Centre's consent if indebted to Centre). GST Council (279A): FM chairs, states 2/3 vote weight, Centre 1/3, decisions need 3/4.

4. Emergency shifts & commissions

  • 352: Union legislative power over any list; 356 President's rule (Bommai 1994: judicial review, floor test, no permanent effects); 360 financial emergency never used.
  • Sarkaria Commission (1983-88): 247 recommendations — Art 356 as last resort, Inter-State Council, Governor's role guide; Punchhi (2007-10): Governor tenure-removal norms, 'localised emergency', cooperative federalism. Modern framing: cooperative-competitive federalism (NITI, GST era).

MCQ hooks

  • Residuary powers = Union (248) — Canada-style, not US/UK.
  • Art 249 needs RS 2/3 'national interest' resolution (1-year validity); emergency era = 250.
  • Inter-State Council = Art 263, Sarkaria idea, set up 1990.
  • Finance Commission = Art 280, every 5 years; surcharge (271) outside divisible pool.
  • GST Council: Centre 1/3, states 2/3; decisions 3/4 of weighted votes.
  • Only 3 All-India Services: IAS, IPS, IFoS.

6. Is India Federal? The Doctrinal Position

The Constitution nowhere uses the word "federation". Article 1 declares India a "Union of States" — language Dr Ambedkar explained deliberately: the Union is not the result of an agreement among states, and consequently no state has the right to secede. India is therefore an indestructible union of destructible states, in contrast to the United States, an indestructible union of indestructible states.

Federal features to cite: a written and supreme Constitution, a dual polity, division of powers through the Seventh Schedule, an independent judiciary with power of judicial review, bicameralism, and a rigid amending procedure for entrenched provisions. Unitary features are equally examinable: a strong Centre with the residuary power, single citizenship, an integrated judiciary, all-India services whose officers serve both levels, a single Election Commission, the emergency provisions that can convert the polity into a unitary one, Governors appointed by and holding office at the pleasure of the President, the Centre's power to alter state boundaries under Article 3 without state consent, and a common Comptroller and Auditor General.

The standard characterisations, each worth attributing: K. C. Wheare called it "quasi-federal", a unitary state with subsidiary federal features; Granville Austin coined "cooperative federalism"; Ivor Jennings described a "federation with a strong centralising tendency"; and the Supreme Court in S. R. Bommai (1994) held federalism to be part of the basic structure, observing that the states are not mere appendages of the Centre. In State of West Bengal v. Union of India the Court had earlier held that Indian federalism is not absolute.

7. The Distribution of Powers

Legislative relations occupy Articles 245–255. The Seventh Schedule divides subjects into the Union List (defence, foreign affairs, railways, banking, atomic energy, currency), the State List (public order, police, public health, agriculture, land, local government) and the Concurrent List (criminal law, marriage, education, forests, wildlife, economic and social planning). Residuary powers vest in Parliament under Article 248 — unlike the United States and Australia, where they rest with the states. In a Concurrent List conflict, Article 254 gives central law primacy; but a state law reserved for and receiving presidential assent prevails within that state, subject to Parliament's power to override it later.

Parliament may legislate on State List subjects in five situations, a guaranteed question:

  • Article 249 — the Rajya Sabha passes a resolution by two-thirds of members present and voting that a State List subject is of national interest; valid for one year, renewable.
  • Article 250 — during a National Emergency.
  • Article 252 — two or more states request it; the resulting law applies to those states and any others adopting it, and can be amended or repealed only by Parliament.
  • Article 253 — to implement international treaties and agreements.
  • Article 356 — during President's Rule in a state.

Interpretive doctrines: pith and substance (a law is judged by its true nature, and incidental encroachment on another list is permitted); colourable legislation (what cannot be done directly cannot be done indirectly); repugnancy under Article 254; territorial nexus; and the doctrine of harmonious construction among the lists.

Administrative relations (Articles 256–263) require states to exercise executive power so as to ensure compliance with central law; the Centre may issue directions to states, and failure to comply can trigger Article 356. Article 262 covers inter-state river water disputes and permits Parliament to bar the jurisdiction of courts including the Supreme Court. Article 263 empowers the President to establish an Inter-State Council, done in 1990 on the Sarkaria Commission's recommendation. Articles 301–307 guarantee freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse throughout India.

8. Fiscal Federalism

The financial division is inherently asymmetric: the Centre commands the more elastic and buoyant tax bases while the states carry the larger expenditure responsibilities in health, education, police and agriculture — the vertical fiscal imbalance that makes transfers structurally necessary.

Transfers flow through three channels. Finance Commission transfers under Article 280 — the constitutionally mandated, formula-based and untied route, comprising tax devolution under Article 270 and grants-in-aid under Article 275. Centrally Sponsored Schemes, funded under the discretionary grant power of Article 282. And loans under Article 293, where a state already indebted to the Centre needs its consent to borrow further.

Live points of friction, each usable in a mains answer:

  • Cesses and surcharges are retained wholly by the Centre and lie outside the divisible pool, so their rising share erodes states' effective entitlement even when the notified devolution percentage is unchanged.
  • The 15th Finance Commission's use of the 2011 census was resisted by southern states as penalising successful population control; the Commission responded with a demographic performance criterion.
  • GST subsumed most indirect taxes and thereby surrendered a large part of states' independent taxing autonomy in exchange for a share of a common pool, with decisions taken in the GST Council where the Centre holds one-third of weighted votes and any decision needs three-fourths — giving the Centre an effective veto but not the power to act alone. The expiry of the five-year compensation guarantee was a major grievance.
  • Conditionalities attached to CSS and to borrowing limits under the FRBM framework constrain state priorities.

9. Stress Points, Commissions and Asymmetric Federalism

Article 356 is the sharpest instrument. S. R. Bommai transformed its use by holding that the proclamation is subject to judicial review, that the floor of the House — not the Governor's subjective satisfaction — is the only test of majority, that the Assembly may be dissolved only after parliamentary approval, and that the Court may restore a dismissed government. The Governor's office remains contested: discretion in inviting a party to form government, in reserving bills for the President under Article 200, and, most recently, in indefinitely withholding assent — which the Supreme Court has held impermissible, requiring the Governor to act within a reasonable time.

Commissions to name: the Sarkaria Commission (1988) — recommended appointing Governors from outside the state and not active in recent politics, sparing use of Article 356 as a last resort, and activating Article 263; the Punchhi Commission (2010) — proposed a fixed five-year gubernatorial tenure removable only by impeachment-like process, "localised emergency" confined to a district or part of a state instead of dismissing an entire government, and a narrower treaty-making power where state subjects are affected; the Rajamannar Committee and the Anandpur Sahib Resolution represent earlier state-autonomy demands.

Asymmetric federalism is a distinctive Indian feature: the Fifth and Sixth Schedules for tribal areas, with Autonomous District Councils in the north-east; special provisions under Articles 371 to 371-J for Maharashtra, Gujarat, Nagaland, Assam, Manipur, Andhra, Sikkim, Mizoram, Arunachal, Goa and Karnataka; and the position of Union Territories, with Delhi governed by Article 239AA — where the Supreme Court held the Lieutenant Governor bound by the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers except on police, public order and land, a position subsequently altered by the GNCTD amendment legislation.

Conclude on the trajectory: India has moved from the "cooperative federalism" of shared schemes toward competitive federalism — ranking states on indices to spur reform — while critics argue the substance of transfers has become more conditional and centralised even as the vocabulary has become more consultative.

Trap watch. Residuary powers rest with Parliament, not the states. Article 3 lets Parliament alter state boundaries by simple majority without state consent — the state legislature is only consulted, and its view is not binding. Under Article 249 it is the Rajya Sabha, not the Lok Sabha, that passes the resolution. A law under Article 252 can be repealed only by Parliament. Cesses and surcharges are not shared with states. Federalism is basic structure per Bommai, but the states have no right to secede.
