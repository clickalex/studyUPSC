Local Government (73rd and 74th Amendments) — Detailed Notes

1992 acts (in force 24 Apr / 1 Jun 1993): constitutionalised panchayats + municipalities. Values, reservations, institutions — direct question zone.

1. Panchayats (Part IX, 243-243O)

  • Definitions: 243 panchayat vs district; 243A Gram Sabha powers state-determined; 243B three-tier (village-intermediate-district; intermediate optional below 20 lakh).
  • Elections: direct, adult franchise; 5-year term (fresh elections within 6 months of dissolution); disqualification; 243D reservations: SC-ST proportional; women ≥1/3 (incl. chairperson posts); state may rotate; OBC state option (many states 27-50%).
  • 243G powers: economic development + social justice, XI Schedule 29 subjects; 243H taxes-funds; 243-I State Finance Commission every 5y; 243K State Election Commission (SEC — removal like an HC judge; constitutionally protected autonomy).
  • History: Balwant Rai Mehta 1957 (3-tier, democratic decentralisation), Ashok Mehta 1977 (two-tier; Mandal panchayats), G.V.K. Rao 1985, L.M. Singhvi 1986 (constitutional status recommendation); Rajasthan Nagaur 2 Oct 1959 launch; Karnataka-AP-WB early acts → 73rd.

2. Municipalities (Part IX-A, 243P-243ZG)

  • Three types: nagar panchayat (transitional), municipal council (smaller urban), municipal corporation (larger urban); wards committees for >3 lakh population.
  • Reservations mirror 243D; term-finances similar; XII Schedule 18 functional items (urban planning, slums, water, sanitation, street lighting, poverty alleviation...); 243ZD District Planning Committee (consolidates rural-urban plans); 243ZE metropolitan planning committees (>10 lakh).
  • Exemptions: scheduled areas (PESA 1996 extends panchayats to Schedule V with gram-sabha primacy), Nagaland-Meghalaya-Mizoram exempt; Parts IX and IX-A are not automatically extended to Scheduled Areas (PESA covers the rural side).

3. Performance & schemes

  • Gaps: 3F devolution (funds-functions-functionaries) uneven; Devolution Index (NCAER-MoPR); SFC delays common; women's share of elected PRI representatives ≈ 46%; eGramSwaraj, SVAMITVA (property cards, drones), RGSA capacity scheme.
  • Urban: SBM-U, AMRUT, Smart Cities (100), PM-eBus, PM SVANidhi (street vendors); ULB own-revenue decline concerns.

MCQ hooks

  • 24 Apr 1993 (73rd in force) = National Panchayati Raj Day; 74th = 1 Jun 1993.
  • Numbers: XI Schedule 29 subjects, XII Schedule 18; ward committees >3 lakh; DPC 243ZD; MPC 243ZE (>10 lakh).
  • Women ≥1/3 in seats + chairs (state can raise; 50% in 20+ states).
  • SEC = state-constitutional officer; SFC every 5 years (243-I / 243Y).
  • PESA 1996 = Schedule V extension; Nagaland-Meghalaya-Mizoram exempt.
  • Committee lineage: Balwant Rai Mehta (3-tier) → Ashok Mehta (2-tier) → G.V.K. Rao → L.M. Singhvi (constitutional demand).

6. Evolution and the Constitutional Turn

Local self-government has deep roots — Lord Ripon's Resolution of 1882 earned him the title "father of local self-government in India" — but the modern story begins with the committees that diagnosed why community development failed.

  • Balwantrai Mehta Committee (1957) — recommended "democratic decentralisation" through a three-tier structure: Gram Panchayat at village level (directly elected), Panchayat Samiti at block level and Zila Parishad at district level (both indirectly constituted). Rajasthan was the first state to implement it, at Nagaur in 1959, followed by Andhra Pradesh.
  • Ashok Mehta Committee (1977) — proposed a two-tier system (Zila Parishad and Mandal Panchayat), open participation of political parties, and compulsory taxation powers.
  • G. V. K. Rao Committee (1985) — found that development administration had become "grass without roots" and recommended the district as the pivot, with a District Development Commissioner.
  • L. M. Singhvi Committee (1986) — the decisive one: recommended constitutional recognition for panchayati raj and the gram sabha as the embodiment of direct democracy.

The 64th Amendment Bill (Rajiv Gandhi, 1989) was defeated in the Rajya Sabha; the effort succeeded with the 73rd and 74th Amendments, enacted 1992 and effective from 24 April and 1 June 1993 respectively. Note that 24 April is observed as National Panchayati Raj Day.

7. The 73rd Amendment — Panchayati Raj

Added Part IX (Articles 243 to 243-O) and the Eleventh Schedule with 29 subjects. Distinguish carefully between compulsory and voluntary provisions — the single most tested aspect.

Compulsory: constitution of a Gram Sabha; a three-tier system in states with population above 20 lakh; direct election to all seats at all three levels; indirect election of chairpersons at intermediate and district levels; a five-year term with fresh elections completed before expiry, and within six months of a dissolution — with the important rider that a body reconstituted after premature dissolution serves only the remainder of the original term; reservation of seats for SCs and STs in proportion to population; one-third reservation for women, including within the SC/ST quota and among chairpersons; a minimum voting age of 18 and candidature age of 21; a State Election Commission; and a State Finance Commission every five years.

Voluntary: representation of MPs and MLAs in panchayats; reservation for backward classes; granting the panchayats the powers and authority to function as institutions of self-government; devolution of the Eleventh Schedule subjects; and authorising panchayats to levy, collect and appropriate taxes.

Two structural points matter. Article 243-O bars courts from interfering in electoral matters, including the delimitation of constituencies. And the State Election Commissioner is appointed by the Governor and may be removed only in the manner and on the grounds prescribed for a High Court judge — a genuine independence safeguard, though the SEC is a state, not a central, authority.

PESA (1996) — the Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act extends Part IX to Fifth Schedule areas with modifications, and is far more radical than the parent amendment: it makes the gram sabha competent to safeguard traditions, customs and community resources, mandates its consultation before land acquisition and resettlement, gives it ownership of minor forest produce, control over minor water bodies and minor minerals, the power to prevent land alienation and restore alienated tribal land, and control over local plans and institutions. Part IX does not automatically apply to Fifth and Sixth Schedule areas — a frequent trap.

8. The 74th Amendment — Urban Local Bodies

Added Part IXA (Articles 243P to 243ZG) and the Twelfth Schedule with 18 subjects. Three types of municipality, determined by the Governor on population, density, revenue, employment in non-agricultural activity and economic importance:

  • Nagar Panchayat — a transitional area moving from rural to urban.
  • Municipal Council — a smaller urban area.
  • Municipal Corporation — a larger urban area.

Besides these are non-constitutional bodies for special purposes: cantonment boards (governed by central legislation), township authorities of public enterprises, port trusts, notified area committees and special purpose agencies such as development authorities.

Two mandatory committees distinguish the urban amendment. The Ward Committee under Article 243S, compulsory in municipalities with a population of 3 lakh or more. The District Planning Committee under Article 243ZD, which consolidates the plans of panchayats and municipalities in the district, with at least four-fifths of its members elected from among the elected members of the district panchayat and municipalities in proportion to rural and urban population. The Metropolitan Planning Committee under Article 243ZE is required for metropolitan areas of 10 lakh or more, with at least two-thirds elected members. These two articles are frequently confused; fix the fraction with the body.

The Mayor is typically a ceremonial head with a short term in many states, while executive authority rests with the state-appointed Municipal Commissioner — the core reason Indian cities lack empowered leadership, and a favourite critique point alongside the absence of directly elected mayors with fixed tenure.

9. Finances, Performance and Reform

Local bodies suffer a severe fiscal deficit of autonomy. Their own revenue sources — property tax, profession tax, entertainment tax, user charges, tolls, and octroi where it survives — are narrow and poorly exploited; property tax collection efficiency in Indian cities is low by international comparison. Most income is therefore assigned or transferred, through State Finance Commission devolution, Central Finance Commission grants (which now flow directly to local bodies, with tied and untied components), and scheme funds.

The recognised weaknesses, best organised as the "three Fs": inadequate devolution of funds, functions and functionaries. Add to these: parallel bodies and para-statals that bypass elected councils; excessive state control through the power to supersede or dissolve; irregular State Finance Commission constitution and non-implementation of its recommendations; the phenomenon of "sarpanch pati", where elected women are proxies for male relatives; caste and elite capture; low capacity and training; and the two-child norm and educational-qualification conditions imposed by some states, which the Supreme Court upheld in Javed v. State of Haryana but which are criticised for excluding the marginalised.

Positive evidence for a balanced answer: reservation has produced a very large cohort of elected women representatives — more than in all other tiers combined — with research (Chattopadhyay and Duflo) showing that women pradhans invest more in drinking water and are judged more competent once voters have experienced them, and evidence that reservation reduces the prejudice it is accused of entrenching. The 106th Amendment extends the logic of one-third reservation to Parliament and state assemblies.

Reform directions: activity mapping to assign each Eleventh and Twelfth Schedule subject unambiguously to a tier; own-source revenue reform, especially GIS-based property tax; direct election of empowered mayors with five-year terms; strengthening gram sabha and ward committees as the real accountability forum; social audit; e-governance through eGramSwaraj and the SVAMITVA property-card programme; and capacity building through the Rashtriya Gram Swaraj Abhiyan.

Trap watch. The three-tier structure is mandatory only for states with population above 20 lakh. Reservation for backward classes is voluntary; for SC/ST and women it is compulsory. A panchayat reconstituted after premature dissolution serves only the remainder of the term. The minimum age to contest is 21. Part IX does not automatically extend to Fifth and Sixth Schedule areas — PESA does that for the Fifth. The DPC needs four-fifths elected members, the MPC two-thirds. The Eleventh Schedule has 29 subjects, the Twelfth has 18. State Election Commissioners are appointed by the Governor, not the ECI.
