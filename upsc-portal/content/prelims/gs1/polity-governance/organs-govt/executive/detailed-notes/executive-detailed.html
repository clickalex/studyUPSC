Executive: President, Vice-President, PM and Council of Ministers — Detailed Notes

Articles 52-78. Election methods, powers, pardons, PM-CoM mechanics — table-friendly.

1. President (52-62)

  • Election (54-55): electoral college = elected MPs + elected MLAs (NCT Delhi and Puducherry — UTs with legislatures — included); proportional representation single-transferable vote; value formulas (MLA value = pop(1971)/seats/1000). 50+ age, RS-qualified, no office-of-profit; oath administered by the CJI (Art 60; Art 76 is the Attorney-General). Impeachment (61): either house initiates, violation-of-Constitution charge, passed by 2/3 of the total membership of each House (not of those present and voting); nominated MPs participate in impeachment but not in the election, elected MLAs in the election but not in impeachment.
  • Powers: executive (appoints PM-CoM-governors-judges), legislative (summon-prorogue-dissolve; addresses 87; ordinances 123; assent options 111 — assent, withhold, return (non-money)), financial (money bills prior recommendation 117, contingency fund 267), judicial (Art 143 reference, pardons 72), diplomatic-military (53 supreme command), emergency (352-356-360).
  • Pardon (72): pardon, commutation, remission, respite, reprieve; covers court-martial + death; vs Governor 161 (no court-martial cases and no death-sentence pardons). Cases: Kehar Singh, Perarivalan (2022 SC under 161 remission).
  • Veto types: absolute (withhold), suspensive (return once; not available for money bills), pocket (inaction — no time limit is prescribed in India; Art 201 reservation for state bills).

2. Vice-President (63-71)

  • Elected by both houses (elected+nominated members — unique, includes RS nominated); no state role; ex-officio Chairman RS (64); removal: RS resolution (14-day notice, effective majority) + LS agreement — different from own-election. Emoluments as RS chairman.

3. PM & Council of Ministers (74-78)

  • 74: CoM aids-advises President; 42nd made advice binding, 44th allowed one reconsideration. 75: PM appointed by President (majority leader convention; sworn even without majority = caretaker), ministers on PM advice; total ≤15% LS (91st); collective responsibility 75(3) to LS. 78: PM's communication duties (decisions, agenda). Deputy PM = extra-constitutional; Art 88: AG may speak in either house (no vote) — ministers enjoy the same speak-right.
  • Cabinet vs CoM: cabinet = top tier (Art 352 cabinet meaning 44th); kitchen cabinet informal; PMO growth.

4. Governor snapshot (153-163)

  • Appointed (Art 155), 35+age; pleasure tenure (no fixed 5y); pardons 161; discretionary powers (356 report, hung-house, reserved bills 200-201); Bommai floor-test norms; recent friction: TN Governor bill-approval delays, SC timelines advice.

MCQ hooks

  • President's college = elected MPs + elected MLAs of Delhi-Puducherry (not nominated MPs, not other UTs).
  • Impeachment charge = 'violation of the Constitution' only.
  • VP election includes RS nominated members; removal starts in RS.
  • 91st: council ≤15% of LS (and ≤15% of state assemblies).
  • President 72 vs Governor 161: court-martial + death only President.
  • 42nd binding advice + 44th reconsideration once.

6. The Office in Practice: Aid, Advice and the Space Left for Discretion

Article 53 vests the executive power of the Union in the President, and Article 74 requires a Council of Ministers to aid and advise in its exercise. Those two propositions look contradictory until the amendment history is read: the advice was made binding by the Forty-second Amendment (1976), and the Forty-fourth Amendment (1978) added the President's power to require reconsideration once, after which the advice binds again. Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab (1974) settled the constitutional reading — the President and the Governor are constitutional heads, the real executive is the Council of Ministers, and wherever the Constitution speaks of the President's satisfaction it means satisfaction on aid and advice unless the text expressly provides otherwise. Article 74(2) then closes the judicial door: no court may inquire whether, and if so what, advice was tendered. The discipline on the executive is therefore political rather than legal — collective responsibility to the Lok Sabha under Article 75(3) — which is why a parliamentary system removes governments between elections and a presidential one cannot.

Discretion survives only in the gaps that advice cannot fill, and the Sarkaria and Punchhi Commissions mapped them. First, appointing a Prime Minister when no party has a majority: the prescription is a pre-poll alliance first, then the single largest party claiming support, then the largest post-poll coalition, with a floor test within a short, fixed period. Second, dissolution advice from a Chief Minister or Prime Minister who has lost the majority: Sarkaria's rule is that the House should not be dissolved if an alternative government can be formed, and a caretaker government should not take major decisions. Third, the one reconsideration the Forty-fourth Amendment allows. The historical record shows how narrow that space is in practice: Rajendra Prasad's differences with Nehru over the Hindu Code Bill and the Somnath temple reconstruction; K.R. Narayanan, the only President to return the Cabinet's Article 356 advice — for Uttar Pradesh in 1997 and Bihar in 1998; A.P.J. Abdul Kalam, who returned the Office of Profit Bill in 2006 and assented when it came back; and Zail Singh, whose inaction on the Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill of 1986 is the textbook pocket veto. Comparatively, the British monarch is bound by identical conventions without a written text (Bagehot's "right to be consulted, to encourage, to warn"), while the American President fuses head of state and head of government and owes no confidence to the legislature — the contrast that explains why Indian executive questions are almost always about advice rather than about power.

7. Pardons, Vetoes and Ordinances: the Three Contested Zones

The pardoning power under Article 72 comes in five forms — pardon, commutation, remission, respite and reprieve — and reaches three categories: court-martial sentences, offences under Union law, and death sentences. Article 161 gives the Governor the same power for offences under state law, minus court-martial cases, and minus the ability to pardon a death sentence (the Governor may suspend, remit or commute it). The jurisprudence is not about the width of the power but about the manner of its exercise: Maru Ram (1980) held it is exercised on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers and not personally; Kehar Singh (1988) held the order is not reviewable on merits but is reviewable if passed on irrelevant, arbitrary, mala fide or wholly extraneous grounds; Epuru Sudhakar (2006) restated that review; Shatrughan Chauhan (2014) held that inordinate delay in disposing of a mercy petition, and supervening mental illness, are grounds to commute death to life; and in the Perarivalan case (2022) the Court used Article 142 to release the convicts after the Tamil Nadu Cabinet's binding recommendation under Article 161 had lain undecided with the Governor for years — the principle being that a constitutional head cannot defeat the Council's advice by inaction. Bilkis Bano (2024) is the rare substantive intervention: the remission granted by Gujarat was set aside because the competent government was Maharashtra's, where the trial had concluded.

The ordinance power is the third contested zone. Article 123 requires that both Houses be out of session and that the President be satisfied of the necessity for immediate action; the power is co-extensive with Parliament's legislative competence and is subject to the same fundamental-rights limits. Two decisions define its edges: D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar (1986) condemned serial re-promulgation as a fraud on the Constitution, and Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar (2017) held that an ordinance is not immune from judicial review, that its consequences are not indefeasible once it lapses, and that re-promulgation without legislative approval is unconstitutional. The Governor's parallel power under Article 213 is narrower in one practical respect: the President's instructions are mandatory where the bill would have required prior presidential sanction, where the Governor would have been obliged to reserve it, or where the corresponding Act would have needed presidential assent to be valid.

8. The Governor and the Federal Faultline, 2023–2025

Every Governor dispute traces to a textual asymmetry. Article 74 says the President acts on aid and advice and Article 74(2) bars judicial inquiry; Article 163(1) says the Governor acts on aid and advice "except in so far as he is required by or under this Constitution to exercise his functions... in his discretion", and Article 163(2) makes the question whether a matter is discretionary final in the Governor's judgment. Add appointment by warrant under Article 155, tenure at the President's pleasure with no impeachment and no fixed term — B.P. Singhal (2010) holds that removal cannot be arbitrary but no reasons need be given — and the Sarkaria and Punchhi recommendations on fixed tenure and consultation with the Chief Minister remain unimplemented. The office is therefore strong in text and fragile in tenure, which is the structural reason it is used as an instrument of Centre–State politics.

The assent litigation runs in three steps. In State of Punjab v. Principal Secretary to the Governor (10 November 2023) the Court held that "as soon as possible" in the first proviso to Article 200 means every day is imperative, and that withholding assent is valid only if the bill is returned to the legislature with a message. In State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor of Tamil Nadu (8 April 2025) a Constitution Bench held that Article 200 offers only three options — assent, withholding by return, and reservation for the President — that there is no absolute or pocket veto, that the Governor must assent to a bill re-passed after reconsideration, that discretion is confined to bills derogating from the powers of the High Court and to those requiring presidential assent, and that acts of the Governor and President are justiciable because, on the S.R. Bommai logic, a legal question dressed as a political one remains reviewable. It prescribed timelines — one month with aid and advice, three months against advice, one month for re-presented bills, three months for the President under Article 201 — and, using Article 142, deemed ten pending bills assented on the date of their re-presentation, 18 November 2023, thereby voiding the President's later action on them. The sixteenth Presidential Reference under Article 143 followed in May 2025, and in November 2025 a five-judge bench delivered its advisory opinion: courts cannot impose timelines on the President or Governors, "deemed assent" is alien to the Constitution and cannot be manufactured under Article 142, the President's subjective satisfaction on reserved bills needs no reference to the Court, but indefinite inaction is nonetheless unconstitutional as a violation of federalism, of the legislature's will and of constitutional morality, and judicial review survives — limited to process and to mala fides, not to the merits of assent.

What survives all three stages is the operative rule for examinations: a Governor must act and cannot sit on bills; on re-presentation the choice narrows to assent or reservation; reasons must be recorded and communicated. Around this sit the wider faultlines — Article 356 as constrained by S.R. Bommai (1994), which made the floor test the only democratic proof of majority and presidential rule reviewable; President's Rule in Manipur imposed in February 2025 and extended in August 2025; the Delhi question, where the Constitution Bench judgment of May 2023 placed services with the elected government except public order, police and land, and Parliament's GNCTD (Amendment) Act 2023 responded by creating the National Capital Civil Service Authority under the Lieutenant Governor — a statute reversing a judicial allocation of power; the chancellor disputes, with states legislating Governors out of university appointments and the UGC's 2025 draft regulations pushing back; and the Vice-Presidency itself, where Jagdeep Dhankhar's resignation in July 2025 forced the first early Vice-Presidential election since 1987 and C.P. Radhakrishnan, then Governor of Maharashtra, was elected on 9 September 2025 as the fifteenth Vice-President, defeating Justice B. Sudershan Reddy by 452 votes to 300 on an electoral college of 781 members of both Houses — the only constitutional office in India elected by a college that includes nominated members.

9. The Council of Ministers, the PMO and the Machinery of Accountability

Article 75 makes the Prime Minister the hinge: the President appoints the Prime Minister and, on that advice, the other ministers; the Council is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha under clause (3), which is what converts a no-confidence motion into a motion against the entire government; ministers hold office during the President's pleasure under clause (2), and there is no legal responsibility in the British sense because no officer countersigns a minister's act; clause (5) requires a minister who is not a member of either House for six consecutive months to cease to be one, which is why Prime Ministers have served from the Rajya Sabha — Manmohan Singh throughout 2004–14 — and why a non-member appointment is always provisional. The Ninety-first Amendment capped the Council at fifteen per cent of Lok Sabha strength (and of state assemblies, with a floor of twelve), and the Forty-fourth Amendment inserted the word "Cabinet" into Article 352 so that emergency advice must come from the Cabinet rather than from the whole Council. Article 78 completes the loop in the other direction: the Prime Minister must communicate all decisions of the Council, furnish any information the President calls for, and place before the Council, if the President so requires, any matter on which a minister has decided but the Council has not considered — the textual basis of the President's residual right to be informed.

Beneath the constitutional layer sits the administrative machinery that actually runs the executive: the Allocation of Business Rules 1961 and the Transaction of Business Rules 1961, made under Article 77, which distribute subjects among ministries and prescribe how business is transacted; the Cabinet Secretariat headed by the Cabinet Secretary, who is also the senior-most civil servant; eight standing Cabinet committees — Appointments, Economic Affairs, Political Affairs, Security, Parliamentary Affairs, Accommodation, Investment and Growth, and Skill Development and Employment; the Prime Minister's Office, whose growth since the 1970s is the standard example of authority migrating from the formal Cabinet to an informal secretariat; the extra-constitutional "kitchen cabinet"; and the Deputy Prime Minister's office, which has no constitutional existence. Accountability runs through Question Hour, calling-attention notices, short-duration discussions, the no-confidence motion, the departmental standing committees, the Comptroller and Auditor General, and the Attorney General's right under Article 88 to speak in either House without voting — a right ministers share. The absence of any requirement that a minister resign when a department fails is the clearest indicator that individual responsibility in India is political convention, not law.

Trap watch. Article 53 vests executive power in the President, but Shamsher Singh (1974) makes the Council of Ministers the real executive; advice is binding after the Forty-second Amendment and the President may require reconsideration only once (Forty-fourth). Article 74(2) bars courts from inquiring into advice, while Article 163(2) makes the Governor's discretion final — that asymmetry, not any grant of power, generates every Governor dispute. Presidential impeachment under Article 61 requires a two-thirds majority of the total membership of each House, not of those present and voting; nominated members take part in impeachment but not in the election, and elected MLAs take part in the election but not in impeachment. The President's oath is administered by the Chief Justice under Article 60 — Article 76 is the Attorney-General; the Governor's oath is administered by the Chief Justice of the High Court. Article 72 covers court-martial sentences and death sentences, Article 161 does neither, though a Governor may suspend, remit or commute a death sentence; pardons are exercised on the Council's advice (Maru Ram, 1980) and are reviewable for mala fides (Kehar Singh, 1988; Epuru Sudhakar, 2006), with delay and mental illness as commutation grounds (Shatrughan Chauhan, 2014). A money bill cannot be returned; an ordinary bill can be returned once and re-passage obliges the President to assent. Zail Singh's pocket veto (1986 postal bill) and Kalam's suspensive veto (2006 Office of Profit Bill) are the two standing examples, and Narayanan is the only President to have returned Article 356 advice (Uttar Pradesh 1997, Bihar 1998). Re-promulgated ordinances are unconstitutional (D.C. Wadhwa 1986; Krishna Kumar Singh 2017) and the President's satisfaction is reviewable for mala fides. Cite the April 2025 Tamil Nadu judgment and the November 2025 advisory opinion together: the first imposed timelines and deemed assent, the second withdrew both while retaining the prohibition on indefinite inaction. The Vice-President is elected by members of both Houses including nominated members, is removed by a Rajya Sabha resolution carried by an effective majority plus Lok Sabha agreement, and cannot be a member of either House.
