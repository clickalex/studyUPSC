India — Bilateral Relations — Detailed Notes

Neighbours first, then major powers, West Asia, Indo-Pacific. Match partner → treaty/year/initiative.

1. Neighbours

  • Pakistan: 1947-48 war-Kashmir, Simla 1972 (bilateral LoC), Lahore 1999, post-Uri/Balakot (2019) freeze; Article 370 reorganisation 2019; trade suspended (2019).
  • China: Panchsheel 1954, 1962 war, 1993-96 agreements, Wuhan-Mamallapuram informal summits, 2020 Galwan clash + disengagement phases, trade deficit squeeze, Brahmaputra hydro-project watch.
  • Bangladesh: 1971 liberation partnership, Ganga treaty 1996, LBA 2015 (100th amendment), Teesta pending, Maitri bridge-ports, 2024 political transition watch.
  • Nepal: 1950 Peace-Friendship treaty; Kalapani-Lipulekh, EPG report, Mahakali treaty 1996, open border-rokars.
  • Bhutan: 1949 treaty revised 2007 (sovereignty clause), hydropower (Tala-Punatsangchhu), RuPay-Druk link.
  • Sri Lanka: civil war support, 1987 Indo-Lanka Accord + IPKF, 13A devolution, Trincomalee oil-farms, Adani terminal, fisherman issues, IMF support.
  • Maldives: Operation Cactus 1988, Greater Male connectivity, India-Out politics cycle, 2024 troop-replacement friction then reset.
  • Afghanistan: post-2021 humanitarian line (wheat, medicines); Myanmar: Act East + border FMR regime (2024 suspension), coup response.

2. Major powers

  • USA: 123 Agreement civil nuclear 2008, Defence frameworks (CISMOA-LSA-BECRA → COMCASA-BEA) + foundational agreements, 2+2 ministerials, iCET (2023 tech) + TRUST-era updates, GCC (Major Defence Partner 2016), trade pacts (2019 restores GSP status — GSP withdrawn 2019 actually; phrase: GSP withdrawal 2019), diaspora-remittance anchor.
  • Russia: 1971 treaty legacy, S-400 deliveries, crude-oil pivot 2022+, nuclear (Kudankulam), BrahMos co-production, annual summits; payment-mechanism friction.
  • UK: historical ties, Roadmap 2030, CETA signed 2025; France: Rafale, P75 Scorpene, reciprocal logistics agreement 2018; Japan: Mumbai-Ahmedabad HSR, CEPA 2011, Quad anchor.

3. West Asia & others

  • UAE: CEPA 2022, I2U2, rupee-dirham settlement
  • Saudi: Strategic Partnership Council 2019, Haj-Nitaqat
  • Israel: full ties 1992, I2U2 tech, defence legacy
  • Iran: Chabahar (2016 trilateral; 2024 10-year deal), INSTC
  • Qatar energy+workers
  • Oman Duqm access.
  • Indo-Pacific framing: SAGAR (2015) + MAHASAGAR (2025 update), Quad revival 2017-leaders 2021, IMEC (2023 G20), IORA, IONS maritime.

⚡ MCQ hooks

  • LBA 2015 = 100th amendment (Bangladesh enclaves swap).
  • Bhutan treaty revised 2007; Nepal treaty 1950 unchanged.
  • IPKF = 1987 accord consequence; 13A = provincial councils.
  • Operation Cactus = Maldives 1988; Operation Rahat/Yemen 2015 evac.
  • Chabahar ≠ Gwadar (Iran vs Pakistan-China).
  • Galwan = 2020; Panchsheel = 1954.
  • CEPA: UAE 2022, Australia 2022 (ECTA), Japan 2011, Singapore 2005.

4. The Doctrinal Frame of Indian Foreign Policy

Bilateral questions are better answered through a framework than as country lists. India's foreign policy rests on Panchsheel (mutual respect for sovereignty and territorial integrity, non-aggression, non-interference, equality and mutual benefit, peaceful coexistence — enunciated in the 1954 agreement with China), the tradition of non-alignment reformulated today as strategic autonomy and multi-alignment, and a set of operating doctrines: Neighbourhood First, Act East (upgraded from Look East in 2014), Link West, SAGAR (Security and Growth for All in the Region) for the maritime neighbourhood, and the Indo-Pacific Oceans Initiative. The "Gujral Doctrine" of non-reciprocal concessions to smaller neighbours remains a citable historical principle.

  • Recurring structural themes worth deploying in any bilateral answer: the China factor in South Asia and the debate over "debt-trap diplomacy"
  • connectivity as the new currency of influence
  • the diaspora as an instrument of soft power and a source of remittances (India is the world's largest recipient)
  • energy security
  • and India's balancing act between the West and Russia.

5. The Immediate Neighbourhood

Pakistan. Governed by the Simla Agreement (1972), which committed both to settling disputes bilaterally — the basis of India's rejection of third-party mediation on Kashmir — and converted the ceasefire line into the Line of Control. Earlier, the Tashkent Declaration (1966) followed the 1965 war and the Lahore Declaration (1999) preceded Kargil. The Indus Waters Treaty (1960), brokered by the World Bank, allocates the eastern rivers (Ravi, Beas, Sutlej) to India and the western rivers (Indus, Jhelum, Chenab) largely to Pakistan, with India permitted non-consumptive and limited run-of-river use; disputes over the Kishanganga and Ratle projects go to a Neutral Expert or Court of Arbitration. Cross-border terrorism remains the central obstacle; India revoked Pakistan's Most Favoured Nation status in 2019, and SAARC has been effectively frozen since 2016.

China. The boundary is undefined, with the Line of Actual Control in three sectors — Western (Aksai Chin, held by China), Middle, and Eastern (Arunachal Pradesh, claimed by China as "South Tibet", where the McMahon Line of the 1914 Simla Convention is India's basis and is rejected by China). Confidence-building agreements of 1993, 1996, 2005 and 2013 were strained by Doklam (2017) — a tri-junction standoff involving Bhutanese territory — and the Galwan clash of 2020, after which India's position is that relations cannot be normal while the border is not. Other frictions: a very large trade deficit, the CPEC passing through Pakistan-occupied Kashmir (India's sovereignty objection to the Belt and Road Initiative), China's blocking of India's NSG membership and of terrorist designations at the UN, and the Brahmaputra upstream dam question, managed only through limited hydrological data-sharing MoUs since there is no water treaty.

Bangladesh — the most successful recent relationship. The Land Boundary Agreement (2015), implemented through the 100th Constitutional Amendment, exchanged enclaves and settled a long-standing anomaly; the maritime boundary was settled by UNCLOS arbitration in 2014, which India accepted despite an adverse outcome — a strong illustration of rule-based conduct. The Ganga Water Treaty (1996) on Farakka runs to 2026; the Teesta agreement remains pending because of West Bengal's objection, illustrating the role of federal units in foreign policy. Connectivity through Akhaura-Agartala, Maitri Setu and inland waterways is transforming north-east access.

Nepal — the Treaty of Peace and Friendship (1950) provides an open border and national treatment, but Nepal seeks its revision as unequal. Frictions: the Kalapani-Lipulekh-Limpiyadhura territorial dispute and Nepal's revised map; the 2015 constitution and the Madhesi question; and the "blockade" perception. Cooperation: hydropower and power trade, the Motihari-Amlekhgunj petroleum pipeline, and the recruitment of Gorkha soldiers.

Sri Lanka — the Katchatheevu island and the recurring arrest of fishermen in the Palk Bay; the 13th Amendment to Sri Lanka's constitution, arising from the 1987 Indo-Lanka Accord, on devolution to the Tamil-majority provinces; India's $4 billion assistance during the 2022 economic crisis and its role in the IMF debt-restructuring assurances; and the Hambantota port lease to China as the exemplar of the strategic contest.

Bhutan — the closest partner, with the 1949 treaty revised in 2007 to remove the clause requiring Bhutan to be "guided by" India in foreign policy, thereby removing the protectorate character. Hydropower cooperation and the Doklam episode define the relationship. Maldives — the "India First" versus "India Out" oscillation, Operation Cactus (1988), and the Greater Malé Connectivity Project. Myanmar — the Kaladan Multimodal Transit Transport Project and the India-Myanmar-Thailand Trilateral Highway, complicated by the coup, refugee inflows and insurgent sanctuaries. Afghanistan — the Salma Dam and the Parliament building as development diplomacy, with access dependent on Chabahar port in Iran, since Pakistan denies transit.

6. Major Powers

United States. The transformative moment was the Indo-US Civil Nuclear Agreement — the 123 Agreement, the IAEA safeguards agreement separating civil and military facilities, and the 2008 NSG waiver, which ended India's nuclear isolation without signing the NPT. India was designated a Major Defence Partner and granted STA-1 status. The four "foundational agreements" are frequently asked: GSOMIA (intelligence sharing), LEMOA (logistics exchange), COMCASA (secure communications) and BECA (geospatial intelligence). Institutional mechanisms include the 2+2 Ministerial Dialogue, iCET on critical and emerging technology, and the Quad. Frictions: trade and tariffs, immigration and H-1B, data localisation, and Indian purchases of Russian equipment attracting CAATSA risk.

Russia — a Special and Privileged Strategic Partnership, historically the dominant defence supplier (S-400, BrahMos as a joint venture, Su-30MKI and T-90 licensed production, the Kudankulam nuclear plant). Post-Ukraine, India abstained at the UN while sharply increasing discounted crude oil imports and expanding rupee-rouble settlement — the clearest illustration of strategic autonomy in practice, and of the dilemma created by growing Russia-China alignment.

  • European Union and others — the EU is among India's largest trading partners, with FTA negotiations resumed alongside a Trade and Technology Council
  • France is a durable strategic partner (Rafale, Scorpène, ISA co-founder, Indian Ocean cooperation)
  • Japan contributes the Mumbai-Ahmedabad high-speed rail, the Asia-Africa Growth Corridor and Quad membership
  • Israel supplies defence and agricultural technology under I2U2 alongside the UAE and US
  • the Gulf states are critical for energy, remittances and the roughly nine million-strong diaspora, with the UAE CEPA and the IMEC (India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor) as recent initiatives
  • Iran matters for Chabahar and the INSTC, constrained by sanctions
  • and Africa and Latin America feature through the Voice of Global South framing, lines of credit, ITEC training and India's role in securing the African Union's G20 membership.

7. Instruments and Evaluation

Name the instruments precisely when writing: lines of credit through the EXIM Bank, ITEC capacity-building scholarships, disaster relief and HADR operations, vaccine diplomacy under Vaccine Maitri, evacuation missions (Operations Ganga, Kaveri, Rahat) that demonstrate reach, defence exercises (Malabar, Yudh Abhyas, Indra, Varuna, Garuda, Hand-in-Hand), soft power through the ICCR, yoga diplomacy and cinema, and economic statecraft through CEPAs and FTAs.

For a balanced conclusion, acknowledge the constraints: an unsettled boundary with two nuclear neighbours; limited hard-power projection relative to China; a modest aid budget compared with Chinese lending; slow project execution abroad, which has damaged credibility in Nepal, Sri Lanka and Iran; and the tension between strategic autonomy and the deeper commitments that partners increasingly expect. The durable Indian argument is that multi-alignment maximises options in a multipolar world, provided delivery matches declaration.

Trap watch. The Simla Agreement made disputes bilateral and created the LoC. Under the Indus Waters Treaty India holds the eastern rivers. The 1949 Bhutan treaty was revised in 2007, ending the "guidance" clause. The India-Bangladesh maritime dispute was settled by UNCLOS arbitration, and the enclave exchange required the 100th Amendment. India is not in the NSG or the NPT but did receive a 2008 NSG waiver. The four foundational agreements with the US are GSOMIA, LEMOA, COMCASA and BECA. Chabahar is in Iran, Gwadar and Hambantota are Chinese-linked ports in Pakistan and Sri Lanka.
