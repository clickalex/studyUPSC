Financial Inclusion and Money Markets — Detailed Notes

Inclusion stack (JDY-credit-pension-insurance) plus money-market instrument vocabulary.

1. Inclusion stack

  • PMJDY (28 Aug 2014): zero-balance, RuPay card + ₹2L accident cover, ₹10k overdraft; ~53 crore accounts. JAM: JDY + Aadhaar + Mobile → DBT (~₹44 lakh crore cumulative — official DBT portal band); BC (business-correspondent) model + doorstep banking standards.
  • Credit: MUDRA (2015; Shishu ≤₹50k, Kishore to ₹5L, Tarun to ₹10L; Tarun Plus ₹20L (2024, repeat successful)); KCC (1998 animal-fisherman extension 2020; interest subvention); Stand-Up India (2016; SC-ST-women, ₹10L-1cr greenfield); PM SVANidhi (2020 street vendors); PM Vishwakarma (2023 artisans); NRLM SHG-bank linkage; DAY-NRLM women credit deepening; 'Lakhpati Didi' 3-crore target.
  • Pension-insurance trio (2015): APY (unorganised, guaranteed ₹1-5k pension 60+), PMJJBY (₹436 premium, ₹2L life), PMSBY (₹20 premium, ₹2L accident); PMFBY crop insurance (2016); NPS-APY architecture (PFRDA).

2. Money market instruments

  • Call-notice-term money (interbank overnight-14d); T-bills (91-182-364 days, discount issue); Cash Management Bills; Commercial Paper (7 days to 1 year), Certificates of Deposit (7d-1yr banks, 1-3yr FIs), Treasury-bills-vs-CP maturity grids; TREPS (repo platform replacing NDS-CDS); LAF-VRR auctions; FBIL benchmarks; CCIL clearing.
  • Short-term policy hooks: liquidity coverage, CBS (cash-credit), standing facilities; MSF uses; money-market mutual funds (liquid-fund regulation 2024 tightening).

MCQ hooks

  • PMJDY = 2014, RuPay + ₹2L cover + ₹10k OD.
  • MUDRA tiers: Shishu ≤50k < Kishore ≤5L < Tarun ≤10L < Tarun-Plus 20L (2024).
  • APY = guaranteed pension; PMJJBY = life ₹2L; PMSBY = accident ₹2L — trio swap trap.
  • Stand-Up India = SC-ST-women 10L-1cr greenfield (vs Mudra small-ticket).
  • T-bills = 91-182-364; CP = corporate; CD = bank-issued — instrument-origin pair.
  • TREPS = overnight repo platform (CCIL).

6. What Financial Inclusion Means and Why It Failed for So Long

The Rangarajan Committee (2008) defined financial inclusion as "the process of ensuring access to financial services and timely, adequate credit where needed by vulnerable groups such as weaker sections and low-income groups at an affordable cost." Four elements are embedded in that sentence and each is a separate policy problem: access (a physical or digital point of service), usage (accounts that are actually transacted in, not dormant), quality (products suited to irregular incomes) and affordability. Inclusion is not one product but a bundle — a savings account, credit, insurance, pension and payments.

The historical sequence is worth knowing as a narrative of successive attempts and their limits. Cooperative credit from 1904 was the first institutional answer to the moneylender, and it decayed into politicisation and dual control. Bank nationalisation in 1969 and 1980 brought branch expansion under the 1:4 licensing rule and the birth of priority sector lending, and it did dramatically widen rural branch networks — but it also produced directed lending with weak recovery. Regional Rural Banks (1975, on the Narasimham Committee's recommendation) were designed as low-cost rural institutions combining the local feel of a cooperative with the discipline of a commercial bank. NABARD (1982) became the apex refinancing body. The SHG-Bank Linkage Programme from 1992, now the world's largest microfinance programme, showed that group liability and social collateral could substitute for physical collateral, and it carried a strong women's empowerment dividend. Business correspondents from 2006 extended reach without brick-and-mortar branches. Yet by 2011 barely half of Indian households used formal banking.

The reasons for failure are instructive: high transaction costs of servicing small-value accounts; the absence of identity documents and hence KYC failure; distance and opportunity cost of travel; documentation and collateral requirements; low and irregular incomes; financial illiteracy; and, importantly, the demand-side problem that a household with no surplus has little use for a savings account. Any answer that treats inclusion as purely a supply-side problem is incomplete.

7. The JAM Trinity and the Current Architecture

The decisive change was the combination of three infrastructures — Jan Dhan, Aadhaar and Mobile — into what the Economic Survey christened the JAM trinity, enabling identity-verified, low-cost, direct transfers at population scale.

  • PMJDY (2014) — zero-balance Basic Savings Bank Deposit Accounts with a RuPay debit card, accident insurance cover, an overdraft facility after satisfactory operation, and no minimum balance requirement. Over 50 crore accounts, a majority held by women and most in rural and semi-urban areas. The valid critique is dormancy and duplicate accounts; the valid defence is that the accounts became the rails for DBT during the pandemic.
  • Aadhaar — biometric identity solving the KYC problem, with e-KYC and the Aadhaar Enabled Payment System allowing a business correspondent with a fingerprint reader to serve a village. The Supreme Court in Puttaswamy upheld Aadhaar for subsidies and benefits under Article 110-linked money bill reasoning while striking down mandatory linkage for private services under Section 57.
  • Direct Benefit Transfer — plugging leakage by transferring subsidies to accounts instead of routing them through intermediaries, most visibly in PAHAL for LPG. Counter-arguments deserve airing: exclusion errors from biometric failure, the last-mile problem where the nearest banking point is distant, and the substitution of cash for goods where markets are thin.

New institutional forms created on the Nachiket Mor Committee's differentiated-banking logic: Payments Banks, which may accept deposits up to a ceiling and offer remittances and payments but cannot lend or issue credit cards, and must invest in government securities; and Small Finance Banks, which can lend, must direct 75% of credit to the priority sector and keep at least half their loans below 25 lakh rupees. Alongside these sit NBFC-MFIs, regulated after the 2010 Andhra Pradesh microfinance crisis on the Malegam Committee's recommendations — margin caps, interest ceilings, limits on multiple lending and coercive recovery — and now governed by a harmonised, indebtedness-based regulatory framework.

Social security schemes delivered through these accounts: PMJJBY (life cover), PMSBY (accident cover), Atal Pension Yojana (guaranteed pension for the unorganised sector), PM-KISAN, PMMY / MUDRA loans in Shishu, Kishor and Tarun categories, Stand-Up India for SC/ST and women entrepreneurs, and PM SVANidhi for street vendors. Priority sector lending targets remain the central regulatory instrument: 40% of adjusted net bank credit overall, with sub-targets for agriculture, micro enterprises and weaker sections, and Priority Sector Lending Certificates allowing banks to trade surpluses and shortfalls.

8. Digital Payments and the India Stack

The India Stack is the layered public digital infrastructure that made inclusion cheap: an identity layer (Aadhaar, e-KYC, e-Sign), a payments layer (UPI, AEPS, IMPS, Bharat Bill Pay), and a data layer (DigiLocker and the Account Aggregator framework, which lets an individual consent to share financial data with a lender, converting data into collateral for the thin-file borrower).

UPI, operated by the NPCI, is an interoperable, real-time, 24×7 system built on virtual payment addresses so that the payer need not know the payee's account number. Its interoperability and zero merchant discount rate on person-to-merchant transactions explain the explosive adoption; the corresponding concern is that zero MDR leaves no revenue model, so the ecosystem depends on incentives, and that two apps dominate volumes, prompting NPCI's market-share cap. Extensions to know: UPI 123PAY for feature phones, UPI Lite for small offline payments, UPI AutoPay, credit lines on UPI, and cross-border linkages with Singapore's PayNow, the UAE and others. RuPay, Aadhaar Pay, NEFT and RTGS now round the clock, and Bharat QR complete the picture.

Central Bank Digital Currency — the digital rupee — is being piloted in wholesale and retail forms. It is a sovereign liability, unlike a cryptocurrency; the retail version offers programmability and potential offline use, and the policy tension is between anonymity and traceability, and the risk of disintermediating banks if households shift deposits into CBDC during stress.

Persisting challenges: dormant accounts and the gap between access and usage; the digital divide by gender, age and connectivity; rising digital fraud and the inadequacy of grievance redress; over-indebtedness from multiple microfinance and app-based lending; unregulated digital lending apps, for which the RBI has issued guidelines requiring direct flow of funds between borrower and regulated entity; data privacy, now governed by the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023; and the weak viability of business correspondents, whose commissions rarely cover costs. The measurement framework to cite is the RBI's Financial Inclusion Index, built on access, usage and quality, together with the World Bank Global Findex.

Trap watch. Payments Banks cannot lend or issue credit cards; Small Finance Banks can and carry a 75% priority sector obligation. RRBs were set up in 1975 on the Narasimham Committee's recommendation, sponsored jointly by the Centre, State and a sponsor bank. Financial inclusion is access and usage and quality, not merely account opening. UPI is run by NPCI, not the RBI directly. CBDC is a central bank liability; cryptocurrency is not. The Malegam Committee followed the Andhra Pradesh microfinance crisis. PSL target is 40% of adjusted net bank credit for domestic scheduled commercial banks.
