Money, Banking and RBI Policy — Detailed Notes

Money supply, RBI instruments, MPC frame, banking structure. Rate-name precision decides marks.

1. Money supply & creation

  • RBI measures: M1 = currency + demand deposits + other deposits with RBI
  • M2 = M1 + savings with Post Office
  • M3 = M1 + time deposits + government deposits with RBI (aggregate monetary resources)
  • M4 = M3 + total Post Office deposits (ex-NSC). High-powered money (M0) = currency + banks' reserves with RBI.
  • Money multiplier = M3/M0
  • driven by reserve behaviour
  • seigniorage
  • demonetisation 2016 (86% value returned; 99.3% deposited)
  • digital currency: e-rupee CBDC (pilots since 2022; retail + wholesale).

2. RBI instruments (LAF corridor era)

  • Repo rate = single policy rate (since 2021 revised framework docs); SDF (standing deposit facility) = floor (2022, replaced fixed reverse-repo role); MSF = ceiling (+25 bp). Bank rate = penal/long-term signal (aligned to MSF). CRR = 4% (no interest, bi-fortnight maintenance); SLR = 18% (statutory, includes G-sec holdings). OMOs (outright + switches), forex swaps, VRR/VRRR variable-rate auctions, SDF-MSF corridor ±25 bp.
  • MPC (RBI Act, 1934 — amended 2016): 6 members (3 RBI incl. Governor — casting vote; 3 external), inflation target 4% CPI ±2%, failure band (3 consecutive quarters outside band triggers report); bi-monthly cadence — six meetings a year since Oct 2022.
  • Transmission: base rate → MCLR (2016) → EBLR (2019 external-benchmark for retail/SME loans); repo-linked pricing.

3. Banking structure & regulation

  • Segments: PSBs (consolidation 2019-20: 10 mergers → 12 PSBs), private (incl. small finance banks, payments banks — Paytm Payments Bank RBI curbs 2024), RRBs ('One State One RRB' — 28 RRBs from May 2025), co-ops (dual control; 2020 amendment widened RBI power), NBFCs (scale-based regulation 2022), DFIs: NABARD (1982), SIDBI, NaBFID (2021 infrastructure DFI).
  • Regulatory actions: PCA framework, Prompt-corrective
  • deposit insurance DICGC ₹5 lakh (2021 amendment)
  • Resolution: IBC 2016 (2021 amendment added NBFC route)
  • cyber-fraud norms
  • UPI-credit lines.
  • Priority sector 40% ANBC (agri 18%, weaker sections 12% target raise, MSME 7.5%)
  • financial inclusion: PMJDY, JAM
  • BC model
  • KCC animal-husbandry extension 2020.

⚡ MCQ hooks

  • Repo = policy rate; SDF = floor; MSF = ceiling (±25 bp corridor).
  • MPC = 6 (3+3), Governor casting vote; target 4%±2 CPI; bi-monthly.
  • CRR 4% unremunerated; SLR 18% remunerated; both RBI-set (current CRR 4%, SLR 18% with 40% ceiling).
  • M1 M2 M3 M4 composition order; M0 = high-powered.
  • EBLR = external benchmark (repo-linked) since Oct 2019.
  • DICGC cover = ₹5 lakh; NBFC scale-based 2022; RRB 'one state one bank' 2025.

4. The Corridor System and How Policy Actually Transmits

The modern RBI operates a corridor system in which the weighted average call rate — the operating target — is held inside a band whose ceiling is the Marginal Standing Facility, whose floor is the Standing Deposit Facility, and whose centre is the repo rate. Since the revised liquidity framework, the corridor has been symmetric at ±25 basis points around the repo rate: with the repo rate at 5.25 per cent (the August 2026 review, the fourth successive hold under Governor Sanjay Malhotra with a neutral stance), the SDF absorbs surplus liquidity at 5.00 per cent and the MSF injects against collateral at 5.50 per cent, with the Bank Rate aligned to the MSF. The causal logic runs from the corridor to the economy in three steps. First, the RBI's variable-rate repo and reverse-repo auctions (VRR and VRRR) steer the call rate toward the repo rate, keeping it pinned near the centre of the corridor. Second, the call rate anchors the rest of the money market — treasury bills, commercial paper, certificates of deposit — because banks price short-term funds off the overnight rate. Third, and slowest, the money market feeds bank lending rates through the external benchmark regime, which is why every discussion of transmission now begins at the corridor and ends at the loan contract.

The SDF deserves separate attention because it changed the meaning of the floor. Before April 2022 the floor was the fixed-rate reverse repo, an administered rate the RBI could move at will and one that required collateral from the central bank. The SDF, created under the amended RBI Act, is a collateral-free absorption window at the RBI's discretion: banks park surplus overnight with the central bank without the RBI needing to furnish government securities in return. That removed the collateral constraint that had bound absorption during surplus phases, and it is why the fixed reverse repo now survives only as a legacy rate (3.35 per cent, untouched since May 2020) while the SDF does the actual work of the floor. The MSF, by contrast, is a penal window — banks borrow overnight against SLR securities up to a limit, at repo plus 25 basis points — and its utilisation is the market's distress signal: persistent MSF borrowing means systemic deficit, just as persistent SDF parking means systemic surplus. The Bank Rate, aligned to the MSF, is no longer an operating rate at all; it survives as the penal reference for CRR and SLR shortfalls and for certain long-term rediscounting, which is why the June 2025 statement revised it mechanically from 6.25 to 5.75 per cent alongside the repo cut and it now stands at 5.50 per cent.

Transmission — the journey from a repo cut to a cheaper loan — is where Indian monetary policy visibly improved and where its limits are most examinable. The chain had three links historically: base rate (2010), marginal cost of funds (MCLR, 2016), and the external benchmark regime (EBLR, October 2019), under which floating-rate retail and MSME loans must reset against an external anchor, in practice the repo rate, at least once a quarter. The EBLR reform is the reason the 100 basis points of repo cuts delivered between February and June 2025 (including the outsized 50-point cut of June) passed into fresh home and auto loans within a quarter, whereas pre-2019 easing routinely died inside banks' internal spreads. But three frictions survive, and examiners test all of them. First, only new floating-rate loans reprice automatically; the large stock of older MCLR-linked loans reprices at its own reset dates, so transmission is always front-loaded on fresh credit. Second, banks fund themselves substantially through term deposits whose rates adjust with a lag, so deposit-side stickiness compresses margins and slows pass-through whenever easing is rapid — the precise \"wait for transmission\" argument the MPC invoked through its 2025–26 holds. Third, small savings schemes administered by the government (PPF, NSC, post-office deposits) set an effective floor under deposit rates that no repo cut can pierce, because households arbitrage between bank deposits and small savings. The durable lesson is that the repo rate is the price of marginal liquidity, not the price of credit: between the two stand the corridor, the funding mix, and the fiscal footprint of administered rates.

5. Flexible Inflation Targeting: Framework, Record, and the March 2026 Review

India's monetary framework since 2016 is flexible inflation targeting (FIT), legislated through the 2016 amendment to the RBI Act, 1934: the government notifies the target, the six-member MPC pursues it, and failure is defined mechanically — average CPI inflation above 6 per cent or below 2 per cent for three consecutive quarters obliges the RBI to report to the government the reasons, the remedial action, and the horizon for return. The word \"flexible\" carries the whole doctrine: the MPC must keep inflation near 4 per cent while keeping in mind the objective of growth, which is the statutory permission for the rate-cutting cycle of 2019 and the pandemic easing of 2020, and equally the permission for the 250-point hiking cycle of 2022–23. Candidates who describe FIT as \"the RBI targets 4 per cent inflation\" have missed the flexibility; candidates who describe it as \"growth versus inflation\" have missed that the hierarchy is fixed — price stability first, growth within that constraint.

The framework's record divides into three phases, and the periodisation itself is the analysis. From 2016 to 2019 the regime earned credibility: inflation averaged near target, expectations surveys moderated, and the MPC's published minutes made dissent visible for the first time. From 2020 to 2022 the framework absorbed two shocks it was never designed for — the pandemic collapse and the commodity surge after the Russia–Ukraine war — and the breach was instructive: inflation held above the 6 per cent upper tolerance through the first three quarters of 2022, triggering the only failure report the MPC has yet filed (November 2022), and the episode demonstrated both the framework's accountability (the letter, the published reasoning) and its honesty about supply shocks (rates rose, but the MPC stated plainly that food and fuel shocks cannot be cured by demand management). From 2023 the regime entered its vindication phase: inflation glided back inside the band, the 2025 easing cycle began only after headline inflation printed below 4 per cent from February 2025, and CPI inflation ran near 3–3.5 per cent through early 2026 — comfortably inside tolerance when the review fell due.

The second five-year review, concluded in March 2026, retained the target at 4 per cent with the 2–6 per cent tolerance band for 1 April 2026 to 31 March 2031, and retained headline CPI — including food — as the target variable. The retention of food in the target was the contested question: with food carrying roughly 46 per cent weight in the CPI basket, some government voices had argued for a core or trimmed target that would free monetary policy from monsoon arithmetic. The RBI's internal panel recommended against exclusion, on the ground that food inflation shapes household expectations and wage bargaining more than any other component, and that a target households cannot recognise cannot anchor expectations. That reasoning is the portable analytical point: the target variable is chosen not for statistical elegance but for expectational grip. The review also settled, at least for five years, the base-year question — the CPI's 2012 base is now widely acknowledged as dated, and the pending revision of the series (with debated weights for food, housing and services) remains the live technical reform. For the examination, the FIT story compresses to a causal sentence: a legislated target plus a voting committee plus a defined failure procedure converted Indian monetary policy from an opaque rate-setting ritual into an accountable rule, and the 2022 breach proved the rule binds because the letter had to be written.

6. Banking Structure: Ownership, Consolidation, and the Regulatory Perimeter

Indian banking is best understood as four concentric rings around the RBI, each ring created by a different decade's policy anxiety. The inner ring is the public sector: twelve banks after the 2019–20 amalgamations merged ten PSBs into four anchor banks (Punjab National Bank, Canara Bank, Union Bank, Indian Bank absorbing Oriental Bank of Commerce and United Bank; Syndicate; Andhra and Corporation; Allahabad respectively), with the State Bank group already consolidated in 2017. The consolidation logic was fiscal as much as managerial — larger balance sheets absorb provisioning shocks, the government's recapitalisation bill falls, and a smaller set of banks can be pushed toward risk-based supervision — and its vindication arrived in the mid-2020s when PSB balance sheets, cleaned by the IBC and write-off discipline, posted record profits and the government began speaking of the next reform as privatisation rather than merger. The second ring is private banking, itself two generations: the post-1993 entrants (HDFC Bank, ICICI, Axis, Kotak) now grown systemic — HDFC Bank's July 2023 merger with HDFC Ltd. created a balance sheet larger than several PSBs combined — and the differentiated licences of the 2015 vintage, small finance banks and payments banks, designed to bank the unbanked without replicating full-service charters. The payments-bank experiment is the cautionary tale of the ring: constrained to deposits of ₹2 lakh per customer with no lending book, most payments banks struggled for viability, and the RBI's January 2024 curbs on Paytm Payments Bank — for persistent compliance failures — demonstrated that a differentiated licence carries undifferentiated supervision.

The third ring is rural and cooperative credit, where the landmark reform is the most recent. The 'One State, One RRB' amalgamation effective 1 May 2025 merged 26 regional rural banks across ten states and one Union Territory into single state-level entities, cutting the RRB count from 43 to 28 with over 22,000 branches covering nearly 700 districts. The economics are scale economies in technology and treasury management: a single state-level RRB can afford core-banking upgrades, harmonised HR, and a professional board in a way three sub-scale RRBs sharing one state could not. Above the RRBs sit the cooperatives — urban cooperative banks, state and district central cooperative banks — historically under dual control (RBI for banking functions, state registrars for management), a duality the 2020 amendment to the Banking Regulation Act narrowed by widening RBI powers over UCB governance, audit and resolution after the PMC Bank failure of 2019. The outer ring is non-bank credit: NBFCs under scale-based regulation since 2022 (four layers — base, middle, upper, top — with the upper layer facing bank-like capital, governance and disclosure norms), housing finance companies under RBI jurisdiction since 2019, and the infrastructure DFIs — NABARD (1982) for agriculture and rural development, SIDBI (1990) for MSME refinancing, and NaBFID (2021) as the dedicated infrastructure financier with a Crisil-estimated multi-trillion-rupee pipeline role. The regulatory arc across all four rings points one way: activity-based supervision replacing charter-based forbearance, so that the same risk attracts the same rule whether it sits in a bank, an NBFC or a fintech balance sheet.

Resolution and safety-net architecture complete the picture, and the comparison to run is before versus after 2016. Before the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, large-loan default meant Corporate Debt Restructuring, Strategic Debt Restructuring and Scheme for Sustainable Structuring — acronyms for evergreening, under which promoters kept control and banks kept pretending. The IBC's 330-day outer limit, creditor-in-control committee of creditors, and Section 29A's bar on defaulting promoters rebidding reversed the polarity: Essar Steel (ArcelorMittal, 2019), Bhushan Steel (Tata Steel, 2018) and DHFL (Piramal, 2021 — the first financial-services resolution) established that ownership transfers and haircuts are real. The 2021 amendment extended the Code's logic to NBFCs through RBI-notified rules. Alongside resolution sits deposit protection: the DICGC cover of ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank (raised from ₹1 lakh by the 2021 amendment, with interim pay-out within 90 days under the 2021 Act's provisions). The portable judgment is that Indian banking safety now rests on three legs — risk-weighted capital under Basel III as implemented by the RBI, time-bound resolution under the IBC, and explicit deposit insurance — where a decade ago it rested substantially on the implicit sovereign guarantee behind public ownership.

7. Financial Inclusion as Infrastructure: PMJDY, UPI, and the e-Rupee

The inclusion story of the last decade is best read as stack-building: each layer assumed the previous one, and the sequence explains why Indian inclusion scaled faster than microfinance or branch expansion ever managed. The base layer is identity and accounts — Aadhaar (over 1.3 billion enrolments) plus the Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana, launched August 2014, which opened hundreds of millions of zero-balance-tolerant accounts with RuPay cards and built the rails for direct benefit transfer. The middle layer is payments interoperability — the Unified Payments Interface (2016), built by the NPCI as an open protocol on which competing apps (BHIM, PhonePe, Google Pay, Paytm) offer the same addressing, so that network effects accrue to the system rather than to any single firm. The top layer is credit and data — the Account Aggregator framework (2021), the Open Credit Enablement Network, UPI credit lines and UPI Lite/X for small and offline payments. The causal claim that examiners reward is that interoperability, not subsidy, did the work: UPI's zero-merchant-discount-rate regime (the MDR on UPI and RuPay debit was removed from January 2020) made acceptance free for merchants, which made issuance valuable for banks, which made the protocol ubiquitous — volumes crossing ten billion monthly transactions in 2023 and rising thereafter — without any single institution bearing the cost of the network.

The Central Bank Digital Currency — the e-rupee — is the next layer, and its two pilots must be kept distinct. The wholesale pilot (November 2022) settles interbank government-securities transactions in central-bank money, targeting settlement risk and the call-money market's collateral churn. The retail pilot (December 2022, extended city-by-city through 2023–24) issues a tokenised liability of the RBI into consumer wallets, intermediated by banks, with programmability features (expiry-dated subsidy transfers, purpose-bound fertiliser or scholarship money) tested from 2023 onward. The design choice that matters analytically is intermediated, not disintermediated: the RBI issues but banks distribute, precisely to avoid the disintermediation panic — deposits fleeing to risk-free CBDC wallets — that a direct central-bank account would trigger. The honest assessment, which is what scores, is that the e-rupee's retail use case remains unproven while UPI works superbly: CBDC's durable justification is programmability of state transfers and resilience of settlement, not replacing a payments system that already clears instantly at zero cost. Priority-sector architecture runs in parallel: 40 per cent of adjusted net bank credit (18 per cent agriculture, with sub-targets for small and marginal farmers; 7.5 per cent micro-enterprises; 12 per cent weaker sections), enforced through shortfall contributions to NABARD's RIDF and SIDBI's funds — the mechanism that converts an unmet quota into rural and MSME refinancing rather than a fine.

The inclusion edifice therefore stands on a division of labour that is itself the examination point. The state built protocols (Aadhaar, UPI, Account Aggregator, ONDC for commerce), the RBI licensed differentiation (SFBs, payments banks, NBFC layers, co-lending models pairing banks' balance sheets with NBFCs' last-mile reach), and markets supplied competition on top of open rails. The failure modes are equally structural: Jan Dhan accounts that remain dormant or overdrawn-unviable, business-correspondent economics that collapse where transaction density is thin, digital fraud scaling with digital volume (the RBI's 2024 tightening of card and internet-banking norms and the DPDP Act 2023's consent architecture are the regulatory response), and the persistent gender and smartphone gaps that mean the stack reaches men before women and towns before villages. An answer that names the stack, credits interoperability, concedes the e-rupee's open question, and ends on the fraud-and-gap constraint has covered inclusion at the depth the paper demands.

Trap watch. The repo rate is the single policy rate (5.25 per cent at the August 2026 hold, neutral stance); the SDF (5.00 per cent) is the floor and the MSF (5.50 per cent) the ceiling — not the reverse. The fixed reverse repo (3.35 per cent, untouched since May 2020) is a legacy rate, no longer the floor; the SDF replaced that role in April 2022 and is collateral-free. The Bank Rate (5.50 per cent, aligned to MSF) is a penal reference, not an operating rate. CRR is 3.00 per cent of NDTL (cut 100 bp in June 2025 in four tranches to November 2025, releasing about ₹2.5 lakh crore) and is unremunerated; SLR is 18 per cent and is remunerated through the securities themselves — candidates routinely swap the two. The MPC has six members (Governor with a casting vote, two RBI officials, three government-appointed externals); the FIT target is 4 per cent CPI with a 2–6 per cent band, retained in March 2026 for 2026–31, with headline CPI including food — not core inflation — as the target variable, and failure means three consecutive quarters outside the band. M1 is currency plus demand deposits plus other deposits with RBI; M3 adds time deposits and is the broad-money aggregate, while M0 (reserve money) is currency plus bankers' deposits with RBI. EBLR (October 2019) governs new floating retail and MSME loans; the old MCLR stock reprices only at reset dates. DICGC cover is ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank (2021). There are 12 PSBs after the 2019–20 mergers and 28 RRBs after 'One State, One RRB' (1 May 2025) — not 43. NaBFID (2021) is the infrastructure DFI; NABARD (1982) serves agriculture and SIDBI (1990) MSMEs. The retail e-rupee pilot began December 2022 and the wholesale pilot November 2022 — the wholesale came first.
