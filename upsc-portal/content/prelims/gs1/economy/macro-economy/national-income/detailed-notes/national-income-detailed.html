National Income and Accounts — Detailed Notes

GDP-GVA-NNP ladder, India's 2015 series revision, estimation sides. Formula-statement questions.

1. The aggregate ladder

  • GDP (territorial): market value of final goods-services within domestic territory. GNP = GDP + NFIA (net factor income from abroad — negative for India). NNP = GNP - depreciation (consumption of fixed capital). NDP/NNP at factor cost = NNI; market price = factor cost + net indirect taxes (indirect taxes minus subsidies).
  • Three approaches: income (compensation + operating surplus + mixed income), product/value-added (GVA by sector), expenditure (C + I + G + NX). Identities hold ex-post; statistical discrepancy line.
  • Per-capita income = NNI/population; real vs nominal (constant 2011-12 prices); GDP deflator = nominal/real x 100 (India uses WPI-CPI hybrid implicit deflator).

2. India's national accounts practice

  • Compiled by NSO (MoSPI) — CSO-NSSO merger 2019; quarterly + annual estimates; first-revised-final release calendar (e.g. T+56 days for quarterly GDP).
  • 2015 series change: base 2011-12; GVA at basic prices replaced GVA at factor cost (production minus product taxes now separate); MCA-21 corporate database, enterprise-based approach; controversy on informal-sector extrapolation (MCA-21 over-coverage critique — Subramanian-Sundaram debate).
  • Sectoral GVA (8 sectors, NIC-2008); crops-livestock shares; ASI for manufacturing; unorganised-periodic surveys; PLFS for employment linkage; HCES 2022-23 (new consumption survey after 2011-12 gap).
  • New developments: base-revision to 2022-23 announced (2025-26 cycle); real-time nowcasting attempts; state GSDP parallel series.

3. Related concepts & debates

  • Purchasing power parity (PPP) comparisons (India 3rd-largest PPP economy); GDP-welfare critique (Green GDP, HDI complement); informal sector share (~45% GVA, ~90% employment — band quotes); digital-economy satellites (DEA papers).
  • WPI-CPI distinction in deflation; CPI headline (4%±2 target) vs GVA growth — MPC reacts to CPI not GDP.

MCQ hooks

  • NNP(FC) = national income; India's NFIA negative → GNP < GDP.
  • Market price = factor cost + NIT; basic prices vs factor cost = + production taxes, - production subsidies (2015 change).
  • Base year 2011-12 (revision to 2022-23 under way); GVA-basic = headline production metric.
  • NSO under MoSPI; deflator formula; per-capita = NNI/pop.
  • Expenditure identity C+I+G+NX; three approaches equivalent ex-post.
  • CPI is the MPC's target variable (not WPI, not GDP).

6. The Aggregates and How They Interconnect

National income accounting is pure definitional precision, and the paper rewards exactness. Build the chain from the most basic aggregate outward.

  • GDP (Gross Domestic Product) — the market value of all final goods and services produced within the domestic territory in a year, regardless of who owns the factors.
  • GNP (Gross National Product) = GDP + Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA). NFIA is income earned by residents abroad minus income earned by foreigners domestically. For India NFIA is typically negative, so GNP is slightly less than GDP — a favourite one-liner.
  • NDP = GDP − depreciation (consumption of fixed capital). NNP = GNP − depreciation.
  • NNP at factor cost = National Income. This is the formal definition.
  • Per capita income = National Income ÷ population.

The market price vs factor cost bridge is asked constantly:

Market Price = Factor Cost + Indirect Taxes − Subsidies
Factor Cost = Market Price − Indirect Taxes + Subsidies
(Indirect Taxes − Subsidies = Net Indirect Taxes)

Since 2015 India's Central Statistics Office presents headline figures as GVA at basic prices and GDP at market prices. The relationship is: GVA at basic prices = GDP at market prices − product taxes + product subsidies. Basic price sits between factor cost and market price — it includes production taxes (such as land revenue and stamp duty, levied irrespective of output) but excludes product taxes (such as GST, levied per unit of output). The base year was revised to 2011–12, with the MCA-21 corporate database replacing the older enterprise survey — a methodological change that itself has been the subject of debate.

7. Nominal, Real and the Deflator

Nominal GDP is measured at current prices and therefore rises with inflation even if output is flat. Real GDP is measured at constant (base-year) prices and reflects genuine volume growth. The relationship gives the GDP deflator:

GDP Deflator = (Nominal GDP ÷ Real GDP) × 100

The deflator is the most comprehensive price index because it covers every good and service in GDP, with weights that change each year, unlike the fixed basket of the CPI or WPI. It excludes imports (since GDP is domestic production), whereas CPI includes imported consumption goods — precisely the discrimination examiners target.

Note also that when nominal growth exceeds real growth, inflation is positive; if real growth exceeds nominal, the economy is in deflation. India's fiscal deficit targets are expressed as a percentage of nominal GDP, which is why an inflation surprise mechanically flatters the ratio.

8. The Three Methods of Measurement

All three must yield the same figure, since one person's expenditure is another's income and both equal the value of output.

MethodWhat is summedPrincipal pitfall
Production (Value Added)Gross value added across all sectors: output minus intermediate consumptionDouble counting if intermediate goods are included; avoided by counting only final goods or only value added
IncomeCompensation of employees + operating surplus + mixed income of the self-employedMust exclude transfer payments (pensions, scholarships, subsidies) since no production corresponds to them
ExpenditureC + I + G + (X − M) — private final consumption, gross capital formation, government final consumption, net exportsMust exclude expenditure on second-hand goods and on financial assets; imports are subtracted because they are not domestic output

Items included despite common confusion: imputed rent of owner-occupied houses, the value of output retained for self-consumption by farmers, and government services valued at cost. Items excluded: purely financial transactions, capital gains, second-hand sales, illegal activity (in principle), unpaid household and care work, and leisure. The systematic exclusion of unpaid domestic labour — overwhelmingly performed by women — is a standard critique with a gender dimension worth deploying in mains.

9. Sectors, Institutions and the Indian Context

The classification into primary (agriculture, forestry, fishing, mining), secondary (manufacturing, construction, electricity) and tertiary (services) supports the structural-transformation narrative. India's distinctive pattern is a services-led transition: the tertiary sector contributes over half of GVA while the primary sector still employs the largest workforce share — a mismatch producing low agricultural productivity and what economists call premature deindustrialisation, since manufacturing's share plateaued at a lower level and earlier stage than in East Asia. Some also speak of a quaternary (knowledge, R&D) and quinary (top-level decision-making) sector.

Institutional history worth citing: Dadabhai Naoroji made the first estimate in Poverty and Un-British Rule in India (his "drain theory"), followed by V. K. R. V. Rao, whose method was the first scientifically rigorous one. The National Income Committee was set up in 1949 under P. C. Mahalanobis, with Gadgil and Rao as members. Today the National Statistical Office under MoSPI compiles the accounts, releasing advance, provisional and revised estimates.

Limitations of GDP as a welfare measure — a reliable mains question. GDP ignores distribution (growth may accrue to the top decile), counts regrettables positively (pollution clean-up, disaster reconstruction, crime prevention all add to GDP), ignores environmental depletion and the depreciation of natural capital, omits the informal and household sectors, and says nothing about health, education or freedom. The alternatives to name: the Human Development Index (Mahbub ul Haq and Amartya Sen, combining life expectancy, education and GNI per capita), the Inequality-adjusted HDI, the Multidimensional Poverty Index, the Genuine Progress Indicator, Green GDP and natural-capital accounting, the Gini coefficient and Lorenz curve for distribution, and Bhutan's Gross National Happiness. Sen's capability approach — development as the expansion of substantive freedoms rather than the accumulation of income — is the theoretical anchor for this critique.

Trap watch. National Income is NNP at factor cost, not GNP or GDP. GDP is territorial; GNP is by nationality of factors. For India NFIA is negative, so GNP < GDP. Transfer payments are excluded from national income. The GDP deflator has a variable basket, unlike CPI and WPI. GVA at basic prices excludes product taxes but includes production taxes. Depreciation separates "gross" from "net", never "domestic" from "national" — that is NFIA. India's base year is 2011–12.
