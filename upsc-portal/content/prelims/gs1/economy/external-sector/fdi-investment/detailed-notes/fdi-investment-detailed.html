FDI, FPI and Investment Vehicles — Detailed Notes

Routes, sector caps, vehicles (InvIT-REIT-AIF), and the FDI-FPI distinction. Route-cap questions recur.

1. FDI routes & sector map

  • Automatic route (most sectors: manufacturing 100%); approval route (few: multi-brand retail with 30% local sourcing, print media 26%, banking-private 74%, insurance 74%, defence 74% automatic since 2020 with safeguards, space 100% certain activities 2024, telecom 100% automatic era); prohibited list: lottery-gambling-betting, chit funds, Nidhi, real-estate business (trading; construction-development allowed), tobacco manufacturing, agriculture/plantation (with exceptions), atomic energy, railway operations (services FDI 100% allowed, operations state monopoly).
  • Policy anchors: 2016+ liberalisation rounds
  • press-note 3 (2020): land-bordering-country approvals (China screening)
  • DPPM sourcing rules
  • e-commerce FDI (inventory model banned, marketplace allowed).
  • Flows: FY21-24 gross inflows ~$70-85bn band; sources: Singapore > Mauritius > USA (Mauritius declining post-2017 treaty amendment; Singapore-Mauritius treaty tightening 2024); sectors: services, computer-software, trading, automobiles.

2. FDI vs FPI & vehicles

  • FDI = 10%+ stake with lasting interest-management participation (IMF BoP definition); FPI = listed securities without control (FPI framework 2014 merged FIIs-sub-accounts-QFIs; two-category simplification 2019).
  • InvITs (2014 SEBI regs; IRB-IndiGrid-NHAI InvIT), REITs (2014; Embassy-Mindspace), SM REITs (2024)
  • AIF (2012 regs): Cat-I (VC, social-venture, infrastructure, SME), Cat-II (PE-debt), Cat-III (hedge/trading, leverage allowed)
  • FVCI
  • startup-unicorn financing lines.
  • GIFT IFSC (Gujarat; IFSCA act 2019 unified regulator): offshore rupee products, GIFT-Nifty (2023 shift from SGX), aircraft-shipping leasing, FDI-agnostic regime.

⚡ MCQ hooks

  • FDI = 10%+ control test; FPI = no-control listed portfolio.
  • Prohibited list verbatim (lottery-chit-real-estate-trading-tobacco-atomic).
  • Defence = 74% automatic (2020); space liberalisation 2024; insurance-banking = 74%.
  • Press Note 3 (2020) = land-border screening.
  • AIF: Cat-III only leveraged-hedge; InvIT-REIT = trust structures.
  • IFSCA 2019 = unified IFSC regulator; GIFT-Nifty 2023.
  • Top sources: Singapore, Mauritius (post-treaty decline), USA.

3. Foreign Investment: Concepts and the Indian Regime

Foreign Direct Investment is cross-border investment made with the objective of establishing a lasting interest — conventionally 10% or more of the equity or voting power — accompanied by an element of management control. Foreign Portfolio Investment is a purely financial stake below that threshold, made for returns and not control. The distinction drives everything: FDI is stable, long-tenor, brings technology, management practice and market access, and is not easily reversed; FPI is liquid, volatile, herd-prone, and can exit at the first sign of global risk-off sentiment — the "hot money" whose sudden stop caused the 2013 taper tantrum. A country that finances its current account deficit with FDI is far safer than one financing it with FPI, and the quality of financing is therefore as important as the size of the deficit.

Classify FDI by direction and motive. Greenfield investment builds new capacity and creates jobs directly; brownfield investment acquires an existing entity through merger or acquisition and may add no new capacity at all. By motive: market-seeking (serving the Indian consumer), resource-seeking, efficiency-seeking (using India as a low-cost production base for export) and strategic-asset-seeking. Horizontal FDI replicates the same activity abroad; vertical FDI locates a different stage of the value chain. Note that a large share of India's FDI has been market-seeking and brownfield, which is why headline FDI numbers do not translate proportionately into manufacturing jobs.

India's policy architecture. Two routes exist: the automatic route, requiring no prior government approval, only post-facto reporting to the RBI; and the government route, requiring approval from the concerned administrative ministry after the abolition of the FIPB in 2017, processed through the Foreign Investment Facilitation Portal. Sectoral caps in outline: 100% automatic in most manufacturing, single-brand retail, construction, and renewable energy; 100% in insurance intermediaries with insurance companies at 74%; 74% automatic in defence with beyond that by government route where it gives access to modern technology; 49% in public sector banks by government route and 74% in private banks; 26% government route in print media and news broadcasting — the lowest caps, reflecting concern for editorial independence; and prohibited sectors, which are lottery and gambling, chit funds, nidhi companies, real estate business and farm houses, trading in transferable development rights, tobacco manufacturing, and atomic energy and railway operations.

The multi-brand retail position is politically sensitive: 51% is permitted by government route with conditions on minimum investment, sourcing and back-end infrastructure, but several states have not permitted it, and the concern for small retailers keeps it effectively dormant. Press Note 3 of 2020 is a frequently examined provision: it requires government approval for any investment from an entity in a country sharing a land border with India, or where the beneficial owner is situated in such a country. Its purpose was to prevent opportunistic takeovers of distressed Indian firms during the pandemic, and in practice it targets Chinese capital.

4. Determinants, Impact and the Critique

  • What attracts FDI: market size and growth, which is India's principal draw
  • political and macroeconomic stability
  • the quality of infrastructure and logistics
  • labour cost, skills and flexibility
  • the tax regime and its predictability
  • contract enforcement and the rule of law
  • ease of doing business
  • and increasingly supply-chain diversification under the "China plus one" strategy, from which India competes for share with Vietnam, Mexico, Thailand and Indonesia.
  • Benefits: non-debt-creating external finance — an important point, since FDI adds no repayment obligation unlike external commercial borrowing
  • capital formation
  • technology and knowledge spillovers to domestic suppliers
  • management and quality practices
  • integration into global value chains
  • employment
  • export competitiveness
  • and greater product choice and competition for consumers.

Costs and criticisms that a balanced answer must include: crowding out of domestic firms unable to match the scale of multinationals; profit repatriation, which shows up as a primary income deficit in the balance of payments and can exceed inflows over time; transfer pricing and base erosion, whereby profits are shifted to low-tax jurisdictions; round-tripping of domestic money through Mauritius, Singapore and other treaty jurisdictions to claim tax and regulatory benefits — the reason the India-Mauritius treaty was renegotiated in 2016 to tax capital gains at source and introduce a limitation-of-benefits clause; enclave development with weak local linkages; loss of policy autonomy; and concerns about strategic sectors and data. Balance-of-payments logic also cautions that brownfield acquisitions merely transfer ownership.

Where FDI comes from and goes. Top sources are Singapore and Mauritius — a fact explained by treaty structuring and the presence of holding companies rather than genuine economic origin — followed by the United States, the Netherlands, Japan and the UK. Top recipient sectors are services, computer software and hardware, trading, telecommunications, automobiles and construction; top recipient states are Maharashtra, Karnataka, Gujarat and Delhi, a concentration that reinforces regional inequality.

Overseas investment by Indian firms is the mirror image, governed by the Overseas Investment Rules and Regulations, 2022, which simplified the earlier ODI framework, clarified round-tripping and introduced the concept of "strategic sector." Indian outbound investment goes largely to Singapore, the US, the UK, the Netherlands and the UAE, in energy, pharmaceuticals, IT and automobiles.

5. Regulation, Taxation and the Reform Agenda

FEMA, 1999 replaced the draconian FERA, 1973, and the shift is conceptually important: FERA treated foreign exchange as a scarce resource and violations as criminal offences with the burden of proof on the accused; FEMA treats exchange as a manageable asset and violations as civil contraventions attracting monetary penalties, with the burden on the enforcement agency. The philosophy moved from conservation and control to facilitation. Complementing it, the Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002 handles the criminal dimension.

  • Taxation issues that recur: the Vodafone retrospective taxation episode and its damage to investor confidence, resolved by the Taxation Laws (Amendment) Act, 2021, which withdrew retrospective application
  • GAAR, the General Anti-Avoidance Rule targeting impermissible avoidance arrangements lacking commercial substance
  • advance pricing agreements and safe harbour rules to reduce transfer pricing disputes
  • the withdrawal of the equalisation levy
  • and the OECD/G20 two-pillar global tax deal — Pillar One reallocating taxing rights to market jurisdictions, which benefits large-market countries such as India, and Pillar Two setting a global minimum corporate tax of 15% to end the race to the bottom.
  • Investment treaties. India terminated most of its old Bilateral Investment Treaties after a series of adverse investor-state arbitration awards and adopted the Model BIT of 2016. Its defining features: an enterprise-based rather than asset-based definition of investment, excluding portfolio investment
  • the requirement to exhaust local remedies for five years before initiating international arbitration
  • the omission of the most-favoured-nation clause
  • a narrowed fair and equitable treatment standard replaced by a customary-international-law minimum standard
  • and carve-outs preserving the state's right to regulate in taxation, public health and the environment. Critics argue the model is so protective of the state that it deters treaty partners
  • defenders point to the fiscal exposure of unfettered investor-state dispute settlement.
  • Reform priorities: stable and predictable tax administration
  • faster contract enforcement and arbitration
  • land and labour reform at the state level
  • deepening domestic component manufacturing so that FDI produces value addition rather than assembly
  • single-window clearance through the National Single Window System
  • and plurilateral engagement to embed India in global value chains, since the empirical evidence is that FDI follows trade integration rather than preceding it.

Trap watch. The FDI threshold is 10% equity with lasting interest; below that it is FPI. FDI is non-debt-creating. Press Note 3 (2020) requires government approval for investment from land-border-sharing countries. Print media and news broadcasting have the lowest caps at 26% by government route; insurance companies are 74% while intermediaries are 100%. The FIPB was abolished in 2017. FERA violations were criminal, FEMA contraventions are civil. Mauritius and Singapore top the source list because of treaty structuring, not real economic origin. The Model BIT 2016 drops MFN and requires five years' exhaustion of local remedies. Pillar Two sets a 15% global minimum tax.
