Services and Infrastructure — Detailed Notes

Services engine + infrastructure pipeline. Pipeline-number and scheme-name recall.

1. Services economy

  • ~55% of GVA; IT-BPM ~7.5-8% of GDP, exports ~$200bn (NASSCOM industry body); GCC boom (1,600+ centres); startups (3rd-largest ecosystem band); ONDC open network.
  • Tourism: Swadesh Darshan 2.0 (destinations) + PRASHAD (pilgrimage); medical-value travel; hospitality rebound. Logistics: cost ~13-14% → 7.5% target; LPI 2023: India 38th; LEADS = domestic survey; PM Gati Shakti multimodal layer.

  • Transport: DFCs Eastern + Western (largely operational), Bharatmala, UDAN routes, Vadhavan port (2024), metro expansion (25+ cities), NW-1 Haldia-Varanasi cargo-cruise.
  • Energy-urban: PM Surya Ghar rooftop (2024; 1 crore homes), PM-KUSUM pumps, RDSS (2021 DISCOM reform), green corridors + OSOWOG, AMRUT 2.0 + SBM-U 2.0, PM-eBus Sewa (10,000), SVAMITVA property cards, CCTS carbon market (2023 framework).

6. The Services Sector: India's Growth Engine and Its Paradox

Services contribute roughly 55% of India's Gross Value Added but employ only about 30% of the workforce. That mismatch is the defining paradox: India leapfrogged from agriculture to services without the labour-absorbing manufacturing stage, so the sector that generates most output absorbs relatively few workers, and those it absorbs cluster at the two extremes — high-productivity IT and finance at one end, low-productivity retail, domestic work and personal services at the other, with a thin middle.

Composition, in descending order of contribution: trade, hotels, transport and communication; financial, real estate and professional services; and public administration and defence. Within these, the export champions are software and IT-enabled services, business and professional services and travel and transport. India's services exports and the associated remittances together finance most of the merchandise trade deficit, which is why the services surplus is the single most important stabiliser in the external accounts.

Why services grew so fast. Explanations to marshal: liberalisation and the removal of licensing; the English-language advantage and a large pool of technically trained graduates produced by the IITs and engineering colleges; the time-zone arbitrage that allowed round-the-clock delivery to the United States; telecommunications reform and undersea cable capacity that collapsed the cost of remote delivery; and the splintering of services, whereby activities once bundled inside manufacturing firms — design, logistics, accounting, legal — became separately tradable. Note also the measurement caveat that some of the apparent rise reflects outsourcing of functions previously counted as manufacturing.

IT and IT-enabled services have moved up the value chain from body-shopping and Y2K remediation, through application maintenance and BPO, to platform engineering, cloud, cybersecurity, analytics and now AI services. The newest phase is the Global Capability Centre: multinationals establishing captive centres in India that perform core R&D, product management and finance rather than back-office tasks. Challenges: dependence on the US and Europe, visa restrictions such as H-1B tightening, automation eroding the low-end pyramid, protectionism, and the skills mismatch documented by industry employability surveys. Other services worth naming with their policy issues: tourism (high employment multiplier, foreign exchange earner, constrained by infrastructure and seasonality; schemes Swadesh Darshan, PRASHAD, Dekho Apna Desh); medical value travel; higher education exports; retail and e-commerce with the ONDC intervention; logistics; the gig and platform economy, now given statutory recognition and aggregator-funded social security under the Code on Social Security, 2020; and real estate and construction, the largest employer after agriculture, regulated by RERA, 2016.

7. Infrastructure: Transport and Energy

Infrastructure is the classic case of a public good with large externalities and lumpy, long-gestation investment, which is why the state either provides it or must design contracts to attract private capital.

Roads carry about 65% of freight and 90% of passenger traffic. The hierarchy runs National Highways (about 2% of length but 40% of traffic), State Highways, and district and rural roads. Programmes: the Golden Quadrilateral and North-South/East-West corridors under NHDP; Bharatmala for economic corridors, border and coastal roads; PMGSY for all-weather rural connectivity, whose evaluation evidence on market access and school attendance is strong. Contract models are examinable: BOT-Toll (private party takes traffic risk), BOT-Annuity (government pays fixed annuities, so no traffic risk to the developer), EPC (government funds fully, contractor only builds), and the Hybrid Annuity Model, where the government funds 40% during construction and the balance is paid as annuities — designed precisely to revive private participation after BOT-Toll projects stalled on over-optimistic traffic forecasts.

Railways — among the world's largest networks, undergoing electrification, gauge conversion, station redevelopment, Vande Bharat trainsets and the Dedicated Freight Corridors (Eastern and Western), whose purpose is to decongest passenger lines and reverse the loss of freight share to road. The structural problem is cross-subsidisation: freight tariffs are kept high to subsidise passenger fares, which drives freight to road and worsens the operating ratio. The merger of the Railway Budget with the Union Budget in 2017 followed the Bibek Debroy Committee's reform agenda.

Ports and shipping — about 95% of trade by volume moves by sea. Sagarmala covers port modernisation, connectivity, port-led industrialisation and coastal community development; the Major Port Authorities Act, 2021 replaced the Trusts with Authorities and greater autonomy; the Maritime India Vision 2030 and Maritime Amrit Kaal Vision 2047 set the targets. Note the distinction between major ports (under the Centre) and non-major ports (under the states), and the promotion of inland waterways under the National Waterways Act with NW-1 on the Ganga as the flagship. Civil aviation — the UDAN regional connectivity scheme with viability gap funding and capped fares, airport privatisation, and the Cape Town Convention issue in aircraft leasing.

Energy. India's installed capacity is over half non-fossil, well ahead of the Paris pledge timeline, with targets of 500 GW non-fossil capacity by 2030 and net zero by 2070. Note the crucial distinction between installed capacity and generation: coal still supplies about 70% of actual electricity because renewables have low capacity utilisation. The binding constraints are storage and grid balancing for intermittent sources, addressed through battery storage, pumped hydro and the Green Energy Corridor. Institutions and schemes: the International Solar Alliance, the National Green Hydrogen Mission, PM Surya Ghar for rooftop solar, PM-KUSUM for solar pumps, UJALA, Saubhagya and DDUGJY for access, and the One Sun One World One Grid initiative. The persistent weak link is the financial health of DISCOMs — AT&C losses, unpaid state subsidies and populist free power — addressed successively by UDAY and the Revamped Distribution Sector Scheme.

8. Financing Infrastructure and the PPP Framework

India's infrastructure investment need is estimated at over a trillion dollars, articulated through the National Infrastructure Pipeline. Financing instruments to know:

  • PM Gati Shakti — a GIS-based digital platform integrating the plans of sixteen ministries to end siloed, uncoordinated project execution, complemented by the National Logistics Policy.
  • National Monetisation Pipeline — leasing brownfield, revenue-generating public assets (roads, transmission lines, railway stations) to private operators for an upfront payment while ownership is retained by the government and the asset returns at the end of the concession. It is emphatically not privatisation, a distinction frequently tested.
  • NaBFID — the National Bank for Financing Infrastructure and Development, a statutory development finance institution created in 2021 to supply long-tenor debt, solving the asset-liability mismatch that made commercial banks unsuitable infrastructure lenders and contributed to the twin balance sheet problem.
  • InvITs and REITs — trusts that pool investor money into operating infrastructure or real estate assets, offering liquidity and recycling capital for developers.
  • Municipal bonds, viability gap funding, sovereign wealth and pension fund investment with tax exemption, and green bonds including the sovereign green bond.

PPPs were reviewed by the Kelkar Committee (2015), which held that risk should be allocated to the party best able to manage it, criticised the practice of loading all risk onto the private partner, recommended against renegotiation being treated as failure, urged an independent regulator and dispute-resolution mechanism, and warned against using PPPs merely to shift debt off the government's books. Recurrent problems: land acquisition and clearance delays, over-optimistic traffic and demand forecasts, aggressive bidding followed by stress, contract enforcement and arbitration delay, and stranded assets. The counter-instruments are the IBC, arbitration reform, and the shift to HAM and EPC where private risk appetite is absent.

Trap watch. Services are about 55% of GVA but only about 30% of employment. Installed non-fossil capacity has crossed half, yet coal still supplies around 70% of generation — capacity is not generation. Asset monetisation transfers operating rights, not ownership. In HAM the government funds 40% during construction; in BOT-Annuity the developer bears no traffic risk. Major ports are central, non-major ports are with the states. Railways cross-subsidise passengers from freight. NaBFID is a statutory DFI, not a bank. Roughly 95% of trade by volume and 70% by value moves by sea.
