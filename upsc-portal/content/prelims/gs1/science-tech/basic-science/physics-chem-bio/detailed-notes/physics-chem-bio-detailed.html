Basic Science: Physics, Chemistry, Biology — Detailed Notes

Applied-concept anchors: physics of launches-navigation, chemistry of energy-tech, biology of genomics. Award line-items included.

1. Physics anchors

  • Launch physics: multi-staging (PSLV = solid 1st + liquid 2nd + solid strapons + liquid 4th; GSLV/LVM3 = cryogenic upper stage); orbit set (LEO ~400 km, MEO GNSS, GEO comms, L1 halo — Aditya-L1 at 1.5 million km sunward); RLV-LEX autonomous landing demos (2023-24).
  • Navigation-positioning: NavIC/IRNSS (7 satellites; L5+S bands), GAGAN (aviation SBAS with AAI)
  • National Quantum Mission (2023; ~₹6,000 cr; qubits, secure comms, sensing)
  • semiconductors: India Semiconductor Mission (Tata fab Dholera, Micron ATMP Sanand)
  • fusion: ITER (India partner, France)
  • LIGO-India (Hingoli, approved 2023)
  • gravitational waves 2015 detection.
  • Fundamentals: SI seven base units
  • light-year vs AU
  • CMB = Big-Bang evidence
  • redshift-expansion.

4. Physics: Concepts That Recur in the Examination

UPSC tests physics through everyday phenomena and applications rather than derivation, so the priority is the mechanism behind an observation.

Mechanics. Newton's three laws — inertia, force as the rate of change of momentum, and action-reaction. Momentum is conserved in all collisions; kinetic energy is conserved only in elastic ones. Centripetal force acts toward the centre and is a real force; centrifugal force is a pseudo-force appearing only in a rotating frame — the banking of roads and railway curves, and the flattening of Earth at the poles, follow from this. Escape velocity is about 11.2 km per second and is independent of the mass of the escaping body. In free fall and in orbit, apparent weightlessness arises because the body and its surroundings accelerate together, not because gravity is absent — a favourite misconception. Surface tension explains spherical droplets, capillary rise and the working of detergents, which reduce it; viscosity explains terminal velocity; Bernoulli's principle, that pressure falls where flow speed rises, explains aircraft lift, the atomiser and the swing of a cricket ball.

Heat and thermodynamics. Distinguish heat (energy in transit) from temperature (a measure of average kinetic energy). Transfer occurs by conduction, convection and radiation; a thermos flask defeats all three through a vacuum, silvering and an insulating stopper. Water's anomalous expansion below 4 °C — where it is densest — is why lakes freeze from the top and aquatic life survives. Latent heat explains why steam burns are worse than boiling-water burns and why evaporation cools. The first law is conservation of energy; the second law introduces entropy and the impossibility of complete conversion of heat into work.

Waves, sound and light. Sound is a longitudinal mechanical wave that cannot travel through vacuum and moves fastest in solids. Ultrasound above 20 kHz is used in sonography, SONAR and cleaning; infrasound below 20 Hz is produced by earthquakes and elephants. The Doppler effect explains the changing pitch of a passing siren and, in astronomy, the redshift that evidences an expanding universe. Light phenomena and their causes: scattering — Rayleigh scattering, inversely proportional to the fourth power of wavelength, makes the sky blue and the setting Sun red, while larger particles scatter all wavelengths and make clouds white; total internal reflection underlies optical fibres, mirages and the sparkle of diamonds; dispersion with internal reflection produces the rainbow; diffraction and interference explain the colours of soap films and CDs; polarisation confirms light is transverse and is used in sunglasses and LCDs. Defects of vision — myopia corrected by a concave lens, hypermetropia by convex, presbyopia by bifocals, astigmatism by cylindrical lenses.

  • Electricity and magnetism. Ohm's law
  • series versus parallel circuits
  • the heating effect behind fuses and filaments
  • Faraday's electromagnetic induction as the basis of generators and transformers
  • the reason for high-voltage AC transmission — power loss equals I²R, so raising voltage lowers current and cuts losses, and AC is chosen because transformers can step it up and down. Superconductivity gives zero resistance and the Meissner effect below a critical temperature, applied in MRI magnets and maglev.
  • Modern physics. The photoelectric effect establishing light's particle nature
  • wave-particle duality
  • the electromagnetic spectrum in order of increasing frequency — radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray, gamma — with the applications of each
  • radioactivity and half-life, with alpha, beta and gamma emissions of increasing penetration
  • the use of isotopes such as carbon-14 for dating, cobalt-60 in radiotherapy and food irradiation, and iodine-131 for the thyroid
  • LASER, a coherent monochromatic beam produced by stimulated emission
  • and quantum computing's qubits exploiting superposition and entanglement.

5. Chemistry: Materials, Reactions and Applications

  • Atomic structure and the periodic table. Atomic number defines the element
  • isotopes differ in neutrons, isobars have equal mass number, isotones equal neutrons. Across a period, atomic size decreases and electronegativity and ionisation energy increase
  • down a group the reverse. Noble gases are inert owing to complete octets
  • alkali metals are the most reactive metals and halogens the most reactive non-metals.
  • Bonding determines properties, which is the examinable link: ionic compounds are high-melting, brittle and conduct in molten or aqueous state
  • covalent compounds are generally low-melting and non-conducting
  • metallic bonding with delocalised electrons gives conductivity, malleability and ductility. Hydrogen bonding explains water's unusually high boiling point, ice's lower density and the structure of DNA and proteins. Allotropes of carbon illustrate structure-property relations perfectly: diamond, tetrahedral and the hardest natural substance yet a non-conductor
  • graphite, layered, soft, slippery and conducting because of one delocalised electron per atom
  • fullerenes, graphene (a single atomic layer, exceptionally strong and conducting) and carbon nanotubes.

Acids, bases and everyday chemistry. The pH scale from 0 to 14, with 7 neutral; each unit is a tenfold change in hydrogen ion concentration. Applications: antacids neutralising stomach hydrochloric acid, the pH of blood held near 7.4 by buffers, soil pH correction by liming, tooth decay below pH 5.5. Common compounds worth knowing by formula and use — baking soda (sodium bicarbonate), washing soda, bleaching powder, plaster of Paris and gypsum, quicklime and slaked lime. Soaps versus detergents: soaps are sodium or potassium salts of fatty acids and form scum in hard water; detergents are sulphonates that work in hard water but are less biodegradable. Corrosion of iron requires both oxygen and moisture and is prevented by galvanising, painting or alloying into stainless steel.

Polymers and materials: thermoplastics soften on heating and are recyclable, thermosetting plastics do not; biodegradable and compostable plastics such as PLA; natural and synthetic rubber with vulcanisation; and composites. Nanotechnology — materials below 100 nanometres exhibit changed properties owing to a very high surface-area-to-volume ratio and quantum effects, applied in drug delivery, catalysis, sensors and water purification, with toxicity and regulation as the concerns. Catalysis, including enzymes as biological catalysts, lowers activation energy without being consumed.

Environmental chemistry links directly to ecology questions: ozone depletion by chlorofluorocarbons through chlorine free radicals, addressed by the Montreal Protocol and the Kigali Amendment on HFCs; photochemical smog from nitrogen oxides and volatile organic compounds in sunlight, producing ground-level ozone and PAN; acid rain from sulphur and nitrogen oxides; eutrophication; heavy metal poisoning — mercury causing Minamata disease, cadmium Itai-Itai, arsenic and fluoride in groundwater, and lead affecting neurological development.

6. Biology: Cells, Systems and Human Health

  • Cell biology. Prokaryotes lack a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles
  • eukaryotes possess both. Organelle functions are directly examinable: mitochondria as the powerhouse producing ATP and possessing their own DNA inherited maternally
  • ribosomes for protein synthesis
  • chloroplasts for photosynthesis
  • lysosomes as the "suicide bags" containing hydrolytic enzymes
  • the Golgi apparatus for packaging
  • the endoplasmic reticulum, rough and smooth. Mitosis produces two identical diploid cells for growth and repair
  • meiosis produces four genetically varied haploid gametes, and crossing over during it is the source of genetic variation.

Genetics. DNA's double helix with complementary base pairing — adenine with thymine, guanine with cytosine; RNA substitutes uracil and is single-stranded. The central dogma runs DNA to RNA to protein by transcription and translation. Mendel's laws of dominance, segregation and independent assortment. Sex determination in humans is XX female and XY male, with the father determining the sex of the child. Know the sex-linked disorders haemophilia and colour blindness, expressed more often in males; the autosomal recessive conditions sickle-cell anaemia and thalassaemia, both prevalent in India; and the chromosomal disorders Down syndrome (trisomy 21), Turner and Klinefelter syndromes. Modern tools: PCR for amplification, recombinant DNA technology, CRISPR-Cas9 for precise gene editing with its associated ethical debate, gene therapy, and mRNA vaccine platforms.

Human physiology in outline: the digestive system and the roles of saliva, gastric acid, bile as an emulsifier and pancreatic enzymes; the double circulation of blood through a four-chambered heart, blood groups and the Rh factor with its relevance to erythroblastosis fetalis; the respiratory system and gas exchange in the alveoli; the nephron as the functional unit of the kidney; the neuron, reflex arc and the divisions of the nervous system; and the endocrine glands with their hormones and deficiency diseases — insulin from the pancreas and diabetes, thyroxine and goitre or cretinism, adrenaline as the emergency hormone, growth hormone and its excess or deficiency.

Nutrition and disease. Vitamins and deficiency conditions: A and night blindness, B1 and beriberi, B3 and pellagra, B12 and pernicious anaemia, C and scurvy, D and rickets or osteomalacia, K and impaired clotting; minerals iron, iodine, calcium and zinc. Distinguish communicable diseases by pathogen — viral: influenza, dengue, chikungunya, hepatitis, HIV, COVID-19, polio, measles, rabies; bacterial: tuberculosis, cholera, typhoid, tetanus, leprosy, plague; protozoal: malaria, kala-azar, amoebiasis; fungal: ringworm; helminthic: filariasis. Note that antibiotics act only on bacteria, and their misuse drives antimicrobial resistance, a major current policy concern. Vector-borne diseases and their vectors — Anopheles for malaria, Aedes for dengue, chikungunya and Zika, Culex for filariasis and Japanese encephalitis, sandfly for kala-azar. Immunity — innate versus acquired, active versus passive; vaccines confer active immunity, antisera passive. Non-communicable diseases — cardiovascular disease, diabetes, cancer and chronic respiratory disease — now account for the majority of Indian deaths, driven by tobacco, diet, inactivity and pollution.

Trap watch. Weightlessness in orbit is free fall, not the absence of gravity. Escape velocity does not depend on the escaping body's mass. Sound needs a medium; light does not. Rayleigh scattering varies inversely with the fourth power of wavelength. Graphite conducts, diamond does not, though both are pure carbon. Mitochondrial DNA is maternally inherited. The father determines a child's sex. Antibiotics do not act on viruses. Vaccines give active immunity, antisera passive. Water is densest at 4 °C. Momentum is conserved in all collisions; kinetic energy only in elastic ones.
