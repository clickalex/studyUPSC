Climatology — Detailed Notes

Atmosphere layers, circulation, cyclones, Koppen-in-India, and monsoon mechanics — the prelims workhorse.

1. Atmosphere structure

  • Layers: troposphere (0-12 km, weather, lapse ~6.5C/km) → tropopause → stratosphere (calm; ozone layer 15-35 km; jets sit near its base) → mesosphere (meteors burn) → thermosphere-ionosphere (aurora, radio) → exosphere.
  • Insolation budget: albedo ~30%, absorbed ~70%; heat transfer (conduction-convection-advection); temperature inversion types (radiation — valley fog, frontal, subsidence, advection). Greenhouse effect basics; aerosols-CCN role in cloud physics.

2. Pressure belts & winds

  • Belts: equatorial low (ITCZ, doldrums), subtropical highs (horse latitudes ~30), subpolar lows, polar highs; seasonal migration follows the sun (ITCZ shifts over India in monsoon). Winds: planetary (trades NE/SE, westerlies, polar easterlies), periodic (monsoon, land-sea breeze, mountain-valley), local (loo, kalbaisakhi, mango showers, mistral, bora, chinook, foehn, sirocco, harmattan).
  • Jet streams: subtropical westerly jet (winter, drives western disturbances), tropical easterly jet (summer, near 15N, upper-air monsoon aid); Rossby waves.

3. Cyclones & precipitation

  • Tropical cyclones: warm-core, no fronts, eye-eye-wall; form 5-20 lat (Coriolis minimum at equator); conditions: SST >27C, low shear, pre-existing disturbance, upper divergence; seasonal peaks (pre/post-monsoon Bay of Bengal dominance); IMD naming list (13 countries, 2020 revision). Temperate cyclones: front-based (warm-cold-occluded), west-to-east, winter western disturbances → NW India rain-snow.
  • Precipitation forms: convectional (4pm equatorial), orographic (Cherrapunji-Mawsynram relief trap), frontal/cyclonic; humidity terms (RH, dew point); fog types (radiation, advection).

4. Koppen-in-India & monsoon

  • India Koppen: Am (Malabar coast), Aw (most of peninsula-interior), BWhw/BShw (NW interior, rain shadows), Cwg (Gangetic plain), Cwb (NE hills-shillong), Dfc (Arunachal high altitude), E (Ladakh).
  • Monsoon mechanics: differential heating + ITCZ shift + Tibetan plateau heating + jet-stream reversal + Somali current + Indian Ocean Dipole; onset Kerala ~1 Jun (±1 week), MOK withdrawal Oct; breaks-monsoon lows; ENSO-IOD-MJO modulators; monsoon sub-divisions (IMD 36).

MCQ hooks

  • Ozone in stratosphere (not mesosphere); lapse 6.5C/km normal.
  • Subtropical jet = winter western disturbances; tropical easterly jet = summer monsoon helper.
  • Tropical cyclone formation minimum ~5 lat (Coriolis zero at equator).
  • Koppen codes in India: Am-Malabar, Aw-peninsula, Cwg-plain, Dfc-NE, E-Ladakh.
  • Western disturbances = winter NW India rain (rabi aid).
  • Chinook-foehn = warm descent winds; mistral = cold north wind (France).

6. Atmosphere: Composition, Structure and Heat Budget

Dry air is about 78% nitrogen and 21% oxygen, with argon and carbon dioxide making up most of the remainder. The trace constituents matter disproportionately: water vapour (up to 4%, the most important greenhouse gas and the source of all precipitation), carbon dioxide, ozone and aerosols, which serve as condensation nuclei.

Vertical structure, defined by the temperature gradient:

  • Troposphere — to about 18 km at the equator and 8 km at the poles (thicker where convection is stronger). Temperature falls with height at the normal lapse rate of about 6.5 °C per km. All weather occurs here. The upper boundary is the tropopause.
  • Stratosphere — temperature rises with height because ozone absorbs ultraviolet radiation. Stable and cloud-free, hence favoured for jet aviation. Contains the ozone layer at roughly 15–35 km.
  • Mesosphere — temperature falls again to the coldest point in the atmosphere at the mesopause; meteors burn here.
  • Thermosphere / Ionosphere — temperature rises steeply; ionised layers reflect radio waves, enabling long-distance communication; auroras occur here.
  • Exosphere — merges into space.

Temperature inversion is the reversal of the normal lapse rate, with warm air overlying cold. It requires long nights, clear skies, dry calm air and snow-covered or radiating surfaces. Valley inversion traps cold dense air in basins, producing frost and fog — and, in industrial valleys, severe pollution episodes. This is the mechanism behind Delhi's winter smog and, historically, the Donora and London disasters.

Insolation and the heat budget. Incoming solar radiation is short-wave; outgoing terrestrial radiation is long-wave. The atmosphere is largely transparent to short-wave but absorbs long-wave — the basis of the greenhouse effect. Of incoming radiation, roughly 35% is reflected (the albedo), 14% absorbed by the atmosphere and 51% absorbed at the surface; the Earth radiates an equal amount back, keeping the budget balanced. Heat is transferred by conduction, convection (vertical), advection (horizontal — the mechanism of the loo) and radiation. Factors controlling temperature distribution: latitude, altitude, distance from the sea (continentality giving extreme ranges), ocean currents, prevailing winds, slope and aspect, and cloud cover.

7. Pressure Belts, Winds and the General Circulation

Four pressure belts occur in each hemisphere. The equatorial low (the doldrums, thermally induced by intense heating and convection); the subtropical high at about 30° (dynamically induced by descending air, home to the horse latitudes and the world's great deserts); the subpolar low at about 60° (also dynamic, where warm and cold air converge along the polar front); and the polar high (thermal). Recognising which belts are thermal and which dynamic is a standard question.

Pressure gradient force drives wind from high to low pressure; the Coriolis force, arising from Earth's rotation, deflects it right in the northern hemisphere and left in the southern (Ferrel's Law), is zero at the equator and maximum at the poles — which is why cyclones cannot form within about 5° of the equator. Where these two balance in the free atmosphere, the result is the geostrophic wind, blowing parallel to the isobars. Buys Ballot's Law: standing with your back to the wind in the northern hemisphere, low pressure lies to your left.

Planetary winds: trade winds (north-easterly in the northern hemisphere) converging at the ITCZ; the westerlies, strongest in the southern hemisphere where land is absent — the Roaring Forties, Furious Fifties and Shrieking Sixties; and the polar easterlies. The three-cell model comprises the thermally direct Hadley cell, the indirect Ferrel cell and the Polar cell. Jet streams are fast, narrow ribbons of upper-air westerlies — the subtropical and polar front jets, plus the seasonal tropical easterly jet that strengthens the Indian monsoon.

Local winds recur as pairings: Loo (India, hot dry), Foehn (Alps) and Chinook (Rockies, "snow-eater") as warm descending leeward winds, Mistral (France, cold) and Bora (Adriatic), Sirocco (Sahara to Mediterranean, dust-laden), Harmattan (West Africa, the "doctor"), Khamsin (Egypt), Santa Ana (California), Berg (South Africa) and Norwester (New Zealand). Land and sea breezes and mountain-valley (anabatic and katabatic) winds are the diurnal local circulations.

8. Humidity, Clouds, Precipitation and Air Masses

Absolute humidity is the actual mass of water vapour per unit volume; relative humidity is the ratio of actual to saturation vapour content at that temperature, expressed as a percentage. Because saturation capacity rises with temperature, relative humidity falls when air is heated even though the vapour content is unchanged — a favourite conceptual question. The dew point is the temperature at which saturation is reached; below it, condensation forms dew, frost, fog or cloud. Radiation fog forms on clear calm nights, advection fog when warm moist air moves over a cold surface (the Grand Banks), and smog is fog mixed with pollutants.

Clouds are classified by altitude and form: high cirrus, cirrostratus, cirrocumulus (ice crystals, wispy); middle altostratus, altocumulus; low stratus, stratocumulus, nimbostratus (the steady-rain cloud); and the vertically developed cumulus and cumulonimbus — the thunderstorm cloud responsible for lightning, hail and tornadoes.

Precipitation by mechanism: convectional (intense heating, convection, heavy afternoon downpours — characteristic of the equatorial belt, giving the "four o'clock rain"); orographic or relief (air forced to rise over a barrier, cooling adiabatically and raining on the windward side, leaving a rain shadow to the lee — the Western Ghats and the Deccan interior); and cyclonic or frontal (warm air rising over cold along a front). Adiabatic temperature change — cooling on ascent through expansion without heat exchange — is the underlying physics, with the dry adiabatic lapse rate about 10 °C per km and the saturated rate lower because condensation releases latent heat.

Air masses are large bodies of uniform temperature and humidity, classified by source: maritime or continental, and tropical, polar or arctic (mT, cT, mP, cP). Fronts form where they meet: a warm front has a gentle slope and produces prolonged light rain from stratiform cloud; a cold front is steep and produces short intense rain from cumulonimbus; an occluded front forms when the faster cold front overtakes the warm; a stationary front does not move.

9. Cyclones and Climate Classification

Tropical cyclones require sea-surface temperature above about 27 °C, a large warm ocean expanse, the Coriolis force (hence not within 5° of the equator), low vertical wind shear, pre-existing low-level disturbance and upper-level divergence. Structure: a calm, warm, subsiding eye; the eyewall of maximum winds and rainfall; and spiralling rain bands. They weaken rapidly on landfall as the moisture and latent heat source is removed. Regional names — cyclone in the Indian Ocean, hurricane in the Atlantic and eastern Pacific, typhoon in the western Pacific, willy-willy in Australia. The chief killer is the storm surge, not wind, which is why low-lying deltaic coasts such as Odisha, Bengal and Bangladesh are worst affected. The Bay of Bengal is more cyclogenic than the Arabian Sea, though Arabian Sea activity is rising with warming.

Temperate (extratropical) cyclones differ fundamentally: they form along the polar front through the interaction of contrasting air masses, occur in mid-latitudes in winter, cover a far larger area, move west to east, have fronts and no eye, and produce moderate prolonged precipitation. India's western disturbances are of this type. Being able to contrast the two systems point by point is a standard mains and prelims requirement.

Köppen's classification uses letters: A tropical, B dry, C warm temperate, D cold snow-forest, E polar, with a second letter for precipitation seasonality (f no dry season, m monsoon, w dry winter, s dry summer) and a third for temperature severity. It is empirical, based on temperature and precipitation values correlated with vegetation; Thornthwaite's scheme, by contrast, is based on precipitation effectiveness and potential evapotranspiration, and is considered more rational.

Trap watch. Temperature rises with height in the stratosphere and thermosphere, falls in the troposphere and mesosphere; the mesopause is the coldest point. Subtropical high and subpolar low are dynamically induced; equatorial low and polar high are thermal. Coriolis force is zero at the equator, so cyclones cannot form there. Relative humidity falls as temperature rises with vapour content constant. Tropical cyclones have an eye and no fronts; temperate cyclones have fronts and no eye. Orographic rain falls on the windward side. The tropopause is higher at the equator than at the poles.
