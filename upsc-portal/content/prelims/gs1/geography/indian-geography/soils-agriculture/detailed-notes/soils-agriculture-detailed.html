Soils, Agriculture and Cropping — Detailed Notes

ICAR soil map + crop-calendar + policy stack. Crop-region pairs and scheme numbers are the ask.

1. Soils (ICAR 8-fold)

  • Alluvial (largest ~43%): Indus-Ganga-Brahmaputra-coasts; khadar (new, fine, flood-refreshed) vs bangar (old, kankar); very fertile but zinc-deficiency common.
  • Black/regur (~15%): Deccan basalt (MH-MP-KA-TS); cotton-sugarcane; moisture-retentive, self-ploughing (crack-swell), poor in N-P but rich in Fe-Mg-Al; sticky when wet.
  • Red (~18%): crystalline weathering (eastern-southern plateau); fine for millets-pulses with inputs. Laterite: intense-leach product (Western Ghats, NE, Chotanagpur); poor fertility — tea-coffee-cashew-casuarina; brick-making.
  • Arid: west Rajasthan-Kutch-Gujarat north; high Ca-kankar, wind erosion; saline-usara: UP-west, Haryana, Punjab canal-tails (waterlogging + evaporation). Peaty: Kerala backwaters (high organic, acidic); forest/mountain: Himalaya slopes (orchards).

2. Cropping seasons & crop pairs

  • Kharif (Jun-Oct): rice (W Bengal-UP-Punjab-AP; jute = WB 'golden fibre'), cotton (black-soil belt), bajra-jowar-maize, groundnut, tur. Rabi (Oct-Mar): wheat (UP-MP-Punjab-HR), mustard, gram, barley. Zaid: watermelon-cucumber-fodder.
  • Plantation-exports: tea (Assam-Darjeeling-Nilgiri), coffee (Karnataka ~70%; arabica-robusta), rubber (Kerala), spices (Kerala-TN), sugarcane (UP-MH), oilseeds (MP-RJ-GJ; mustard-soybean-groundnut), cotton (GJ-MH-TS).
  • Concepts: cropping intensity (above 150% nationally), shifting cultivation (jhum-NE), plantation economics, contract farming debates, natural farming (Andhra model), zero-budget (Subhash Palekar).

  • MSP: CACP recommends (22 crops plus copra), procurement via FCI-NAFED-NCCF; PM-AASHA (2018: PMSSY price-support, PDPS). 6. Soil Formation and Profile

Pedogenesis is controlled by five factors: parent material, climate (the most influential at continental scale, through temperature and rainfall), relief, organisms and time. Two opposed processes dominate in India. Laterisation occurs under high temperature and heavy seasonal rainfall, leaching away silica and bases and leaving iron and aluminium oxides — producing acidic, infertile red-hued soils. Calcification occurs in dry regions where evaporation exceeds precipitation, drawing calcium carbonate upward to form kankar hardpans. Salinisation and alkalisation result from over-irrigation with poor drainage, and gleisation from waterlogging.

The soil profile runs in horizons: O (organic litter), A (topsoil, humus-rich, zone of eluviation or leaching out), E (maximum eluviation), B (subsoil, zone of illuviation or accumulation), C (weathered parent material) and R (bedrock). In-situ or residual soils form in place (black, red, laterite); transported soils are deposited by agents — alluvial by rivers, loess by wind, glacial till by ice.

Properties that determine fertility: texture (the proportion of sand, silt and clay — loam being the ideal balance), structure (aggregation of particles), porosity and permeability, pH, cation exchange capacity, and soil organic carbon. Note carefully that fertile soil is not necessarily the most productive: black soil is chemically rich but hard to work when wet, while alluvial soil combines moderate fertility with excellent workability.

8. Soil Degradation and Conservation

Soil erosion is the leading form of degradation. Types: sheet erosion (uniform removal of topsoil, insidious because it is invisible), rill, gully (the advanced stage producing the Chambal badlands and ravines of the Yamuna, Chambal and Mahi), stream bank, and wind erosion (deflation, saltation and surface creep) in arid tracts. Other degradation: waterlogging and salinisation in canal commands, desertification, acidification, nutrient mining where crops remove more than is replenished, and loss of soil organic carbon, which Indian soils suffer acutely.

Conservation measures should be presented as mechanical and biological. Mechanical: contour bunding and contour ploughing across the slope, terracing on steep hillsides, gully plugging and check dams, and shelter belts of trees against wind. Biological: crop rotation including legumes, strip cropping, cover crops and mulching, agroforestry and afforestation, and zero or minimum tillage which preserves soil structure and organic carbon. Regulating shifting cultivation (jhum in the north-east, podu in Andhra) and overgrazing addresses the social drivers.

Policy instruments: the Soil Health Card scheme, providing farm-level nutrient recommendations to correct the NPK imbalance created by disproportionately subsidised urea; the Nutrient Based Subsidy regime; integrated nutrient management combining organic and inorganic sources; watershed programmes; the National Mission for Sustainable Agriculture under the NAPCC; and India's commitment to land degradation neutrality under the UNCCD, whose fourteenth Conference of Parties India hosted in New Delhi.

9. Soil-Crop-Region Linkages for the Exam

Most questions ultimately test the association between soil, climate and crop. Rehearse the reasoning rather than memorising pairs:

  • Cotton on black soil — high clay content retains the moisture that a long-duration crop needs through the dry spell; the self-mulching crack habit reduces evaporation loss.
  • Rice on alluvial and clayey soils — requires standing water, so impermeable subsoil is an advantage; needs high temperature and over 100 cm rainfall or assured irrigation.
  • Tea on laterite hill slopes — needs an acidic, well-drained soil, since stagnant water kills the roots; hence slope cultivation with shade trees.
  • Coffee on the Western Ghats slopes — well-drained loam rich in humus, with arabica on higher and robusta on lower elevations.
  • Groundnut and millets on red and sandy soils — tolerant of low fertility and moisture stress.
  • Sugarcane on alluvial and black soils — a long-duration crop needing deep fertile soil and heavy irrigation.

Two frameworks add analytical value in mains. Agro-climatic zones — the Planning Commission's fifteen zones based on soil, climate, rainfall and water resources, used to align cropping patterns with natural endowment. And the agro-ecological regions delineated by the National Bureau of Soil Survey and Land Use Planning, which combine soil, physiography and length of growing period.

Trap watch. Alluvial soil is the most extensive in India, not black soil. Black soil is rich in lime and iron but poor in nitrogen, phosphorus and organic matter. Laterite is acidic and infertile despite forming under heavy rainfall, because leaching removes the bases. Khadar is newer and more fertile than bhangar. Red colour comes from ferric oxide diffusion, not from high iron availability. Sheet erosion is the most widespread and least noticed. Regur is formed in situ from basalt, so it is a residual, not transported, soil.
