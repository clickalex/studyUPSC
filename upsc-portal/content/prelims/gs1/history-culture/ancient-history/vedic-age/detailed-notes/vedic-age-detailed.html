Vedic Age — Detailed Notes

Two Vedic ages, one text ladder. UPSC tests text→content, god→period, term→meaning.

1. Early Vedic / Rigvedic (c. 1500–1000 BCE)

  • Homeland: Sapta-Sindhu (Indus + Saraswati belt). Rigveda's oldest mandalas = II–VII (family books); X (Purusha sukta — varna origin).
  • Economy: pastoral — cow = wealth unit (go-dhana); godhuli = distance; agriculture minor; nishka = gold ornament (later coin-name).
  • Polity: tribe (jana) based; raja = war-leader elected-ish; sabha & samiti check king; officers: senani, gramani, purohita; bali voluntary tribute (no regular tax yet).
  • Society: dasa varna (early class colour), women visible (Lopamudra, Ghosha, Apala; no child marriage, widow could remarry).
  • Religion: personified nature — Indra (~250 hymns, war/rain), Varuna (rita), Agni (messenger), Soma; worship = hymn + sacrifice, no temples/idols.

2. Later Vedic (c. 1000–600 BCE)

  • Texts: Yajur, Sama, Atharva + Brahmanas + Aranyakas + early Upanishads; geography pushes east ( Videha, Kuru-Panchala).
  • Economy: iron (shyama ayas / krishna ayas) → plough, rice-wheat; occupations hereditary; nishka/satamana as value units.
  • Polity: janapadas; hereditary kingship; grand rites — rajasuya (consecration), vajapeya (chariot race), ashvamedha (imperial claim); sabha declines to elite council.
  • Society: varna hardens (Brahmana–Kshatriya–Vaishya–Shudra); 4 ashramas codified (Jabala Upanishad); women's status drops (no early-Vedic participation).
  • Religion: Prajapati (creator) tops; Rudra, Vishnu rise; sacrifice becomes mechanical (Brahmana literature = rule-books).
  • Reaction: Upanishads (c. 800–500 BCE) — atmabrahma monism (Yajnavalkya–Maitreyi dialogues).

MCQ hooks

  • Gayatri mantra = RV 3.62.10 (the Savitri; composed in the gayatri meter).
  • 'Ayas' = metal generally in RV; iron specifically appears later Vedic.
  • Upaveda pairs: Ayurveda–Rig, Dhanurveda–Yajur, Gandharvaveda–Sama, Shilpaveda–Atharva.
  • Purusha sukta (RV X) = first varna statement; Vaishya term appears first here.
  • Atharvaveda = folk beliefs, medicine, magic — 'Veda of magical formulae'.
  • Uttaramerur ≠ Vedic (that's Chola); Vedic assemblies = sabha/samiti/vidatha/gana.

6. Sources and the Problem of Chronology

The Vedic age is reconstructed almost entirely from literary sources, in contrast to the Harappan civilisation, which is known archaeologically. This asymmetry is itself examinable: we know Harappan drains but not their language; we know Vedic hymns but cannot securely identify their cities.

The corpus is arranged in a fixed hierarchy. The four Vedas — Rigveda (hymns of praise, the oldest, 10 mandalas, of which II–VII are the "family books" and the earliest, while I and X are later additions), Samaveda (melodies, the origin of Indian music), Yajurveda (sacrificial formulae, the first in prose, divided into Krishna and Shukla recensions) and Atharvaveda (charms, spells and folk beliefs — the most valuable for social history and the last to gain canonical status). Each Veda has four layers: the Samhita (collection), Brahmana (ritual prose commentary), Aranyaka ("forest books", mystical interpretation) and Upanishad (philosophical speculation, also called Vedanta, the end of the Veda). The six Vedangas — Shiksha, Kalpa, Vyakarana, Nirukta, Chhanda, Jyotisha — are auxiliary disciplines.

Key attributions worth memorising: the Gayatri mantra addressed to Savitri appears in Rigveda III (attributed to Vishvamitra); the Purusha Sukta describing the origin of the four varnas from the cosmic being is in Mandala X, hence late; the Nasadiya Sukta (creation hymn, expressing radical doubt) is also Mandala X; Satyameva Jayate is from the Mundaka Upanishad; and "Asato ma sadgamaya" is from the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad.

Archaeologically, the Early Vedic phase is loosely associated with Ochre Coloured Pottery and the Later Vedic with Painted Grey Ware (PGW), succeeded by Northern Black Polished Ware (NBPW) in the mahajanapada period. The PGW–iron correlation in the upper Ganga-Yamuna doab is the standard archaeological anchor for the Later Vedic.

7. Early (Rigvedic) Period, c. 1500–1000 BCE

Geography. The Rigvedic people occupied the Sapta Sindhava, the land of seven rivers, in the Punjab and eastern Afghanistan. The Nadi Sukta (Mandala X) lists the rivers; the Rigveda mentions the Indus (Sindhu) most frequently and eulogises the Saraswati as naditama, the foremost river. The Ganga is mentioned only once and the Yamuna thrice — proof of a western focus. Older river names to match: Vitasta (Jhelum), Askini (Chenab), Parushni (Ravi), Vipasa (Beas), Shutudri (Sutlej), Drishadvati, Kubha (Kabul), Suvastu (Swat), Gomati (Gomal).

Polity. Tribal and pastoral, not territorial. The unit was the jana (tribe), and the term janapada (territory) is absent. The chief was the rajan or gopati ("lord of cattle") — a war leader, not an absolute monarch, with no standing army and no regular taxation; voluntary offerings were called bali. Assemblies checked his power: the Sabha (a smaller body of elders and nobles), the Samiti (a larger popular assembly that could elect the king), and the less-attested Vidatha (the oldest, with military, religious and social functions in which women participated) and Gana. Officials: Purohita (chief priest; Vasishtha and Vishvamitra are the named rivals), Senani (army commander), Gramani (village head), Vrajapati (officer of pasture land), Spasa (spies). The Battle of Ten Kings (Dasarajna), fought on the Parushni, saw the Bharata chief Sudas, guided by Vasishtha, defeat a confederacy of ten tribes.

Economy. Predominantly pastoral. The centrality of cattle is proved by vocabulary: gavishti ("search for cows") means war, wealth is gomat, a wealthy man gomat, and the daughter is duhitr ("one who milks"). Agriculture was known but subsidiary (krishi, the plough langala, barley or yava being the only certain grain — rice and wheat are not mentioned in the Rigveda). Metals: ayas denotes copper or bronze; iron was unknown. There was no coinage, no writing, no urban life; Nishka was an ornament used in barter, not a coin.

Society. Patriarchal but relatively egalitarian. Varna originally denoted colour and distinguished Aryas from Dasas/Dasyus; occupational division was fluid, illustrated by the famous hymn in which a single family contains a poet, a physician and a corn-grinder. Women's position was comparatively good: they attended assemblies, could be educated, performed sacrifices with husbands, and women seers — Lopamudra, Ghosha, Apala, Vishvavara — composed hymns. There was no sati, no child marriage, and widow remarriage (niyoga) existed. Social units: kula (family), grama, vis, jana.

Religion. Worship of personified natural forces through sacrifice (yajna), without temples or idols. Indra (Purandara, breaker of forts) is the most invoked with about 250 hymns; Agni follows with about 200, as intermediary between gods and men; Varuna upholds Rita, the cosmic and moral order; Soma is the plant-deity of the sacred drink; Surya, Savitri, Usha, Rudra, Yama, Ashvins, Maruts and the Vishvedevas complete the pantheon. The aim was material and this-worldly — cattle, sons, health, victory — not liberation.

8. Later Vedic Period, c. 1000–600 BCE

Eastward expansion into the Ganga-Yamuna doab and beyond, enabled by iron (called krishna ayas or shyama ayas, first attested at Atranjikhera and Ataranjikhera) which cleared dense forest, and by the shift to rice (vrihi) and wheat cultivation. The centre of gravity moves to Kurukshetra and the Kuru-Panchala realm; the Satapatha Brahmana narrates Videgha Mathava carrying Agni eastward across the Sadanira (Gandak) — a legend encoding the agricultural colonisation of the east.

Polity becomes territorial and monarchical. Jana gives way to janapada; the king's title inflates to Rajadhiraja, Samrat, Ekrat, Virat, Bhoja. Elaborate consecratory sacrifices legitimise power: Rajasuya (royal consecration), Ashvamedha (horse sacrifice asserting suzerainty over territory the horse traverses), and Vajapeya (a chariot race the king must ritually win). The Sabha and Samiti decline, women are excluded from the Sabha, and the Vidatha disappears. Taxation becomes regular through bali, shulka and bhaga, collected by the Sangrihitri (treasurer) and Bhagadugha. The twelve Ratnins are the king's principal functionaries. There is still no standing army and no bureaucracy in the later imperial sense.

Society rigidifies. The four-fold varna hardens into a birth-based order (Purusha Sukta), with Brahmanas and Kshatriyas contending for primacy and the Shudra excluded from the upanayana and hence from the dvija (twice-born) status. Gotra appears as an exogamous institution. The four ashramas — Brahmacharya, Grihastha, Vanaprastha and Sannyasa — take shape, though the fourth is not fully established in this period. Women's position declines sharply: exclusion from assemblies and from upanayana, loss of property rights, and the first references to child marriage; the Aitareya Brahmana calls a daughter a source of misery. Gargi Vachaknavi and Maitreyi are the celebrated exceptions, debating Yajnavalkya in the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad.

Religion transforms. Indra and Agni recede; Prajapati becomes supreme creator, with Vishnu and Rudra-Shiva rising. Ritual becomes elaborate, expensive and priest-dominated, with sacrifice believed to compel the gods rather than petition them. The reaction is twofold: the Upanishadic turn toward jnana, questioning ritual and expounding Brahman, Atman, karma, samsara and moksha; and, by the sixth century BCE, the heterodox challenge of Buddhism and Jainism. The purusharthas (dharma, artha, kama, moksha) also crystallise.

Trap watch. Iron and rice are Later Vedic, not Rigvedic; ayas in the Rigveda means copper or bronze. The Purusha Sukta is in Mandala X and is late. The Saraswati, not the Ganga, is the most eulogised river; the Indus is the most frequently mentioned. Indra has the most hymns, Agni second, Varuna third. Nishka is an ornament, not a coin. The Vidatha is the oldest assembly and admitted women; the Samiti could elect the king. The Atharvaveda was the last to be accepted as canonical. The Gayatri mantra addresses Savitri, a solar deity.
