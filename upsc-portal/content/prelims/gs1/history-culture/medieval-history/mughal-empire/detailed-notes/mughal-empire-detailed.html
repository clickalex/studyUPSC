4. The Mughal State as a System: Mansab, Zabt, and the Court Economy

Section 1's anchors (mansabdari, dahsala, suba–sarkar–pargana) describe parts; this section assembles the machine — because Prelims increasingly tests how the parts meshed, not merely what they were called. The mansabdari system ranked every imperial servant (military commander, governor, courtier — even, anomalously, scholars) on a dual numerical scale: zat (personal rank, fixing salary and status) and sawar (cavalry obligation, fixing the troopers to be maintained and mustered). The dagh (branding of horses) and chehra (descriptive rolls of troopers) — inherited from Alauddin Khalji's market-control machinery — policed the muster against the perennial fraud (the officer drawing pay for phantom horsemen). Mansabs were not hereditary, not landed, and revocable: the mansabdar held no fief, only a salary-claim, usually discharged through a jagir (revenue-assignment over a territory whose assessed income — jama — matched the rank). The jagir was transferable at will — the average tenure ran to three or four years — and this transferability is the system's master-key: it prevented landed-magnate power (no officer could root himself), but it also destroyed stewardship (why invest in land you lose next year?), incentivising maximum short-term extraction — the seed of Habib's jagir-crisis thesis that Section 2's decline mechanics invoke.

The zabt/dahsala revenue settlement (Todar Mal's reform, c. 1580) supplied the machine's fiscal base: measured fields, classified soils, ten-year average yields, and a cash-demand schedule (dastur-ul-amal) fixed crop by crop — the dahsala (ten-year rates) giving the peasant predictability and the state a maximised, monetised claim (conventionally one-third of produce, higher in practice near the capitals). The assessment's honesty depended on measurement (the gaz-i-Ilahi, the tanab rope-and-bamboo), records (patwari–qanungo village–pargana accountants — the Mughal system's deepest institutional legacy, surviving into British and independent India), and the karori experiment's lesson (1574–75 — revenue-farmers assigned crore-income tracts, collapsing into over-exaction — the experiment that taught Akbar to measure rather than farm). The suba–sarkar–pargana hierarchy administered the claim: the subadar (governor) and diwan (revenue head) as the province's deliberately-divided pair (executive versus fiscal — the Mughal separation-of-powers), the faujdar (military-police), the kotwal (urban order), the qazi (justice) and muhtasib (morals-and-markets — weights, prices, public conduct). Below them the court economy circulated the surplus: the karkhana state workshops (textiles, arms, luxury goods — employing thousands), the nazr–peshkash gift-tribute flows (presentation as taxation-by-ceremony), the imperial mints (the silver rupiya — Sher Shah's standard, Mughalised — as the subcontinent's most trusted coinage), and the nobility's consumption cities (Agra, Delhi, Lahore, Fatehpur Sikri — each a demand-engine for artisan hinterlands).

The MCQ logic of the machine is relational: zat is personal rank while sawar is cavalry obligation (never interchange them); jagir is transferable revenue-assignment while iqta was its Sultanate predecessor (more administrative, less monetised); zabt is measurement-based assessment while ghalla-bakhshi (crop-sharing) and nasaq (estimation) were its alternatives; the diwan is the fiscal check on the subadar's executive power; the muhtasib polices markets-and-morals while the kotwal polices the city. Questions increasingly present the pair and ask the function — learn functions, not just terms.

5. Sulh-i-Kul to the Deccan Ulcer: The Religious-Policy Arc and Its Debates

Section 1 stages Akbar's tolerance against Aurangzeb's reversal; this section traces the full arc and the historiography, because the examination rewards the candidate who can argue the policy's evolution, not merely its endpoints. Akbar's settlement was constructed in layers: abolition of the pilgrim tax (1563) and jizya (1564), Rajput marriage-alliances (Amer 1562 — Man Singh–Bhagwan Das as the alliance's military dividend), the Ibadat Khana debates (1575 — Sunni–Shia–Sufi–Hindu–Jain–Jesuit disputation at Fatehpur Sikri), the mahzar (1579 — the emperor as final arbiter in disputed religious law — \"infallibility decree\" in hostile readings, jurisdictional supremacy in sympathetic ones), and Din-i-Ilahi (1582) — the court order (not a mass religion: Birbal its famous Hindu adherent, membership in the dozens) blending solar veneration, administrative loyalty, and Akbari charisma. Sulh-i-kul (\"universal peace/civility\" — Abul Fazl's formulation in the Ain) was the doctrine: state neutrality among faiths, appointment by merit (Todar Mal, Birbal, Rahim), justice without confessional bias. Jahangir preserved the settlement (Khusrau's rebellion punished politically, Guru Arjan's 1606 execution read as political punishment for rebel-blessing rather than theological persecution — the contested edge), Shah Jahan tilted orthodox at the margins (new-temple restrictions proclaimed, 1633 — enforcement patchy; the court's Persianate-Shia cultural tilt under Mumtaz's family), and the architecture carried the message (mosque–tomb–fort as imperial idiom, Rajput craftsmen building Mughal monuments, Sanskrit texts translated — Dara Shukoh's Majma-ul-Bahrain, 1655 — \"mingling of two oceans\" — and the Sirr-i-Akbar Upanishad translation).

Aurangzeb's reversal (1658–1707) must be itemised before it is judged: reimposition of jizya (1679), temple-destruction orders (selective but real — Mathura's Keshav Rai, Varanasi's Vishwanath-adjacent actions — the \"political punishment of rebellious zamindars\" reading versus the \"theological bigotry\" reading), the Fatawa-e-Alamgiri (Hanafi compendium — sharia alignment of justice), court austerity (music–dance pullback, the kalima removed from coins lest it be defiled by non-Muslim touch), and the execution of Guru Tegh Bahadur (1675) and Sambhaji (1689) — the two killings that militarised Sikhism (Guru Gobind Singh's Khalsa, 1699) and Maratha resistance respectively. The Deccan ulcer (1682–1707 — the emperor camped south for 25 years, annexing Bijapur 1686 and Golconda 1687 but never subduing Maratha guerrilla warfare — the bargi-giri raiders, the fort-recapture cycle) converted religious policy into fiscal catastrophe: the war consumed the surplus, jagirs multiplied beyond available jama (the be-jagiri crisis — rank without revenue), and the mansabdari's loyalty-market collapsed. The Sarkar-versus-revisionist debate (Jadunath Sarkar's History of Aurangzeb — bigotry as the empire's suicide — versus the revisionists — Richards's Deccan-revenue overextension, Athar Ali's nobility-faction data, Habib's structural crisis, Eaton's temple-politics contextualisation) is the historiographical frame: Prelims tests the facts (1679, 1675, 1686–87, 1699), Mains tests the argument — carry both.

The arc's coda matters for the 18th century: Bahadur Shah I's conciliation (Guru Gobind Singh's alliance, Maratha–Rajput settlements — the \"last chance\" reading), the Sayyid Brothers' kingmaking (Farrukhsiyar's 1719 blinding-and-deposition — sovereignty auctioned), and Muhammad Shah Rangeela's cultural efflorescence amid political hollowing (Urdu's court triumph — Mir, Sauda — while Nadir Shah marched on Delhi) — the empire as civilisation surviving the state that built it.

6. Culture as Statecraft: Architecture, Atelier, Chronicle, and the Travellers' Lens

Section 3 lists the cultural outputs; this section reads them as instruments of rule — because the Mughals built, painted and chronicled the way they taxed: systematically. Architecture evolved as imperial messaging: Babur's charbagh (Aram Bagh, Agra — the Persian paradise-garden naturalised), Humayun's tomb (1569–70 — Mirak Mirza Ghiyas's Timurid double-dome on a high plinth — the Taj's grandfather), Akbar's red-sandstone synthesis (Fatehpur Sikri 1571+ — Salim Chishti's white-marble tomb inside the red city, Buland Darwaza 1601, the Panch Mahal's Buddhist-vihara echo, Jodha Bai's palace — Gujarati–Rajput craftsmen inside Timurid geometry), Jahangir's marble transition (Itmad-ud-Daula — Nur Jahan's father — first fully-marble, pietra dura inlay perfected), Shah Jahan's marble apex (Taj 1632–53 — Ustad Ahmad Lahori; Red Fort's Shahjahanabad; Jama Masjid 1656; Moti Masjid's pearl-purity; Shalimar Lahore), Aurangzeb's austerity coda (Bibi-ka-Maqbara 1678 — the \"poor man's Taj\" at Aurangabad; Badshahi Mosque Lahore 1673 — scale without splendour). The technical vocabulary (double dome, pietra dura, jali screens, charbagh, hasht-bihisht eight-paradise plan, red-sandstone-to-marble gradient) is directly MCQ-testable — each monument carries one technique.

The atelier (imperial painting workshop — Persian masters Abdus Samad–Mir Sayyid Ali under Humayun, Indianised under Akbar — the Hamzanama's 1,400 cloth folios, Basawan–Daswanth as the first Indian masters; Jahangir's connoisseurship — Ustad Mansur's natural history (dodo, zebra, turkey-cock — the Mughal Audubon), Abul Hasan's allegories, muraqqa albums; Shah Jahan's formalisation — padshahnama imperial-image painting) paralleled the chronicle tradition (Babur's Baburnama — Chagatai Turki candour; Abul Fazl's Akbarnama–Ain-i-Akbari — the empire's official theory, c. 1590s; Jahangir's Tuzuk — autobiographical connoisseurship; Lahori's Badshahnama; Khafi Khan's Muntakhab-ul-Lubab — the Deccan-ulcer critique from inside; Badayuni's Muntakhab-ut-Tawarikh — the orthodox dissent against Akbar). Music (Tansen's dhrupad — the Gwalior gharana's fountainhead; Jahangir–Shah Jahan court music; qawwali's formalisation — Amir Khusrau's Sultanate foundations, Mughal courtly adoption), language (Persian as state language — the munshi culture; Braj Bhasha court verse — Rahim's dohas; Urdu's 18th-century court triumph), and the travellers' lens (Roe 1615–18 — the EIC embassy, Surat factory 1613; Hawkins before him; Tavernier (diamonds–trade), Bernier (Travels — the \"no private property\" thesis that shaped European Orientalism and Marx's Asiatic mode), Manucci (the artilleryman-memoirist — Storia do Mogor), Thevenot, Peter Mundy (the 1630–32 famine) — each traveller with one book and one thesis) complete the culture-system: build the monument, paint the image, chronicle the reign, and let the foreigner testify.

7. Sher Shah, the Marathas, and the 18th-Century Bridge

Two bookends complete the Mughal lesson: the Sur interregnum that pre-built Mughal institutions, and the Maratha–successor bridge that carried the 18th century. Sher Shah Suri (1540–45 — five years that out-administered decades): the rupee–dam standardisation (silver rupiya of ~178 grains, copper dam — the currency the Mughals inherited and the British adapted), the patta–qabuliyat system (recorded title-deed and revenue-agreement — the peasant's paper protection against the collector's whim), the zabt-prototype measurement (land surveyed, yields scheduled — Todar Mal served Sher Shah before serving Akbar, and the dahsala's paternity runs through Sasaram), the Grand Trunk Road (Agra–Burhanpur–Lahore — sarais, wells, dak runners — the subcontinent's arterial road rebuilt), and the Sasaram tomb (the Afghan-idiom masterpiece on its lake-plinth). Islam Shah (1545–53) extended the machinery before the Sur collapse (1555) restored Humayun — the interregnum's lesson being institutional, not dynastic: the Mughal system was half-Sur before it was Mughal.

The Maratha polity (Shivaji 1674 coronation — Raigad; the ashtapradhan council — peshwa–majumdar–wakenavis–dabir and four more; the fort-system — Raigad–Pratapgad–Sinhagad as sovereignty made stone; chauth (one-fourth) and sardeshmukhi (one-tenth) as the tribute-economics of expansion; the Agra escape 1666 — the sweets-basket legend; the 1680 death at 53) matured into the Peshwa confederacy (Balaji Vishwanath 1713 — the Chhatrapati-to-Peshwa power shift; Baji Rao I — the northern thrust, Palkhed 1728; Balaji Baji Rao — the confederacy's zenith and Panipat-3, 1761 — Abdali's coalition defeating the Maratha host — the \"national\" defeat that was really a coalition failure; Mahadji Scindia's northern revival; the Anglo-Maratha wars — 1775–82, 1803–05, 1817–18 — ending at Koregaon–Sitabuldi). The successor states (Nizam-ul-Mulk's Hyderabad 1724, Saadat Khan's Awadh 1722, Murshid Quli's Bengal — the Nawabi political economies; Haider–Tipu's Mysore — the military-fiscalist challenger; Ranjit Singh's Punjab — the Sikh empire's 1799–1839 arc) plus the foreign raids (Nadir 1739 — Karnal, the Delhi sack, Peacock Throne + Koh-i-noor; Abdali 1748–67 — the Afghan shadow over Punjab–Delhi) plus the EIC's fiscal capture (Plassey 1757 — the Clive–Mir Jafar transaction; Buxar 1764 — Munro defeating the triple alliance — Mir Qasim–Shuja-ud-Daula–Shah Alam II; the Diwani 1765 — Allahabad treaty, the Company as Bengal's revenue-sovereign) complete the bridge: the Mughal emperor as pensioner-presider (Shah Alam II's Allahabad years, Akbar II's Rammohun-Roy embassy to London, Zafar's 1857 figureheadship and Rangoon exile) over a century his ancestors would not recognise — and the MCQ hooks (Section 4's battle-triple, the twin reforms, the throne-and-diamond, the fiscal-capture pair) now anchored in a system rather than a list.
