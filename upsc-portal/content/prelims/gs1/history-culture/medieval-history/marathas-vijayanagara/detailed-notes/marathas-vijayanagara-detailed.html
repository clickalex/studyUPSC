Vijayanagara, Bahmani and Marathas — Detailed Notes

Three Deccan-centred powers: Vijayanagara (1336–1565+), Bahmani & successors (1347–1600s), Marathas (1640s–1818).

1. Vijayanagara (1336–1646 shell)

  • Founding: Harihara I + Bukka (Sangama dynasty; Hampi — UNESCO; Vidyanagara Muslim-episode lineage claim); dynasties: Sangama → Saluva → Tuluva → Aravidu.
  • Devaraya I (dam on Tungabhadra; Italian travellers — Nicolo Conti), Devaraya II (Gajapati wars; Abdur Razzaq visit; Muslim archers in his army), Krishnadevaraya (1509-29; Raichur doab win 1520; Gulbarga-Bijapur pressure; Amuktamalyada (Telugu) and 'Usha Parinayam'; patron of Ashtadiggajas; Vitthala temple completion era) → Achyuta → Sadasiva (puppet) under Rama Raya → TALIKOTA 1565 (Rakkasa-Tangadi; confederacy of Deccan sultanates; Rama Raya executed; Hampi sacked) → Aravidu shell till 1640s.
  • Administration: nayankara system (nayak military-land grants, tribute), ayagar village officials; amara-nayaka military landholders; commerce: varaha (pagoda) gold coinage, active horse trade, horse-import monopoly; irrigation works; Hampi urban quarters (Virupaksha + Vitthala + royal centre).

2. Bahmani & Deccan sultanates

  • Alauddin Hasan Bahman Shah (1347; capital Gulbarga → Bidar 1424); rival loop vs Vijayanagara (Raichur doab, Krishna-Godavari); Mahmud Gawan (wazir, land reform, executed 1481 → decay); 5 successors: Ahmadnagar (Nizam Shahi), Bijapur (Adil Shahi), Golconda (Qutb Shahi), Berar (Imad Shahi), Bidar (Barid Shahi); Deccani vs Afaqi faction; Ibrahim Adil Shah II ('Jagadguru Badshaha' syncretism, Kitab-i-Nauras), Quli Qutb Shah (Telugu patron, Hyderabad 1591 founding).

3. Marathas (Shivaji → Peshwas → annexation)

  • Shivaji (1630-80): Raigad-Torna-Purandar forts; guru Samarth Ramdas; Dadaji Konddev early mentor; Afzal Khan 1659 (Pratapgad), Shaista Khan 1663 raid, Agra escape 1666, Treaty of Purandar 1665 (Jai Singh; 23 forts ceded), coronation 1674 (Raigad; Chatrapati; formal state); ashtapradhan (8 ministers); chauth + sardeshmukhi levies; cavalry-guerrilla (ganimi kava); navy (Kanhoji Angre lineage at Konkan).
  • Sambhaji (1680-89, executed by Aurangzeb) → Rajaram (Jinji flight) → Tarabai resistance → Shahu I returns 1707 → Peshwa dominance: Balaji Vishwanath (1713-20, Poona seat) → Baji Rao I (1720-40; Malwa, Bundelkhand, Delhi raid 1737) → Balaji Baji Rao (Nanasaheb; PANIPAT-3 1761 vs Abdali — Vishwasrao + Sadashivrao Bhau deaths; Third battle aftermath) → Madhavrao I (recovery) → Narayan Rao → Raghunath Rao (Barbhai council) → Baji Rao II (Treaty of Bassein 1802 → Anglo-Maratha wars close 1818, Peshwa pensioned at Bithur).
  • Anglo-Maratha wars: I (1775-82 — Treaty of Salbai), II (1803-05 — Assaye, Argaon, Delhi), III (1817-18 — final).

MCQ hooks

  • Talikota 1565 = Rakkasa-Tangadi; Rama Raya = executed leader.
  • Amuktamalyada = Krishnadevaraya (Telugu); ashtadiggajas = his court poets.
  • Nayankara = Vijayanagara; Iqta = Sultanate parallel.
  • Ashtapradhan = Shivaji's 8; chauth (1/4) + sardeshmukhi (1/10 extra) = levies.
  • Afzal Khan → Pratapgad 1659; Shaista Khan → Lal Mahal raid 1663; Jai Singh → Purandar 1665.
  • Peshwa line order: Balaji Vishwanath → Baji Rao I → Balaji Baji Rao → Madhavrao → ... → Baji Rao II.
  • Bassein 1802 → Anglo-Maratha II trigger; 1818 = end.

4. Vijayanagara's Political System: Kings, Nayakas, and the Segmentary Debate

Vijayanagara (1336–1646, effective to Talikota 1565) is the most argued-over polity in medieval Indian historiography, and the argument — centralised empire versus segmentary state — is the examination's real test. The traditional narrative (Nilakanta Sastri, Sewell's A Forgotten Empire) reads a strong military monarchy: Harihara I and Bukka I (Sangama brothers, Telugu–Kannada chiefs consolidated under Vidyaranya's Sringeri legitimation) founding Vidyanagara/Hampi in 1336 as a Hindu bulwark against the Madurai Sultanate and the advancing Bahmanis; Devaraya I (1406–22, Tungabhadra dam, Nicolo Conti's visit) and Devaraya II (1422–46, the Gajapati wars, Abdur Razzaq's 1443 embassy account, Muslim cavalry archers recruited into the army) building the imperial core; the Saluva usurpation (1485) as interregnum; and Krishnadevaraya (1509–29, Tuluva) as the apogee — the Raichur Doab victory (1520, artillery-and-cavalry combined arms against Bijapur), the Udayagiri–Kondavidu Gajapati campaigns, Gulbarga's humiliation, the Amuktamalyada (Telugu kavya on statecraft-as-devotion) and the Ashtadiggaja court (Allasani Peddana foremost), the Vitthala temple's musical pillars and the Hazara Rama narrative friezes. Then Achyuta's weakness, Sadasiva's puppetry under the Aliya Rama Raya regency, and Talikota/Rakkasa-Tangadi (23 January 1565) — the four-sultanate confederacy (Bijapur, Ahmadnagar, Golconda, Bidar; Berar abstaining), Rama Raya's execution mid-battle, Hampi's systematic sack — reducing the Aravidu successors to the Penukonda–Chandragiri rump till the 1640s.

Burton Stein's segmentary-state thesis (1980) inverted this reading: Vijayanagara as ritual sovereignty over autonomous nadus, the king's power real only in the core rajya, the nayankara system (military chiefs holding amaram land-tenures for cavalry service and tribute) as decentralised warlordism rather than bureaucracy, and warfare as status-competition rather than state-building. Karashima, Subrahmanyam and Stein's critics (the \"fiscal-military\" school) countered with the empire's evident extractive capacity — the varaha/pagoda gold coinage, the horse-import monopoly (Arab–Persian Gulf trade controlled through Bhatkal–Goa ports, thousands of warhorses yearly), customs and mint revenues, the ayagar village establishment (twelve functionaries — headman, accountant, watchman and the artisan–service roster) collecting the agrarian surplus, and the irrigation state (Tungabhadra anicuts, the Kamalapuram tank, the Daroji–Hospet channels). The synthesis to carry is that Vijayanagara combined a strong fiscal-military core with a negotiated periphery: the Tungabhadra–Krishna heartland was directly administered and heavily taxed, while Tamil–Telugu–Kannada nayakas (the Madurai, Tanjore and Gingee lines that would outlive the empire as independent kingdoms) ruled as tributary magnates — centralised enough to field the Talikota host, segmentary enough that the host's destruction dissolved the state. The amara-nayaka versus iqta comparison (both military revenue-assignments; the nayaka hereditary in practice, the muqti transferable in theory) is the cross-lesson pairing examiners reward.

Hampi's urbanism supplies the material corroboration: the sacred centre (Virupaksha's 50-metre gopuram, the Vitthala complex, the Achyutaraya–Kodandarama ensembles), the royal centre (Mahanavami dibba's Dasara-review platform, the Lotus Mahal's Indo-Saracenic fusion, the elephant stables' eleven domes, the stepped tank's geometric precision), and the Islamic quarter's evidence of cosmopolitan soldiery — a capital whose ruins (UNESCO World Heritage, 1986) still map the empire's sociology: temple, court and bazaar in planned procession.

5. The Bahmani Experiment and the Five Sultanates: Deccan Synthesis

The Bahmani Sultanate (1347–1527, effective to Mahmud Gawan's execution in 1481) is the Deccan's mirror to Delhi — founded when Alauddin Hasan Bahman Shah (Zafar Khan, the Tughlaq governor who rebelled as Muhammad Tughlaq's southern system collapsed) crowned himself at Daulatabad and built Gulbarga, then Bidar (1424, Ahmad Shah I), into imperial capitals. The Vijayanagara–Bahmani rivalry loop (eight wars across a century over the Raichur Doab's double-crop wealth, the Krishna–Godavari delta ports, and Marathwada's forts) structured Deccan politics: Firoz Shah Bahmani versus Devaraya I (the goldsmith's-daughter casus belli, the chronicles' relish), Ahmad Shah I's grinding campaigns, Alauddin Ahmad II's marriage-alliance interlude — warfare as the two states' fiscal lifeblood (tribute, plunder, horse-trade control) and their mutual legitimation (jihad against kafirs, dharma-yuddha against turushkas). The rivalry's institutional product was military modernisation on both sides: Bahmani artillery and cavalry-archer corps, Vijayanagara's reciprocal Muslim cavalry and gun-foundries — the Deccan as India's early-gunpowder laboratory.

Mahmud Gawan (Malik-ut-Tujjar, the Persian merchant become wazir, 1461–81) is the Bahmani apogee in one career: administrative rationalisation (the eight tarafs replacing four oversized provinces, salaries graded and audited, land measured and assessed), military expansion (Goa–Dabhol ports seized from Vijayanagara, the Konkan secured), cultural cosmopolitanism (the Bidar madrasa — 36,000-volume library, Persian–Indian scholars, the theological college as statecraft), and the classic fall — executed in 1481 on forged treason letters, the Deccani–Afaqi factionalism (Indian-born Muslims versus foreign immigrants — Persians, Arabs, Turks, Habshis) he had managed exploding into the five successor Sultanates: Ahmadnagar's Nizam Shahis (1490, Malik Ahmad — the Junnar–Daulatabad state, Chand Bibi's 1595–1600 heroics, absorbed by the Mughals 1636), Bijapur's Adil Shahis (1490, Yusuf — the Gol Gumbad state, Ibrahim II's syncretism, 1686), Golconda's Qutb Shahis (1518, Quli — the diamond state, Hyderabad founded 1591, Charminar, 1687), Berar's Imad Shahis (1490, absorbed by Ahmadnagar 1574) and Bidar's Barids (1528, absorbed by Bijapur 1619). The factional sociology (Deccanis versus Afaqis, Sunnis versus the Shia-leaning courts — Bijapur and Golconda's Muharram statecraft, the Persianate–Dakhani cultural fusion) explains both the creativity and the fragility: immigrant talent built the states, nativist–immigrant rivalry dissolved them.

The Deccani synthesis — Dakhani Urdu (the Gulshan-i Ishq–Nusrat tradition, courtly Hindavi-Persian fusion), Bijapur's Indo-Saracenic architecture (Ibrahim Rauza, Gol Gumbad's whispering dome — the world's second-largest dome at building), Qutb Shahi Hyderabad (Charminar 1591, Mecca Masjid, the diamond–textile–horse economy), Ahmadnagar's Nujum al-Ulum manuscript — is the counterpoint to both Delhi's Persianate and Vijayanagara's Sanskritic idioms: a third high culture, Shia-inflected, Telugu–Marathi–Kannada absorbing, whose destruction (Aurangzeb's 1686–87 sieges) the Mughal empire would pay for in the Maratha war that followed. Talikota (1565) had united four of these sultanates against Rama Raya; two generations later, Mughal pressure would unite their Maratha subjects against Delhi — the Deccan devours every empire that conquers it piecemeal.

6. Shivaji's Swarajya: Forts, Fleet, and the Ashtapradhan State

Shivaji Bhonsle (1630–80) built the only new state of the 17th century to outlive its founder by 138 years, and the building's logic was terrain weaponised by administration. The Western Ghats' basalt forts — Torna (1646, the first seizure), Raigad (the capital-in-the-clouds), Purandar, Pratapgad, Sinhagad, the 240-odd hill forts at the kingdom's peak — functioned as arsenals, treasuries, prisons and refuge-citadels in a single system: each fort triple-officered (havaldar, sabnis, karkhanis, deliberately of different castes for mutual surveillance), provisioned for siege, and linked by the ganimi kava cavalry (light Maratha horsemen — bargirs on state horses versus silahdars on own — living off speed and country knowledge against Mughal heavy cavalry and artillery). The political milestones each encode a method: Afzal Khan at Pratapgad (1659) — the Bijapur general's assassination mid-embrace (bagh-nakh, tiger-claws) demonstrating that diplomacy with stronger powers was war by other means; the Shaista Khan raid (1663) — the Lal Mahal night attack on Aurangzeb's viceroy-uncle demonstrating reach into the enemy's bedroom; the Agra escape (1666) — the sweet-basket flight from Aurangzeb's court demonstrating that Mughal honour was a trap to be sprung; the Treaty of Purandar (1665) — 23 forts ceded to Jai Singh demonstrating tactical retreat (every fort retaken by 1670); and the Raigad coronation (6 June 1674) — Gagabhatta's Vedic legitimation, the Chatrapati title, the Raishak era — demonstrating that swarajya claimed sovereignty, not rebellion.

The ashtapradhan (eight-minister council — peshwa, amatya, sachiv, mantri, senapati, sumant, nyayadhish, panditrao) institutionalised the state: Brahmin administrators, Maratha commanders, Prabhu secretaries — a caste-division of governance labour that professionalised revenue (ryotwari assessment at 33 per cent, kathis measurement, the abolition of hereditary deshmukh intermediaries where possible), justice and correspondence. The fiscal-military pair of chauth (one-fourth of revenue, protection-price from unconquered territory) and sardeshmukhi (one-tenth additional, the hereditary sardeshmukh claim over Maharashtra) funded expansion without annexation — tribute-empire economics that the Peshwas would scale across India. And the navy (Kanhoji Angre's later Konkan fleet — Vijaydurg–Sindhudurg sea-forts, the gurab–gallivat squadrons that fought Europeans and Sidis alike — prefigured by Shivaji's own 1657–59 dockyards) made the Marathas India's only pre-colonial sea power. Shivaji's religious policy (temple patronage alongside Muslim commanders — Ibrahim Khan, Siddi Ibrahim — and the Quran-protecting orders) and his succession failure (Sambhaji's 1680–89 reign ending in Aurangzeb's execution, Rajaram's Jinji flight, Tarabai's regency resistance) complete the portrait: a founder who built institutions for everything except his own replacement.

The guru-lineage (Dadaji Konddev's Pune stewardship, Samarth Ramdas's Dasbodh counsel — \"Maratha–Hindu uplift through disciplined action,\" the Sajjangad matha) supplies the ideological frame examiners probe: swarajya as Hindavi swarajya (the Baji Pasalkar-letter phrase, authenticity debated, influence undoubted) — Hindu kingship reclaimed, yet administratively inclusive. The debate (Hindu-nationalist icon versus secular-administrator) is best answered structurally: Shivaji fought Muslim states (Bijapur, Mughals) with Muslim soldiers for a Hindu-titled throne — identity as legitimation, inclusivity as administration.

7. Peshwas, Panipat, and the End: Confederacy to Annexation

Shahu's release (1707, after Aurangzeb's death) and the Khed victory over Tarabai (1707) transferred power from the Chatrapati's Satara throne to the Peshwa's Poona secretariat — hereditary Brahmin prime ministers ruling in a monarch's name, the Mughal–Maratha settlement of 1719 (Balaji Vishwanath extracting swarajya recognition plus Deccan chauth–sardeshmukhi rights from the Sayyid-backed emperor) licensing expansion in the empire's own name. Baji Rao I (1720–40, appointed at twenty) executed the expansion: Malwa (1728), Bundelkhand (Chhatrasal's bequest — Mastani's romance as the human footnote), Gujarat's edges, the Konkan coast, and the Delhi raid of 1737 (the Peshwa watering his horses at Talkatora, demonstrating that the Mughal capital was indefensible) — \"strike at the withering trunk, the branches fall themselves,\" the northward strategy his father had doubted. Balaji Baji Rao/Nanasaheb (1740–61) inherited the confederacy at its zenith — Holkars at Indore, Scindias at Gwalior, Gaekwads at Baroda, Bhonsles at Nagpur, the Peshwa primus inter pares — and led it to catastrophe: the Mughal–Maratha–Sikh–Jat anti-Afghan entente collapsing into Panipat-III (14 January 1761), where Ahmad Shah Abdali's Afghan–Rohilla–Shuja coalition annihilated Sadashivrao Bhau's host (Vishwasrao, the Peshwa's son, dead; perhaps 60,000–70,000 Maratha dead; Nanasaheb dying of shock at Parvati hill). The causes debated (Bhau's tactical rigidity, the artillery–cavalry mismatch, supply-line strangulation, Ibrahim Gardi's unsupported guns, Abdali's superior generalship and camel-swivel guns) matter less than the consequence: Maratha expansion halted for a decade, Mughal restoration discredited forever, and the Company's Bengal opportunity (Buxar, 1764) arriving into a northern vacuum.

Madhavrao I (1761–72, \"the last great Peshwa\") rebuilt the confederacy in eleven years — re-subduing Mysore (Hyder Ali checked), re-entering Delhi (1771, Shah Alam II restored as Maratha pensioner), disciplining the sardars — before tuberculosis killed him at twenty-seven; the succession murders (Narayanrao, 1773; Raghunathrao's treason; the Barbhai council's regency for Sawai Madhavrao) then hollowed the centre while Nana Phadnavis's diplomacy preserved the shell. The Anglo-Maratha wars closed the account: the First (1775–82 — Raghoba's Treaty of Surat, the Wadgaon humiliation of 1779, Mahadji Scindia's mediation, Salbai 1782 restoring the status quo); the Second (1803–05 — Baji Rao II's Treaty of Bassein, 31 December 1802, accepting subsidiary alliance and triggering Scindia–Bhonsle resistance; Assaye and Argaon — Wellesley's \"the hardest-fought battle I ever saw\" — Delhi and Laswari; the Deogaon–Surji-Anjangaon settlements dismembering the confederacy); the Third (1817–18 — the Peshwa's Poona rising, Scindia's neutrality purchased, Holkar's Mahidpur defeat, Baji Rao II's surrender and Bithur pension). The Company's victory owed less to battlefield supremacy (Assaye was near-run) than to confederacy arithmetic: subsidiary alliances peeling sardars one by one, the Peshwa's authority already nominal, finance (the Company's credit versus the sardars' plunder-cycle) deciding the war before the battles. In 1818 the Maratha century ended not with a last stand but with a ledger — fitting, for a power that had lived by tribute and died by debt.
