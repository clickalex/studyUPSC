Music and Dance — Detailed Notes

Two lists to memorise: 8 classical dances and the two music systems' anchors, plus folk favourites.

2. Music systems

  • Sangeet trinity (Natyashastra): vocal (geet), instrumental (vadya), dance (nritya). Rasa theory: 8 rasas + shanta (9th).
  • Hindustani (north): gharana system; dhrupad (oldest gravitas), khayal (Amir Khusrau attribution; Sadarang popularised), thumri (semi-classical), tarana; instruments: sitar (Khusrau lineage claim), sarod, sarangi, tabla, shehnai (Bismillah Khan), santoor (Shivkumar Sharma); Tansen (Akbar's Navaratna — Miyan ki malhar).
  • Carnatic (south): kritis; Trinity — Tyagaraja, Muttuswami Dikshitar, Syama Sastri; structure: pallavi-anupallavi-charanam; 72 melakarta scheme (Venkatamakhin's Chaturdandi Prakasika line); instruments: veena, mridangam, ghatam, kanjira, violin (adapted — Baluswami Dikshitar credit line), nadaswaram.

3. Folk & theatre anchors

  • Yakshagana (Karnataka), Theyyam (Kerala possession-ritual), Bhavai (Gujarat), Tamasha (MH — Lavani), Nautanki (UP), Jatra (Bengal), Bhand Pather (Kashmir), Kutiyattam (Sanskrit drama, UNESCO).
  • Folk dances: Bhangra/Giddha (Punjab), Garba-Dandiya (Gujarat), Lavani (MH), Ghoomar/Kalbelia (Rajasthan — Kalbelia UNESCO), Bihu (Assam), Chhau (Jharkhand-Odisha-WB masks — UNESCO), Rouf (Kashmir), Dalkhai (Odisha), Koli (Maharashtra coast), Dumhal (Kashmir).

MCQ hooks

  • Natyashastra = Bharata (600-200 BCE span); 'dasarupaka' = Dhananjaya (10 forms).
  • Sattriya = newest to classical list (2000); 8-list order often asked.
  • Kathak gharana city mismatch = classic trap (Jaipur gharana ≠ Lucknow grace).
  • Carnatic Trinity order Tyagaraja-Dikshitar-Syama Sastri; Hindustani counterpart = 'Tansen line'.
  • Pung cholom = Manipur; Chenda = Kathakali; Mizhavu = Kutiyattam.
  • Kalakshetra = Rukmini Devi (1936); Kalamandalam = Vallathol (1930).

6. The Common Grammar: Text, Technique and the Test of "Classical"

The eight recognised classical dances look nothing alike on the surface — a Kathakali night-performance with its green faces and thundering chenda shares no visible feature with a Manipuri Ras Leela in which the dancers wear no bells and barely lift their feet — yet they are grouped together for one reason: they draw on a shared textual and technical grammar, not on a shared style. Understanding that grammar converts a long list of facts into a small number of principles, which is the only way the volume is manageable inside a syllabus.

The base text is the Natyashastra, attributed to Bharata and dated loosely between 200 BCE and 200 CE. Its useful distinctions are threefold. First, it separates nritta (pure movement, decorative, without narrative meaning), nritya (movement charged with expression) and natya (the dramatic element) — every classical form mixes the three in a different proportion, and that proportion explains its character: Kathak and Bharatanatyam are nritta-heavy, Mohiniyattam and Manipuri are nritya-heavy, Kathakali and Kuchipudi are natya-heavy because they are danced plays. Second, it sorts expressive means into four abhinayas — angika (the body), vachika (speech and song), aharya (costume, make-up and ornament) and sattvika (the involuntary outward signs of inner feeling). Third, it codifies rasa: eight permanent moods (shringara, hasya, karuna, raudra, vira, bhayanaka, bibhatsa, adbhuta) arising from a sthayi bhava through determinants (vibhava), consequents (anubhava) and transient states (vyabhichari bhava). Abhinavagupta, writing in Kashmir in the tenth–eleventh century, added shanta as the ninth rasa in his commentary Abhinavabharati and argued that rasa is relished only by a sahridaya — a prepared, sympathetic spectator. That single idea explains the whole social position of classical dance: it presupposes a literate audience, which is why it lived in temples and courts, while folk performance, needing no preparation, lived in the village square.

Around this core sit the working manuals, and their names are examinable because each belongs to a specific form: the Abhinaya Darpana of Nandikeshvara supplies the gesture lexicon most Bharatanatyam dancers learn from; the Abhinaya Chandrika of Maheshwar Mahapatra underpins Odissi; the Hastalakshanadeepika gives Kathakali its mudra vocabulary; the Balarama Bharatam of Kartika Thirunal Balarama Varma documents Kerala's lasya tradition behind Mohiniyattam. Music's equivalent chain runs from Matanga's Brihaddeshi (eighth–ninth century), the first text to use the word raga and to separate marga (the classical, transmitted path) from deshi (the regional), through the Sangita Ratnakara of Sarangadeva, written in the thirteenth century at the Yadava court of Devagiri. Sarangadeva is the pivot of the entire subject: his treatise is the last major text claimed by both Hindustani and Carnatic music, so the date of the Sangita Ratnakara effectively dates the beginning of the north–south split.

The criteria for "classical" status, applied by the Sangeet Natak Akademi and the Ministry of Culture, are worth stating because they generate the questions: documented antiquity; a textual basis in a recognised treatise; a codified technique with a distinct repertoire; a clear idea of stylisation (natyadharmi) as against realism (lokadharmi); and a tradition of patronage with trained exponents. Chhau satisfies most of these and is treated as classical by the Ministry of Culture in several official references, while the Sangeet Natak Akademi's formal list remains eight — a genuine institutional discrepancy that question-setters exploit.

7. Patronage, Suppression and Revival: How the Repertoire Actually Survived

The decisive variable behind every classical form is who paid for it. Three patronage sites produced the eight forms: the temple, the court and the monastery–village. Bharatanatyam and Odissi are temple arts performed by dedicated women — the devadasis of Tamil Nadu (who called their own art sadir) and the maharis of the Jagannath temple at Puri. Kuchipudi began as a village dance-drama performed by Brahmin bhagavathulu troupes who also held land grants, which is why men danced female roles (stree vesha) and why the art is inseparable from the village of Kuchipudi in Krishna district. Kathakali grew under Kerala's temples and chieftaincies. Sattriya is monastic, performed inside the Assamese sattra as part of Sankaradeva's fifteenth-century Vaishnavite reform, and it stayed there for four centuries. Manipuri and Mohiniyattam are court arts with ritual roots — Manipur's Ras Leela was codified under Maharaja Bhagyachandra (r. 1759–1798), and Mohiniyattam flourished under Travancore's Swathi Thirunal. Kathak alone moves between two worlds: kathakars narrating the epics in temples, and then court entertainers under the Mughals and under Wajid Ali Shah of Awadh.

Patronage determined everything an examiner can ask about. It determined length — an all-night Kathakali or Yakshagana presumes a patron who feeds an audience; a twenty-minute concert item presumes a ticketed hall. It determined gender: male-only Kathakali, gotipua boy-troupes in Odisha dancing female roles after the maharis were confined to temple service, Brahmin men in Kuchipudi. It determined language — Sanskrit-Telugu-Tamil in Bharatanatyam padams, Odia in Odissi abhinaya, Assamese and the artificial Brajavali of Sankaradeva in Sattriya, Malayalam and Sanskrit in Kathakali's attakatha literature. It even determined music: Kerala's slow, drum-led sopana sangeetham exists because Kathakali and Mohiniyattam needed a vocal style audible against percussion in a temple courtyard, while Tamil temples produced the periya melam of nadaswaram and tavil for processions.

The rupture came in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and it was economic before it was moral. Colonial revenue settlement transferred temple endowments into state hands; evangelical and reformist criticism framed temple dance as prostitution; the anti-nautch campaign of the 1880s–90s removed court patronage from performers; and a series of statutes — the Madras Devadasi (Prevention of Dedication) Act of 1947, Odisha's in the mid-1950s, Karnataka's in 1982 and Andhra Pradesh's in 1988 — ended the hereditary performers' legal and economic base. Muthulakshmi Reddi, the first woman legislator in India, had carried the devadasi abolition bill in the Madras Legislative Council in the 1920s and 1930s, so the reform was Indian-led, not merely colonial. The consequence was double-edged: the art was rescued from stigma and simultaneously severed from the community that carried it. What replaced that community was the urban institution.

The revival was therefore institutional, and its dates and founders are the single most productive table in this lesson. Rukmini Devi Arundale founded Kalakshetra at Adyar in 1936, re-siting Bharatanatyam inside a residential school with a reformed repertoire; Vallathol Narayana Menon founded the Kerala Kalamandalam at Cheruthuruthi in 1930, saving Kathakali from collapse; E. Krishna Iyer argued inside the Madras Music Academy that sadir was art, not vice; and a hereditary exponent, Balasaraswati, preserved the older Tanjore-Pudukkottai lineage outside the institution. Odissi had no single founder but a reconstruction committee in the 1950s — Kelucharan Mohapatra, Deba Prasad Das, Pankaj Charan Das and Mayadhar Raut assembling the form from mahari, gotipua and sculptural evidence — and it was accepted as a classical dance at a Sangeet Natak Akademi seminar in 1958. Sattriya was recognised last, in 2000, when its monastic setting could no longer be mistaken for absence of technique.

Two structural consequences of this history should be stated in any mains answer on the subject. First, the repertoire was standardised for the proscenium: the Tanjore Quartet's margam for Bharatanatyam (reorganised in the early nineteenth century under the Maratha ruler Serfoji II of Thanjavur), Vempati Chinna Satyam's solo Kuchipudi, Kelucharan Mohapatra's Odissi — each is a nineteenth- or twentieth-century consolidation, not an unbroken ancient sequence. Second, the economics moved from patron to market, and the market is now the festival and the sabha: Khajuraho (from 1975), Konark (from 1986), Mamallapuram, the Madras December Season (the Music Academy was founded in 1928), the Tyagaraja Aradhana at Thiruvaiyaru, the Dhrupad Mela at Varanasi and Saptak at Ahmedabad (from 1980). The state entered alongside through the Sangeet Natak Akademi, the Centre for Cultural Resources and Training (1979) and the Indira Gandhi National Centre for the Arts (1987). The cost of all this is well documented: hereditary practitioners — the devadasi and mahari communities — were largely excluded from the prosperity of the revival, a question of cultural justice that now appears in sociology and ethics papers as well as in culture ones.

8. Hindustani and Carnatic: One Grammar, Two Histories

The north–south division of Indian art music is not a difference of race, region or antiquity; it is a difference of patronage history after the thirteenth century. Sarangadeva's Sangita Ratnakara, written in Devagiri just before the Sultanate's Deccan campaigns, still describes a single musical world. Once Delhi's courts absorbed Persian and Central Asian musicians, the northern tradition acquired a new vocabulary (khayal, tarana, qawwali), new instruments and a court-based economics of hereditary households; the south, under Vijayanagara, the Nayakas and later the Marathas of Tanjavur, kept temple endowments and a scribal, guru-centred transmission. The result is two systems that share raga, tala, shruti and rasa but diverge in almost every operational detail.

Raga classification is the clearest divergence and the most tested. The Carnatic melakarta scheme of seventy-two parent ragas was proposed by Venkatamakhin in the seventeenth century in his Chaturdandi Prakasika and given its present names and mnemonic by Govindacharya's Sangraha Chudamani in the eighteenth; it is a generative combinatorial system, built by permuting seven swara positions. The Hindustani thaat system of ten parent scales was formulated by Vishnu Narayan Bhatkhande in the early twentieth century; it is a heuristic filing device, invented precisely because the gharana tradition resisted codification and Bhatkhande wanted music to be teachable in schools. Both men were modernisers responding to the same problem — how to teach an oral art to strangers — and their answers reveal their traditions: the south already had a written grammar, the north had to build one.

Composition and improvisation trade places between the systems. In Carnatic music the kriti is sovereign: a fixed composition by a named composer, elaborated through sangati, niraval and kalpanaswaram, with the ragam-tanam-pallavi as the one fully improvisatory form. In Hindustani music the bandish is a launch pad: the alap-jod-jhala and the improvised taan occupy most of the time, and the composition may be only a few lines. Hence the standard characterisation — Carnatic music is composition-oriented with structured improvisation, Hindustani is improvisation-oriented around a composed nucleus. Ornamentation differs for the same reason: gamaka in the south is a defined oscillation notated in the teaching tradition, while meend and murki in the north are gharana-specific and learned by ear.

Transmission and institution follow. The north organises itself into gharanas — households with a signature style and usually a hereditary core: Gwalior (the oldest, associated with Man Singh Tomar's patronage and the Haddu-Hassu Khan), Agra, Kirana (Abdul Karim Khan; Sawai Gandharva; Bhimsen Joshi), Jaipur-Atrauli (Alladiya Khan; Mogubai Kurdikar; Kishori Amonkar), Patiala (Bade Ghulam Ali Khan), Rampur-Sahaswan, Mewati (Pandit Jasraj), Bhendibazar and Benares for thumri (Girija Devi). The south organises itself into parampara and, since 1928, into the sabha — the Madras Music Academy's December Season created a market in which a musician's standing was certified by public concerts rather than by lineage. Two institutions then made the difference permanent: Vishnu Digambar Paluskar's Gandharva Mahavidyalaya (Lahore, 1901) with its written notation and graded examinations, and the Akhil Bharatiya Gandharva Mahavidyalaya Mandal (1931), which turned Hindustani music into a certificated subject. Purandara Dasa had done something similar in the south four centuries earlier, and is remembered as the Pitamaha for exactly that reason — he systematised beginner lessons and fixed Mayamalavagowla as the teaching raga.

The named composers complete the picture. In the south the Trinity — Tyagaraja (1767–1847, Telugu kritis and the five Pancharatna kritis sung at his annual aradhana), Muthuswami Dikshitar (1775–1835, Sanskrit kritis including the Navagraha set) and Syama Sastri (1762–1827, swarajatis) — worked within a generation of each other in the Thanjavur delta, and the delta's Maratha court is why Carnatic music has an unbroken composer literature. In the north the equivalent figure is Tansen, one of Akbar's nine jewels, credited with Miyan ki Malhar and with the dhrupad line descending from Gwalior; the form that displaced dhrupad, khayal, is conventionally attributed to Amir Khusrau but was in fact popularised by Sadarang (Niyamat Khan) in the court of Muhammad Shah Rangila in the eighteenth century. Attribution questions test exactly this gap between legend and evidence.

9. Instruments, Folk Forms and the Registers of Recognition

The Natyashastra's four-fold classification of instruments is asked almost every year because it is a clean sorting exercise: tata (chordophones — sitar, sarod, veena, santoor, sarangi, violin), sushira (aerophones — bansuri, shehnai, nadaswaram), avanaddha (membranophones — tabla, pakhawaj, mridangam, chenda, maddalam, khol, dhol, kanjira) and ghana (solid idiophones — ghatam, manjira, jal tarang, morsing). The traps are the instruments that look like one class and behave like another: the ghatam is a clay pot, so it is ghana and not a drum; the kanjira is a small frame drum with a lizard-skin face, so it is avanaddha despite its size; and the violin, a European import adapted for Carnatic music by Baluswami Dikshitar in the late eighteenth century, is tata but never indigenous.

Folk and ritual forms are best sorted by origin rather than by state, because the state–form pair is only half the question and the origin explains why the form looks as it does. Possession rituals — Theyyam and Mudiyettu in Kerala, Padayani, the Bhuta Kola of coastal Karnataka — are performed by communities for whom the dancer is not an artist but a vessel, which is why they are performed at night, in a fixed ritual season, and outside any aesthetic grammar. Narrative ballad forms — Pandavani in Chhattisgarh (Teejan Bai), Alha in Bundelkhand, Powada in Maharashtra, Burrakatha in Andhra, the Baul songs of Bengal — carried history and philosophy in regions without mass literacy. Calendar forms — Bhangra and Giddha, Garba, Bihu, Ghoomar and Kalbelia, Rouf, Dalkhai, Chhau at Chaitra Parva — are bound to harvest and festival dates. Martial forms, above all the three Chhau styles (Seraikella in Jharkhand with masks and royal patronage, Mayurbhanj in Odisha without masks, Purulia in West Bengal with large masks made at Charida), descend from training drills, which is why their vocabulary is of jumps, turns and simulated combat rather than abhinaya.

Recognition runs through three distinct registers, and confusing them is a common error. The Sangeet Natak Akademi, established by a Government of India resolution in 1952 and inaugurated in 1953, now autonomous under the Ministry of Culture, confers its Awards (since 1952), its Fellowship or Ratna Sadasya (since 1954) and the Ustad Bismillah Khan Yuva Puraskar (since 2006); it is also the body whose list of eight classical dances is the standard reference. The Ministry of Culture and Parliament confer institutional status — Kalakshetra was declared an institution of national importance by an Act of Parliament in 1993, and the Kerala Kalamandalam became a deemed university in 2006. UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, operating under the 2003 Convention, confers international recognition with a safeguarding obligation. India's performing-arts inscriptions on that list are Kutiyattam (proclaimed in 2001), Ramlila (2005), the Baul songs (2008), Ramman of Uttarakhand (2009), Chhau, Kalbelia and Mudiyettu (2010), the Buddhist chanting of Ladakh (2012), Sankirtana of Manipur (2013) and Garba of Gujarat (2023); with Deepavali, inscribed in December 2025 at the committee's twentieth session held at the Red Fort — the first time India hosted it — the national total moved to sixteen elements. Note that Chhau's UNESCO status does not settle its classical status: the two registers answer different questions.

Trap watch. The Sangeet Natak Akademi's list has eight classical dances; Chhau is a Ministry-of-Culture-level "ninth" and is on UNESCO's ICH list, but the Akademi has not reclassified it. Odissi was accepted as classical at a 1958 Akademi seminar and Sattriya was recognised in 2000 — in that order, not the reverse. Tarangam on a brass plate belongs to Kuchipudi and derives from Narayana Tirtha's Krishna Leela Tarangini, while the Bhama Kalapam is Siddhendra Yogi's — the two are routinely swapped. Kathakali's drums are the chenda and maddalam, Sattriya's are the khol and taal, Manipur's pung cholom uses the pung, and Kutiyattam's is the mizhavu; Kerala's slow temple singing is sopana sangeetham with the idakka. The 72 melakarta ragas are Venkatamakhin's seventeenth-century scheme and the ten thaats are Bhatkhande's twentieth-century one; Purandara Dasa is the Pitamaha of Carnatic music but is not one of the Trinity. Kalakshetra (Rukmini Devi, Adyar, 1936) and the Kerala Kalamandalam (Vallathol, Cheruthuruthi, 1930) are close in date and opposite in region. Khayal is attributed to Amir Khusrau but was popularised by Sadarang under Muhammad Shah Rangila; thumri belongs to Wajid Ali Shah's Lucknow, not to Akbar's court. Sankirtana (Manipur, 2013) is ritual drumming-and-singing, not the Manipuri Ras Leela. Manipuri dancers wear no ankle bells; two classical forms — Kathakali and Mohiniyattam — come from Kerala. And India now has sixteen UNESCO ICH elements, not the fifteen still printed in older notes.
