Fairs and Festivals — Detailed Notes

Anchor facts only: where, when, why, and the UNESCO-intangible list. Prelims loves state-festival mismatch.

1. Kumbh Mela

  • 4 sites rotation: Haridwar (Ganga), Prayagraj (sangam), Ujjain (Shipra — Simhastha), Nashik (Godavari); 12-year cycle + ardha/magh variants; UNESCO ICH 2017; akhara processions (shahi snan procession order); 2019 Prayagraj largest gathering record.

2. Regional festival anchors

  • Harvest trio: Pongal (TN, Jan — Thai month; Mattu day), Baisakhi/Lohri/Maghi (Punjab — Khalsa founding 1699 hook), Bihu (Assam — Bohag magh kati trio; Rongali = spring).
  • South: Onam (Kerala — Mahabali-Vamana; pookalam, snake-boat Vallam Kali), Thrissur Pooram (Sakthan Thampuran origin), Hampi Utsav, Pongal-adjacent Mattu Pongal.
  • East: Durga Puja (WB — UNESCO ICH 2021), Chhath (Bihar-Jharkhand — Surya-Shashthi, Kartik), Rath Yatra (Puri — 3 chariots; Jagannath, Balabhadra, Subhadra), Ambubachi Mela (Kamakhya, Assam — tantric, temple closes).
  • North: Hornbill Festival (Nagaland Dec-1 — 'festival of festivals', tribal unity), Losar (Ladakh-Arunachal, Tibetan new year), Baisakhi hook, Gangaur-Teej (Rajasthan — Gauri), Makar Sankranti family (Uttarayan Gujarat kites, Magh Bihu, Poush Sankranti Bengal, Suggi Karnataka).
  • West: Pushkar Fair (Kartik, camel trade), Shigmo (Goa spring), Gudi Padwa (Maharashtra new year), Modhera dance festival (Sun temple).
  • NE-tribal: Chapchar Kut (Mizoram), Wangala (Garo — 100-drum), Moatsu (Ao Naga), Sarhul (Jharkhand tribal spring), Nuakhai (Odisha new rice).

3. Fair classics (commodity + faith)

  • Pushkar (Rajasthan — camels, Brahma temple), Sonepur (Bihar — cattle+elephants, Harihar Kshetra), Surajkund Mela (Haryana — crafts, Feb), Hemis (Ladakh — Guru Padmasambhava mask), Gangasagar (WB Makar Sankranti), Nauchandi (Meerut), Bateshwar (UP cattle fair), Shamlaji (Gujarat tribal).

4. UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage — India

  • Kutiyattam (2001), Vedic chanting (2003), Ramlila (2005), Ramman (Uttarakhand, 2009), Mudiyettu (Kerala, 2010), Chhau (2010), Kalbelia (2010), Buddhist chanting of Ladakh (2012), Sankirtana (Manipur, 2013), Traditional brass/utensil craft of Thatheras (Punjab, 2014 — only craft), Yoga (2016), Nawrouz (multinational, 2016), Kumbh Mela (2017), Kolkata Durga Puja (2021), Garba of Gujarat (2023). Chronology asked directly.

MCQ hooks

  • 4 Kumbh cities + rivers pair (Shipra-Ujjain, Godavari-Nashik).
  • Thatheras = only craft on the ICH list (2014).
  • Hornbill = Dec 1, Nagaland; Chapchar Kut = Mizoram.
  • Ambubachi = Kamakhya closure, not open darshan.
  • Onam snake-boats = Vallam Kali (Aranmula).
  • Durga Puja 2021 & Garba 2023 = latest India adds.

6. Fairs and Festivals as Cultural Geography

Festivals are not merely religious observances; for the examination they are best understood as expressions of agricultural cycles, astronomical events, regional identity and syncretic history. Almost every major Indian festival can be traced to one of four origins: the harvest, the solar or lunar calendar, a mythological or historical commemoration, or a tribal or community assembly. Recognising the origin usually determines the season, and the season in turn determines the region — a structure far more reliable than rote memorisation.

Harvest festivals form the largest family and are best learned as a January cluster corresponding to the winter solstice and the entry of the Sun into Capricorn, Makara Sankranti. Across regions the same moment is Pongal in Tamil Nadu, a four-day festival of Bhogi, Thai Pongal, Mattu Pongal for cattle and Kaanum Pongal; Lohri in Punjab, with bonfires on the eve; Magh Bihu or Bhogali Bihu in Assam, with community feasting and the meji bonfire; Uttarayan in Gujarat with kite-flying; Makar Sankranti in Maharashtra and the north; and Poush Parbon in Bengal. Note that Sankranti is the one major Hindu festival fixed to the solar calendar, which is why it falls on nearly the same date each year while others shift.

The spring harvest brings Baisakhi in Punjab in April, which also commemorates the founding of the Khalsa by Guru Gobind Singh in 1699; Rongali Bihu in Assam, the Assamese new year; Vishu in Kerala with the kani arrangement; Puthandu in Tamil Nadu; Poila Boishakh in Bengal; Ugadi in the Deccan and Gudi Padwa in Maharashtra marking the lunar new year. The autumn harvest gives Onam in Kerala, commemorating the return of King Mahabali, with the Pookalam floral carpet, Onasadya feast, Vallamkali snake-boat race and Pulikali tiger dance; and Nuakhai in western Odisha, the first-rice festival.

Regional and tribal festivals should be paired firmly with their state, since that is how questions are framed: Hornbill of Nagaland, the "festival of festivals" at Kisama; Sekrenyi of the Angami; Losar of the Buddhist Himalaya; Hemis in Ladakh honouring Padmasambhava with the cham masked dance; Wangala, the hundred-drums festival of the Garo; Nongkrem of the Khasi; Chapchar Kut of Mizoram with the bamboo dance; Sarhul and Karma of Jharkhand's Oraon and Munda; Bhagoria of the Bhil of Madhya Pradesh; Sammakka Saralamma Jatara of Telangana, among the largest tribal gatherings in the world; Ambubachi Mela at Kamakhya; and Bihu's three forms — Rongali in spring, Kongali in autumn and Bhogali in winter.

7. Pan-Indian Festivals, Fairs and Melas

Pan-Indian festivals and their regional variants: Diwali, celebrated over five days as Dhanteras, Naraka Chaturdashi, Lakshmi Puja, Govardhan Puja and Bhai Dooj, and marked as Kali Puja in Bengal, Bandi Chhor Divas by Sikhs commemorating Guru Hargobind's release, and as a significant date for Jains as Mahavira's nirvana. Holi, with the Holika Dahan bonfire, and its distinctive forms — Lathmar Holi at Barsana, Basanta Utsav at Santiniketan, Hola Mohalla of the Nihang Sikhs at Anandpur Sahib, Yaosang in Manipur, Shigmo in Goa and Dol Jatra in Bengal. Navaratri and Dussehra, expressed as Durga Puja in Bengal, Garba and Dandiya in Gujarat, Bommai Kolu in Tamil Nadu, Mysore Dasara with the Jamboo Savari procession, Kullu Dussehra which begins when others end, and Bathukamma in Telangana. Ganesh Chaturthi, revived as a public festival by Bal Gangadhar Tilak to build nationalist solidarity — a point that links culture to the freedom movement and is worth deploying in answers.

Fairs and melas are distinct from festivals in being periodic assemblies with an economic as well as a devotional function. The Kumbh Mela, inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of Intangible Cultural Heritage, rotates every twelve years among Prayagraj, Haridwar, Ujjain and Nashik — on the Ganga-Yamuna-Saraswati confluence, the Ganga, the Shipra and the Godavari respectively — with the Ardh Kumbh at six-year intervals and the Magh Mela annually at Prayagraj. Others to place: the Pushkar camel fair in Rajasthan; Sonepur cattle fair in Bihar, Asia's largest; the Rath Yatra of Jagannath at Puri; Gangasagar Mela at Sagar Island; Thrissur Pooram in Kerala with caparisoned elephants and the kudamattam parasol exchange; the Ambubachi and Pushkaram river festivals; Baneshwar and Nagaur fairs in Rajasthan; the Surajkund Crafts Mela in Haryana; the Goa Carnival, of Portuguese origin; the Desert Festival at Jaisalmer; and the Rann Utsav in Kutch.

Festivals of other faiths are equally examinable. Islamic observances follow the purely lunar Hijri calendar, which is about eleven days shorter than the solar year, so Eid-ul-Fitr marking the end of Ramzan, Eid-ul-Adha or Bakrid, Muharram and Milad-un-Nabi move earlier each Gregorian year — a reliable question. Urs festivals at Sufi dargahs, notably Ajmer Sharif, are important sites of syncretic practice. Christian festivals include Christmas, Easter and Good Friday, with distinctive regional forms in Goa and Kerala. Buddha Purnima commemorates birth, enlightenment and parinirvana on a single day; Mahavir Jayanti and Paryushana are the principal Jain observances; Guru Nanak Jayanti and Guru Gobind Singh Jayanti, along with Baisakhi and Hola Mohalla, are the Sikh calendar's anchors; the Parsi Navroz marks the new year; and Jewish and Bahá'í communities add further diversity.

8. Calendars, Cultural Significance and Heritage Policy

Calendars explain why dates move. The Indian national calendar is the Saka era, adopted in 1957, beginning in 78 CE with Chaitra as the first month. The Vikram Samvat begins in 57 BCE. Most Hindu festivals follow a luni-solar reckoning, in which months are lunar but an intercalary month, adhika masa, is inserted roughly every three years to keep the lunar year aligned with the solar seasons — which is why festivals shift within a range of weeks but do not drift through the seasons, unlike purely lunar Islamic dates. Regional variants such as the amanta system ending the month at the new moon in the south and the purnimanta ending at the full moon in the north account for differing names of the same festival.

Why festivals matter analytically. A good mains answer treats festivals as performing identifiable social functions: they sustain social cohesion and collective identity in Durkheim's sense; they encode ecological knowledge, timing sowing, harvest and the monsoon; they support livelihoods in crafts, textiles, food and tourism, with the intangible-heritage economy now a recognised policy area; they enable syncretism, as at Sufi urs and Bonalu, where communities cross confessional lines; and they periodically become sites of political mobilisation, as Tilak's Ganesh festival shows. They also carry costs worth acknowledging in a balanced answer — idol immersion and water pollution, noise, plastic waste, crowd-safety failures and stampedes, and the commercialisation of ritual — against which lie the responses of eco-friendly idols, the Central Pollution Control Board's immersion guidelines, and crowd-management planning at large melas.

Heritage frameworks to cite: UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage list, on which India's entries include the Kumbh Mela, Vedic chanting, Ramlila, Kutiyattam, Ramman, Chhau dance, Kalbelia, Buddhist chanting of Ladakh, Sankirtana of Manipur, Thatheras of Jandiala Guru, Yoga, Nawrouz, Durga Puja in Kolkata and Garba of Gujarat. Institutions: the Sangeet Natak Akademi, Lalit Kala Akademi, Sahitya Akademi, the Indira Gandhi National Centre for the Arts, the Zonal Cultural Centres, the Ministry of Culture's schemes for Guru-Shishya Parampara and repertory grants, and the Ek Bharat Shreshtha Bharat programme pairing states for cultural exchange.

Trap watch. Makar Sankranti follows the solar calendar and so falls on nearly the same date annually; most other Hindu festivals are luni-solar and shift. Islamic festivals follow a purely lunar calendar and move about eleven days earlier each year. The Kumbh rotates among four sites on four different rivers — Ujjain on the Shipra, Nashik on the Godavari. Bihu has three forms in three seasons. Hornbill is Nagaland, Wangala the Garo, Chapchar Kut Mizoram, Sarhul Jharkhand. The Saka era is the national calendar, beginning 78 CE. Tilak made Ganesh Chaturthi a public nationalist festival.
