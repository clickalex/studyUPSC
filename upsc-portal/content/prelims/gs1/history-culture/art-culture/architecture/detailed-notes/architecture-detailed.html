Indian Architecture — Detailed Notes

Four exam arenas: stupas & caves, temple styles, Indo-Islamic, modern-colonial. Every question is a match-the-pair.

1. Stupas & caves

  • Stupa anatomy: anda (dome) on medhi, harmika railing-box, chhatra umbrellas, vedika railing, torana gates (Sanchi's four — Satavahana-era carvings: Jatakas, aniconic Buddha).
  • Sequence: Ashoka core (Sanchi-1, Bharhut, Piprahwa relic-claims) → Sunga railings → Satavahana gates (Sanchi) → Amaravati (white-marble narrative, Satavahana-Ikshvaku) → Gandhara dharmarajika (Kanishka's relic chambers).
  • Caves: Barabar (Ashoka, Lomas Rishi chaitya-arch facade — earliest) → western chaitya-vihara schools (Bhaja, Kondane, Karle — Karle chaitya grandest, Nashik Pandav leni inscriptions of Gautamiputra/Ushavadata) → Ajanta (Hinayana + Mahayana phases: 9,10,12,13 early; 1,2,16,17 Vakataka) → Ellora (Buddhist-Hindu-Jain; Kailasa temple 16 — Rashtrakuta Krishna I, monolithic) → Elephanta (Shiva panels — Rashtrakuta attribution) → Jain rows (Gwalior, Ellora 30-34).

3. Indo-Islamic arc

  • Sultanate: Qutub Minar (Iltutmish completes; Aibak begins), Alai Darwaza (Alauddin — first true arch + dome finishing), Tughlaq battered walls + sloping, Khirki mosque (Khan-i-Jahan), Lodi octagonal tomb-gardens (first garden tombs), Jaunpur Sharqui, Bengal curved-roof (Adina mosque, Pandua), Gujarat white-marble blends.
  • Mughal: Humayun's tomb (first grand garden tomb, charbagh), Purana Qila, Akbar: Fatehpur Sikri (Buland Darwaza; Panch Mahal, Diwan-i-Khas pillar; red sandstone + Gujarati-Chaulukya brackets), Allahabad/Agra forts, Shah Jahan: Taj (1632-53; pietra dura, double dome), Jama Masjid, Red Fort (Diwan-i-Am/Khas, Rang Mahal), Shalimar (Lahore); Sikh: Harmandir Sahib (gold leaf — Ranjit Singh era); Rajput: Amber, Mehrangarh, Udaipur palaces.
  • Colonial: classical revival (Victoria Memorial — Indo-Saracenic hybrid is different: Chepauk, Gateway of India, Lutyens' New Delhi blend — Viceroy's House dome).

MCQ hooks

  • First true arch/dome → Alai Darwaza; first garden tomb → Humayun's; charbagh peak → Taj.
  • Pietra dura (parchin kari) = Taj inlay; panch mahal = Fatehpur Sikri.
  • Kailasa (Ellora 16) carved top-down monolith = Krishna I.
  • Stupa 'anda-harmika-chhatra' order asked verbatim.
  • Konark wheels = sundial function; jagamohana type = deul pair (Odisha: rekha deul + pidha deul).
  • Hoysala star platform + soapstone = Vesara terminal glory.

6. Materials, Liturgy and Power: Why Indian Architecture Took the Shapes It Did

Indian architecture is usually taught as a list of monuments and then remembered as a list of traps. The list becomes stable only when three determinants are understood, because almost every formal feature an examination asks about can be derived from one of them: the structural grammar available, the liturgy the building had to serve, and the political message the patron wished to send.

The first determinant is technical. India built trabeate — post-and-lintel — from the Indus cities until the thirteenth century: columns carry beams, spans stay short. Builders knew the corbelled arch, in which courses project inward until they meet, and this is what Iltutmish's tomb (1235) and most Lodi tombs use. What they did not know was the true arch, built of wedge-shaped voussoirs closing on a keystone, nor the squinch and pendentive that carry a round dome across a square room. Those techniques arrived with the Sultanate, and the first Indian building to deploy them properly is the Alai Darwaza of 1311. That fact explains an otherwise puzzling monument: Aibak's Quwwat-ul-Islam mosque (begun 1198) is visibly built from the columns of demolished temples because trabeate components could be lifted and re-erected, whereas an arcuate building could not be assembled from another tradition's rubble. Spolia were not merely an insult recorded in an inscription; they were the only economical use of the material at hand.

The second determinant is material, and it produces most regional signatures. Sandstone gave Fatehpur Sikri and Shahjahanabad their mass; granite gave the Chola vimana its immensity but denied it filigree — and the Thanjavur temple's real problem was that the Kaveri delta has no granite outcrops, so the stone travelled. Chloritic schist (soapstone), used by the Hoysalas, is soft when quarried and hardens on exposure, which is why Belur and Halebidu carry jewellery-like carving and lathe-turned pillars a granite mason could never have cut. Marble belongs to Dilwara, Itmad-ud-Daulah and the Taj, and permits pietra dura; basalt made Ellora possible; brick dominated Bengal and the Ahom mounds, and brick cannot hold a lintel — hence Bengal's curved cornice, an imitation in masonry of the bamboo-and-thatch roof that preceded it, a form the Mughals then adopted as the bangladar roof. Timber survives almost nowhere, but its ghost is everywhere in stone: the ribbed vaults of Bhaja and Karle imitate wooden rafters, and the chaitya arch is a petrified timber facade.

The third determinant is liturgical, and it separates the two great monument types of ancient India. A stupa is circumambulated, not entered: the relic is sealed inside, so the architecture concentrates on the path around it — vedika, pradakshina patha, and torana gateways on the cardinal axes, each a surface for narrative sculpture. A temple is entered: the image sits in the garbhagriha, so the architecture concentrates on an axis — gateway, courtyard, mandapa, vestibule, sanctum — with a superstructure over the sanctum alone. The two idioms then evolve in opposite directions for one reason. The Dravida temple grows outward, adding concentric prakaras and taller gopurams, because a temple housing a town's ritual life must enclose processions, tanks, kitchens and thousands of pilgrims: Srirangam has seven enclosures, Madurai twelve towers. The Nagara temple grows upward into one dominant shikhara, because its ritual economy is smaller and its statement vertical. This logic lets a candidate place an unfamiliar monument without having seen it.

Patronage then decides scale and ostentation. Inscriptions at Sanchi and Karle record that gateways, pillars and whole chaitya halls were paid for by merchant guilds, ivory carvers of Vidisha, monks, nuns and a court officer — collective commercial patronage, which is why Satavahana-period Buddhist art is dense with narrative and donor portraits rather than royal iconography. Royal patronage reads differently: Rajaraja I named the Thanjavur temple Rajarajesvaram and had his endowments cut into its walls; Krishna I hollowed a hill into the Kailasa at Ellora. Austerity is political too: the Tughlaqs' battered walls, grey quartzite and refusal of inlay answer a treasury strained by Muhammad bin Tughlaq's experiments, while unfinished monuments record ambition outrunning resources — the colossal lingam and surviving earthen ramp at Bhojpur, and the base alone of Alauddin's Alai Minar, intended to be twice Qutb Minar's height.

7. Stupa, Chaitya and Vihara: the Rock-Cut Order

Rock-cut architecture is subtractive: it is sculpture rather than construction. Three consequences are constantly tested. Nothing can be corrected once cut, so iconography is conservative and plans repeat for centuries; the "builder" is a master quarryman working top-downwards, which is why the Kailasa at Ellora had to be planned with total accuracy before the first cut; and the technique permits monolithic results impossible in masonry, since column, wall, shrine and sculpture are one piece of rock.

The earliest surviving rock-cut chambers in India are not Buddhist. In the Barabar and Nagarjuni hills of Bihar, Ashoka and his grandson Dasharatha gave caves to Ajivika ascetics; the Lomas Rishi cave carries the first surviving chaitya-arch facade, with carved elephants, and its interior shows the Mauryan polish. The distinction that follows from Buddhist practice, and that is asked every few years, is between a chaitya (a prayer hall, apsidal in plan, stupa at the far end, vaulted ceiling imitating timber ribs) and a vihara (a monastery, cells around a hall, and in the later Mahayana phase a shrine carved into the rear wall holding a Buddha image).

The sequence is then a matter of patronage shifting from kings to guilds to dynasties: Bhaja and Bedsa (second century BCE) → Karle (first century CE), whose chaitya is the grandest of the early Deccan, with a donor-inscribed wooden umbrella over its stupa → the Pandav Leni at Nashik, where the inscriptions of Ushavadata (Nahapana's son-in-law) and of Gautamiputra Satakarni's family document endowments → Ajanta → Ellora → Elephanta. Ajanta needs separate treatment because it has two phases with different doctrines: caves 9, 10, 12, 13 and 15A belong to the early Hinayana phase (second century BCE to first century CE), while caves 1, 2, 16, 17, 19 and 26 belong to a frantic Mahayana/Vakataka burst of roughly 460–478 CE under Harishena, his minister Varahadeva (whose inscription is in cave 16) and the local ruler Upendragupta. Several caves were left unfinished when patronage collapsed, which is why the site preserves working detail, and why its paintings — the Padmapani and Vajrapani of cave 1, the "dying princess" of cave 17 — survive in dark, dry interiors until a British officer hunting tigers, John Smith, stumbled on cave 10 in 1819. Ellora is the multi-religious site (Buddhist 1–12, Hindu 13–29, Jain 30–34), and its cave 16, the Kailasa, was cut for the Rashtrakuta Krishna I in the late eighth century — gateway, Nandi pavilion, pillared hall, sanctum and epic panels excavated downwards from one basalt hill, with perhaps two hundred thousand tonnes of rock removed. Elephanta's Trimurti Sadashiva panel, attributed variously to the Kalachuris and the Rashtrakutas and named by the Portuguese after a stone elephant since moved to a Mumbai garden, belongs to the same tradition; outside the west, Udayagiri and Khandagiri in Odisha, associated with Kharavela of the Hathigumpha inscription, are the Jain counter-example.

The narrative schools of sculpture follow trade routes and dynasties, and their differences are diagnostic. Sanchi and Bharhut (Shunga, second century BCE) are aniconic: the Buddha appears as footprints, an empty throne, a bodhi tree or a wheel. Human images appear only in the first to second centuries CE, in two unrelated places — Gandhara, under Kushana patronage, in grey schist and later stucco with Greco-Roman drapery and Apollonian features; and Mathura, in red-spotted sandstone, growing out of the indigenous yaksha tradition, with a shaven head, transparent muslin and, later, a halo. The third school, Amaravati and Nagarjunakonda under the Satavahanas and Ikshvagus, worked in white Palnadu marble with slender bodies in violent movement, and its style travelled by sea to Southeast Asia. The examinable point is the inference, not the aesthetic: the stone tells you the quarry, the quarry the region, and the region the patron and the trade network.

8. The Three Temple Idioms and the Politics Behind Them

The Nagara–Dravida–Vesara triad is less a stylistic taxonomy than a record of three political geographies. Nagara is the idiom of northern kingdoms whose temples stood as landmarks on a plain: a curvilinear shikhara crowned by amalaka and kalasha, usually no enclosing wall or monumental gateway, a mandapa before the sanctum, and often the panchayatana plan with four subsidiary shrines at the corners. Its regional schools are diagnostic. In Odisha (the Kalinga school) the temple is a sequence of halls in three deul types — rekha (curvilinear, over the sanctum), pidha (stepped, over the halls) and khakhara (barrel-vaulted, for goddess shrines) — with jagamohana, natamandira and bhogamandapa added in front: Lingaraja at Bhubaneswar (eleventh century, with Ganga additions), Jagannath at Puri (Anantavarman Chodaganga Ganga, twelfth century) and Konark (Narasimhadeva I, c. 1238–1264), whose twenty-four wheels are working sundials and whose tower has collapsed, leaving the sand-filled jagamohana that British engineers sealed in 1903. In central India the Chandela temples of Khajuraho, above all the Kandariya Mahadeva of the early eleventh century, place erotic sculpture as one narrow band among many rather than as the subject. In Gujarat the Solanki idiom pairs temple with water: Modhera's Sun temple under Bhima I (c. 1026–27) with its Surya Kund tank, and the Rani ki Vav of Queen Udayamati (1063).

Dravida is the idiom of the south, where the temple became a town. It begins with the Pallavas, who are useful because their monuments document the transition from cutting rock to building with it: Mahendravarman I's mandapa at Mandagapettu carries an inscription boasting that he built without brick, timber, metal or mortar; Narasimhavarman I (Mamalla) cut the rathas and the great open-air relief at Mamallapuram; Narasimhavarman II (Rajasimha) built the first structural stone temples — the Shore Temple and the Kailasanatha at Kanchipuram. The Cholas then raised the scale: Brihadeshwara at Thanjavur, completed by Rajaraja I in 1010, has a vimana of roughly sixty-six metres and a capstone weighing tens of tonnes hauled up a long earthen ramp; Rajendra I built Gangaikondacholapuram about 1035 with a deliberately lower but broader tower; Rajaraja II's Airavatesvara at Darasuram completed the set. UNESCO inscribed the three together in 1987, extended in 2004, as the Great Living Chola Temples — "living" matters, because functioning shrines force conservation to be negotiated with the temple rather than imposed on it. The Pandya and Nayaka periods pushed the logic further by enlarging the gopuram until the gateway dwarfed the sanctum: Meenakshi at Madurai, rebuilt under Viswanatha and Tirumalai Nayaka, and Srirangam, whose 73-metre rajagopuram was completed only in 1987 — a warning against dating a temple by its tallest element. Vijayanagara added its own vocabulary at Hampi: the kalyana and ratha mandapas, the stone chariot and musical pillars of the Vittala temple, and the Krishna temple built after Krishnadevaraya's Kalinga campaign.

Vesara is the Deccan hybrid, and its logic is political: the Chalukyas ruled the zone where the two idioms met and used both. At Pattadakal (UNESCO 1987) the Virupaksha temple, built by Queen Lokamahadevi to commemorate Vikramaditya II's conquest of Kanchipuram, stands beside the Papanatha temple, which carries a Dravida body under a Nagara crown — two grammars on one site. At Aihole the Durga temple borrows the apsidal plan of a Buddhist chaitya for a Hindu shrine, which makes it the most frequent trap in the triad. The Rashtrakutas took the experiment to its extreme at Ellora; the Later Chalukyas of Kalyani refined it at Ittagi and Lakkundi; and the Hoysalas completed it in chloritic schist with star-shaped plans that multiply light and shadow, a jagati platform for circumambulation, lathe-turned pillars and signed sculptors such as Mallithamma and Dasoja. Their three ensembles — Chennakesava at Belur (1113, Vishnuvardhana), Hoysaleswara at Halebidu (1121, built by the officer Ketamalla) and Keshava at Somanathpur (1258) — were inscribed in 2023 as the Sacred Ensembles of the Hoysalas. A fourth idiom belongs in the same conversation because examiners use it as the odd one out: the Kakatiya Ramappa (Rudreshwara) temple at Palampet, built in 1213 by the general Recharla Rudra for Ganapati Deva, with sandbox foundations and "floating" pillars, inscribed by UNESCO in 2021.

9. Arcuate Synthesis, Colonial Hybridity and the Modern State

The Sultanate's contribution reads best as a sequence of engineering firsts rather than as monuments. The Qutb Minar is a four-ruler sequence: Aibak began it in 1199 and built the first storey, Iltutmish added three, lightning destroyed the top in 1368 and Firoz Shah Tughlaq rebuilt it in marble and sandstone, and Sikandar Lodi repaired it again — which is why "who completed the Qutb Minar" has two defensible answers depending on the options. The Alai Darwaza (1311) introduced the true arch, the finished dome and the squinch. The Tughlaqs added battered walls and grey quartzite; Khan-i-Jahan Maqbul's Khirki Masjid abandoned the open courtyard for four gateways and a roofed interior; Firoz Shah transported two Ashokan pillars from Topra and Meerut to Delhi, an early act of imperial antiquarianism. Sayyid and Lodi tombs introduced the octagonal plan set in a garden — Delhi's first garden-tombs and the formal ancestor of Humayun's. The provinces diverged productively: Jaunpur's Sharqi Atala Masjid hid its dome behind an enormous pylon; Bengal's Adina Mosque at Pandua (Sikandar Shah, 1374) translated Damascus-derived planning into brick with a curved cornice; Gujarat's Jami Masjid at Ahmedabad (1423) and the stone jaalis of the Sidi Saiyyed mosque (1573) fused Hindu craft with an Islamic plan; Mandu's Jahaz Mahal showed Malwa's water-garden architecture.

The Mughal arc is a chain of rehearsals. Humayun's Tomb (1572), commissioned by Bega Begum and built by the Persian-trained Mirak Mirza Ghiyas, introduced the grand charbagh garden tomb with water channels, red sandstone with white marble trim and a true double dome — an inner dome for interior proportion, an outer for the skyline. Akbar's Fatehpur Sikri (from 1571) makes syncretism structural: the Buland Darwaza commemorating the Gujarat victory, the Diwan-i-Khas with its central pillar, Gujarati-Chaulukya brackets in the residential quarters. Itmad-ud-Daulah at Agra (1628), Nur Jahan's tomb for her father, was the first fully marble building with pietra dura — the technical rehearsal for the Taj Mahal (1632–53), where Ustad Ahmad Lahori's team combined the double dome, inward-tilting minarets, Amanat Khan's calligraphy and inlay on a scale never repeated. Shahjahanabad's Red Fort and Jama Masjid (1656), Awadh's imambaras at Lucknow, Hyderabad's Charminar (1591) and the Sikh Harmandir Sahib, whose gilding belongs to Ranjit Singh's period, complete the picture, alongside Rajput works — Amber under Man Singh I, Rana Kumbha's Vijay Stambha at Chittorgarh (1448), Mehrangarh, and Jaipur's 1727 grid planned by Vidyadhar Bhattacharya on shastric principles.

The colonial phase is a study in deliberate style. Indo-Saracenic was a legitimation strategy: British rule presenting itself as heir of the Mughals by clothing European plans in domes, chhajjas, jalis and pointed arches. Chepauk Palace in Madras (Robert Chisholm, 1870s) is usually named as its first instance; the Madras High Court, Muir Central College, the Victoria Memorial (William Emerson, 1906–21) and the Gateway of India (George Wittet, completed 1924) are its grandest. It must be separated from two neighbours that questions habitually confuse with it — the Victorian Gothic of Bombay, Chhatrapati Shivaji Terminus (F.W. Stevens, 1888) and the Rajabai Clock Tower, which is European medieval revival; and Lutyens' New Delhi, imperially classical in mass with Indian detail applied as ornament and a Rashtrapati Bhavan dome alluding to the Sanchi stupa.

Independence produced a second argument about what an Indian building should be. Otto Königsberger's master plan for Bhubaneswar (1946) and Le Corbusier's Chandigarh (from 1951; the Capitol Complex inscribed by UNESCO in 2016 within a multinational listing) represented imported modernism, while B.V. Doshi — the first Indian Pritzker laureate, in 2018 — Charles Correa (Gandhi Smarak Sangrahalaya, Jawahar Kala Kendra) and Raj Rewal (Hall of Nations, Parliament Library) argued that climate, courtyard and shade, not glass, were India's modern idiom. The demolition of Rewal's Hall of Nations in 2017 remains the standard case in debates about protecting post-independence heritage, which the monuments law cannot easily reach because its protections run on age.

The conservation framework completes the picture. The Archaeological Survey of India was founded in 1861 under Alexander Cunningham; Curzon's Ancient Monuments Preservation Act of 1904 gave the first statutory protection; the AMASR Act of 1958 is the operative law, and its 2010 amendment created the National Monument Authority and fixed a prohibited area of 100 metres and a regulated area of 200 metres around centrally protected monuments — the clause behind most litigation over construction near temples and mosques. Documentation runs through the National Mission on Monuments and Antiquities (2007), and international recognition through UNESCO, where India's count reached 45 in 2026: the Maratha Military Landscapes — twelve forts including Raigad, Shivneri, Pratapgad, Vijaydurg, Sindhudurg and Gingee — inscribed in July 2025, and the Ancient Buddhist Site of Sarnath inscribed at the forty-eighth session in July 2026. Of the 45, 37 are cultural, 7 natural and one — Khangchendzonga National Park — mixed.

Trap watch. A corbelled arch is not a true arch: Iltutmish's tomb and the Lodi tombs use corbelling, the Alai Darwaza (1311) is the first with a true arch and finished dome, and Humayun's Tomb (1572) is the first grand charbagh tomb — the Taj (1632–53) is the idiom's peak, not its beginning. The Qutb Minar is a four-sultan sequence (Aibak's first storey, Iltutmish's three, Firoz Shah Tughlaq's top storey after the 1368 lightning strike, Sikandar Lodi's repairs), so "Iltutmish completed it" and "Firoz Shah Tughlaq finished the present top" both appear in answer keys. The Kailasa at Ellora is Rashtrakuta (Krishna I); the Kailasanatha at Kanchipuram is Pallava (Rajasimha); they are different monuments in different idioms, and both are Hindu. Ajanta is entirely Buddhist with Hinayana early caves; Ellora contains Buddhist, Hindu and Jain caves; Elephanta is Hindu only; Barabar is the earliest rock-cut site and is Ajivika, not Buddhist. Konark's wheels are sundials, the Odisha pair is a rekha deul with a pidha deul (jagamohana), and Konark is a Sun temple, not a Jagannath temple. Aihole's Durga temple is Hindu despite its name and its apsidal, chaitya-derived plan; Pattadakal is where Nagara and Dravida stand side by side. Vesara is not a "fourth style" invented later — the Hoysala star plan in soapstone is its terminal form, and Ramappa (Kakatiya, 1213) is the usual odd one out. Srirangam's tallest gopuram was completed in 1987, so a monument's most conspicuous feature may be its newest. Indo-Saracenic is neither Victorian Gothic (Bombay CST, 1888) nor Lutyens' classicism (New Delhi, 1929). The AMASR prohibited and regulated areas are 100 m and 200 m under the 2010 amendment. India has 45 World Heritage Sites as of 2026, with the Maratha Military Landscapes added in 2025 and Sarnath in 2026.
