Scriptures, Languages and Scripts — Detailed Notes

Three chains: scripts (Indus → Brahmi → derivatives), language families (Indo-Aryan/Dravidian/Austro-Asiatic/Tibeto-Burman), and the scripture corpora per faith.

1. Scripts

  • Indus: c.2600-1900; sign-count ~375-400; undeciphered; direction debated (right-to-left majority view), boustrophedon on some seals.
  • Brahmi: Ashokan 3rd c BCE, first deciphered 1837 by James Prinsep); written left-to-right; parent of Devanagari, Tamil-Brahmi, Kannada-Telugu, Bengali-Assamese, Odia, Gujarati, Sinhala, Tibetan lineages.
  • Kharosthi: NW (Gandhara), right-to-left, Aramaic-derived; dies ~3rd c CE; Gandhari Prakrit + Kharosthi manuscripts (Buddhist, found as Gandhara birch-bark scrolls).
  • Evolution mnemonic: Brahmi → Gupta script → Siddham → Devanagari (c.11th); southern: Tamil-Brahmi → Vatteluttu & Tamil.

2. Languages & literature

  • Indo-Aryan: Vedic Sanskrit → Classical (Panini's Ashtadhyayi codifies); epics (Ramayana — Valmiki; Mahabharata — Vyasa; Gita inside); Puranas (18 mahapuranas); darshanas (6 schools); Buddhist Hybrid Sanskrit (Lalitavistara); Prakrits: Magadhi (Jain Ardhamagadhi Agamas), Shauraseni, Maharashtri (Gathasaptashati, Hala), Paisachi (Brihatkatha, Gunadhya); Pali: Tipitaka (Vinaya-Sutta-Abhidhamma), Milindapanha, Dipavamsa-Mahavamsa.
  • Apabhramsa → modern IA: Prithviraj Raso (Chand Bardai), Jain narrative verse; Hindi branch: Avadhi (Ramcharitmanas — Tulsidas), Braj (Sursagar), Khari Boli (Rasos, later Urdu-Hindi).
  • Dravidian: Tamil (Tolkappiyam; Sangam anthologies: Ettuthogai + Pattuppattu; Pathinenkilkanakku (18 works) incl. Tirukkural); Telugu (Nannaya Mahabharata start), Kannada (Pampa, Ponna, Ranna — 'ratnatraya'), Malayalam (Manipravalam blend; Tunchath Ezhuthachan (Adhyatma Ramayanam)).
  • Urdu: Khari Boli + Persian script (Nastaliq); Amir Khusrau early; Deccani branch (Ibrahim Adil Shah II's Kitab-i-Nauras); ghazal-masnavi forms; Ghalib-Iqbal-Faiz line.
  • Others: Santali (Austro-Asiatic), Manipuri/Meitei (Tibeto-Burman; ancient script suppressed-revived), Bodo, Dogri, Santhali — 8th Schedule now 22 languages; classical-language tag: Tamil, Sanskrit, Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam, Odia, + Marathi/Pali-Prakrit/Assamese/Bengali (2024 round — VERIFY current list each attempt).

3. Scripture corpora (pair faith → canon)

  • Hindu: Shruti (4 Vedas + Brahmanas + Aranyakas + Upanishads) vs Smriti (epics, dharmashastras — Manusmriti, nibandhas); Vedas: Rig (hymns, 10 mandalas), Yajus (Yajur — prose ritual), Sama (melodic — rig-based), Atharva (folk-magic); each Veda has shakhas (recensions).
  • Buddhist: Tipitaka; Mahayana add: Prajnaparamita, Saddharma Pundarika; Tibetan Kangyur-Tengyur; Pali commentaries (Buddhaghosa — Visuddhimagga).
  • Jain: Agamas (Angas-Upangas; Shvetambara canon vs Digambara loss-claim); Tattvartha Sutra (Umasvati — accepted by both); Kalpasutra (Bhadrabahu).
  • Sikh: Adi Granth (Arjan, 1604) → Guru Granth Sahib (Gobind Singh, 1708); Dasam Granth separate; Janamsakhis = Nanak biographies.

MCQ hooks

  • Prinsep = 1837 Brahmi decipherment; Kharosthi = right-to-left.
  • Gathasaptashati = Hala (Maharashtri); Brihatkatha = Gunadhya (Paisachi).
  • Adi Granth 1604 by Guru Arjan; installed Harmandir; final Guru 1708.
  • Tattvartha Sutra = both Jain sects accept; Visuddhimagga = Buddhaghosa.
  • Tolkappiyam = oldest Tamil grammar; Tirukkural = Tiruvalluvar.
  • Ashtadhyayi = Panini (~500 BCE?); 'shabdanushasana' = Chandragomin (Buddhist grammar rival).

4. Brahmi's Children: How One Script Became Twenty

The single most productive fact in Indian palaeography is that almost every modern Indian script descends from Brahmi, the left-to-right script of Ashoka's 3rd-century-BCE edicts, deciphered by James Prinsep in 1837. The exceptions define the rule's edges: the undeciphered Indus signs (c. 2600–1900 BCE, some 375–400 distinct signs, predominantly right-to-left with boustrophedon instances on seals) belong to a different, probably logo-syllabic system with no demonstrated continuity into Brahmi; Kharoshthi (right-to-left, Aramaic-derived, the Gandhara administrative script that carried Gandhari Prakrit Buddhist texts on birch-bark scrolls) flourished in the northwest and died around the 3rd century CE, leaving no descendants; and the Perso-Arabic Nastaliq of Urdu arrived with an entirely separate lineage. Everything else — Devanagari, Bengali-Assamese, Odia, Gujarati, Gurmukhi, Kannada-Telugu, Malayalam, Tamil, Sinhala, Tibetan — is Brahmi's progeny, and the examination tests the branching logic, not the full tree.

The branching runs through two corridors, northern and southern, and the corridor distinction is the comparison to carry. The northern corridor runs Brahmi to Gupta script (4th–6th centuries, the Allahabad pillar's evolving letterforms) to Siddham (6th–9th centuries, the Buddhist tantric script that travelled to China and Japan with pilgrims — Siddham is still studied in Japanese Shingon monasteries) to Nagari and finally Devanagari by c. 11th century, with Bengali-Assamese, Odia, Gujarati and Gurmukhi (standardised by Guru Angad in the 16th century from the Landa–Sharda milieu) splitting off along the way. The southern corridor runs Brahmi to Tamil-Brahmi (the Madurai–Tirunelveli cave inscriptions, 3rd–1st centuries BCE, adapted for Tamil phonology by dropping aspirates) to the Pallava-Grantha and Vatteluttu systems (Grantha for Sanskrit in Tamil country, Vatteluttu for Tamil itself) to modern Tamil, Kannada-Telugu and Malayalam scripts. The causal drivers of divergence are phonological (each language reshaping letterforms for its sound inventory), material (palm-leaf writing favouring rounded southern forms that resist tearing, versus the angular northern forms suited to birch-bark and copperplate incision), and political (each court's scribal tradition freezing local variants into standards). A script, in this account, is a fossilised consensus among a language's sounds, its writing surfaces and its states.

Kharoshthi's death deserves its own causal note because it is the exception that illuminates survival. Kharoshthi died not from inferior design but from patronage collapse: it was the administrative script of Gandhara under Indo-Greeks, Shakas, Parthians and Kushans, and when Kushan power dissolved and Gupta-period Brahmi-based administration absorbed the northwest, the scribal ecosystem (training, documents, prestige) vanished within generations. The Gandhari birch-bark scrolls (1st–3rd centuries CE, among the oldest surviving Buddhist manuscripts) are therefore doubly precious: they preserve both a lost Prakrit and the script that carried it. The portable thesis is that scripts live by institutions, not by elegance — Brahmi survived because every successor state needed its scribes, Kharoshthi died because none did, and the modern script map of India is a political map frozen in letterforms.

5. Sanskrit and Its Others: Hierarchies, Revolts, and the Classical List

Indian language history is a hierarchy repeatedly built and repeatedly breached, and the hierarchy's ladder — Sanskrit at the apex, Prakrits in the middle, Apabhramsha and the deshi vernaculars at the base — organises two millennia of literary politics. Vedic Sanskrit (the Rigveda's ten mandalas, c. 1500–1200 BCE for the early books) was already archaic when Panini's Ashtadhyayi (c. 5th–4th century BCE, some 4,000 sutras of generative grammar — \"shabdanushasana,\" the science of correct speech) froze Classical Sanskrit into permanent stability; that freezing is precisely what made Sanskrit eternal (samskrita, \"refined\") and precisely what detached it from speech, opening the space below for Prakrit literature. The Buddhist and Jain canons weaponised that space: the Buddha's reported injunction to teach in the people's own language legitimised Pali (the Tipitaka — Vinaya, Sutta, Abhidhamma — plus the Milindapanha's Indo-Greek dialogue and the Dipavamsa–Mahavamsa chronicles) and the Ardhamagadhi Jain Agamas, while Ashoka's Prakrit edicts gave the middle register imperial prestige. Courtly Prakrits then flowered as literary languages in their own right: Shauraseni (drama's prose, Mathura region), Maharashtri (lyric's vehicle — Hala's Gathasaptashati, c. 1st century CE, 700 erotic verses), Magadhi (low characters in Sanskrit drama, a sociolinguistic fossil of prestige ranking), and Paisachi (Gunadhya's lost Brihatkatha, the \"great story\" that seeded the Kathasaritsagara and much of India's narrative DNA).

The ladder's next two rungs complete the descent into modernity. Apabhramsha (c. 6th–13th centuries, \"fallen away\" speech) is the bridge register — Jain monks' narrative verse, Chand Bardai's Prithviraj Raso in its Pingala form — from which the modern Indo-Aryan languages crystallise: Avadhi (Tulsidas's Ramcharitmanas, c. 1574), Braj (Surdas's Sursagar), Khari Boli (the Delhi-base that splits into Hindi and Urdu). Urdu is the ladder's most instructive breach: Khari Boli grammar in Perso-Arabic Nastaliq dress, born in the Deccan as Dakhani (Ibrahim Adil Shah II's Kitab-i Nauras, the Bijapur court anthology) and Amir Khusrau's early Hindavi experiments, then matured in Mughal Delhi and Lucknow through the ghazal–masnavi forms into the Ghalib–Iqbal–Faiz lineage. The Dravidian story runs parallel but independent: Tamil (Tolkappiyam grammar; Sangam anthologies — the Ettuthogai eight and Pattuppattu ten — plus the eighteen Pathinenkilkanakku including Tiruvalluvar's Tirukkural), Kannada (ratnatraya Pampa–Ponna–Ranna, 10th century), Telugu (Nannaya's Mahabharata, 11th century), Malayalam (the Manipravalam Sanskrit–Tamil blend, then Tunchath Ezhuthachan's Adhyatma Ramayanam). The examination's standing demand — pair language with text with author — is satisfiable only if the ladder is learned as a causal sequence rather than a list.

The classical-language regime (criteria framed 2004, revised by the Linguistic Experts Committee under Sahitya Akademi) now recognises eleven languages after the Union Cabinet's 3 October 2024 conferral on Marathi, Pali, Prakrit, Assamese and Bengali — and the regime's logic is the examination point: antiquity (1,500–2,000 years of recorded history), a body of ancient valued literature, and discontinuity or distinctness from later offshoots. The Eighth Schedule (22 languages, from 14 at commencement — Sindhi 1967; Konkani, Manipuri, Nepali 1992; Bodo, Dogri, Maithili, Santali 2004) is the separate constitutional list for official-language purposes, and conflating the two lists is the standard trap: Santali (Austro-Asiatic–Munda) and Bodo (Tibeto-Burman) are Scheduled but not classical; Pali and Prakrit are classical but not Scheduled. The political economy underneath is that classical status funds research institutions, university chairs and international awards — it converts antiquity into budget lines, which is why the demands (and the LEC's gatekeeping) are perennial news.

6. Canons Compared: How Four Religions Fixed Their Texts

Every scriptural canon answers the same institutional problem — which words bind the community, and who decides — and the four Indian answers differ so systematically that comparison is the only efficient way to learn them. The Hindu solution is the shruti–smriti hierarchy: shruti (\"heard,\" authorless revelation — the four Vedas with their Brahmanas, Aranyakas and Upanishads) outranks smriti (\"remembered,\" human-composed tradition — the epics, the Dharmashastras from Manu onward, the Puranas, the nibandha digests). Fixity here is phonetic rather than editorial: Vedic shakhas (recensions) preserved the Rigveda's 10 mandalas syllable-perfect through oral patha techniques (notably ghana-patha) across three millennia without a central council ever voting on the text. The Buddhist solution is conciliar: the Tipitaka (Vinaya–Sutta–Abhidhamma) claims the authority of communal recitation (sangiti) from Rajagriha (1st council, Ajatashatru's reign) through Vaishali (2nd, c. 383 BCE, Kalashoka) to Pataliputra (3rd, Ashokan) and the Theravada writing-down in Sri Lanka (1st century BCE) — with Mahayana sutras (Prajnaparamita, Saddharma-Pundarika) and the Tibetan Kangyur–Tengyur canon (translations plus commentaries) as later strata whose authority rests on claimed Buddha-word rather than council vote. Buddhaghosa's Visuddhimagga (5th century CE, Anuradhapura) then systematised the Pali commentarial tradition into Theravada orthodoxy.

The Jain solution is the schism case: the Shvetambara canon (45 Agamas — 11 Angas, 12 Upangas and ancillary texts — fixed at the Valabhi council, 5th century CE, under Devardhigani) is rejected by the Digambaras, who hold the original teachings were lost — and the bridge text both accept is Umasvati's Tattvartha Sutra (c. 2nd–5th century CE), with Bhadrabahu's Kalpasutra supplying the Shvetambara monastic and biographical discipline. The Sikh solution is the most institutionally decisive: Guru Arjan's compilation of the Adi Granth (1604, installed at Harmandir Sahib) gathered the Gurus' bani with Bhagat compositions (Kabir, Namdev, Ravidas, Farid — the inter-faith signature of the canon), and Guru Gobind Singh's 1708 conferral of Guruship on the text itself (Guru Granth Sahib, adding Guru Tegh Bahadur's hymns) closed the canon by making the book the living Guru — the Dasam Granth remaining a separate, attributed-but-disputed collection, and the Janamsakhis serving as Nanak's narrative biographies outside the scriptural core. The comparative payoff is a four-cell logic: Hindus fix by recitation discipline, Buddhists by council and translation, Jains by sectarian canonisation, Sikhs by Guru-conferred closure — and each mechanism explains its tradition's characteristic dispute (shakha variation, Mahayana authenticity, Agama loss, Dasam Granth authorship).

7. Literature as History: What Texts Prove and What They Don't

The final section converts literary knowledge into historical method, because UPSC increasingly tests not \"who wrote what\" but what a text can evidence. Court literature (Kalidasa, Banabhadra's Harshacharita, Bilhana's Vikramankadevacharita, Kalhana's Rajatarangini — the 12th-century Kashmiri chronicle that is the closest Sanskrit comes to historiography) evidences royal self-representation: titulature, claimed conquests, court aesthetics — reliable for ideology, unreliable for events without corroboration. The epics evidence social memory in strata: the Mahabharata's core Bharata battle (c. 900 BCE by the most cited archaeological-linguistic correlation) wrapped in centuries of didactic accretion (the Gita, the Shanti Parvan's statecraft), the Ramayana's Kosala–Lanka geography encoding eastern expansion — usable for reconstructing values and regions, treacherous for dating kings. Sangam literature evidences chiefdom society (the Chera–Chola–Pandya ventar, tinai ecology, Roman-trade references corroborated by Arikamedu rouletted ware and coin hoards) but its anthologies were compiled centuries after composition, so individual poems date insecurely. Buddhist Hybrid Sanskrit texts (Ashvaghosha's Buddhacharita, the Lalitavistara) and Pali chronicles evidence sectarian institutional memory — monastery foundings, council narratives, patronage claims — precise on doctrine, partisan on rivals.

Three methodological rules organise all of this for answers. First, genre governs reliability: inscriptions (contemporary, public, formulaic) outrank chronicles, which outrank epics, which outrank Puranic king-lists — the dating of Kanishka (c. 78 CE, Saka era) rests on epigraphy and numismatics, not on the texts that mention him. Second, corroboration is the method: Megasthenes' Pataliputra gains credibility from the Bulandi Bagh wooden-palisade excavations; Sangam Rome-trade from Arikamedu; the Buddha's dates from the Ashokan synchronisms with Hellenistic kings (Antiochus II, Ptolemy II, Antigonus Gonatas, Magas, Alexander of Epirus in Rock Edict XIII) — each text–artefact pair is a potential MCQ. Third, absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, but patterned absence is: the Harappan script's undecipherment constrains everything said about its society, while the total absence of Harappan horse-and-chariot vocabulary against its Vedic centrality is itself the datum that structures the Aryan-debate questions. An answer that names the text, states its genre, and declares what it can and cannot prove has demonstrated historical thinking; an answer that merely cites the text as authority has demonstrated only memory.
