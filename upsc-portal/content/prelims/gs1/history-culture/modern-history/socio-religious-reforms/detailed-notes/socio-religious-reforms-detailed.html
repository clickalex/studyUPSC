Socio-Religious Reform Movements — Detailed Notes

19th-early 20th century awakening: reformer → organisation → year → focus. Prelims staple.

1. Bengal & the Brahmo line

  • Rammohan Roy: Atmiya Sabha 1815, Brahmo Sabha 1828 (→ Brahmo Samaj), Sati pamphlets ('Sahamaran' debate vs Dharma Sabha and Srinath Roy), sati abolition 1829 (Reg XVII, Bentinck); Persian-style works: Tuhfat-ul-Muwahhidin; 'Precepts of Jesus'; Sambad Kaumudi paper.
  • Debendranath Tagore (Tattvabodhini Sabha 1839; Brahmo covenant 1843); Keshab Chandra Sen (split 1866 — Brahmo Samaj of India; Special Marriage Act III 1872 lineage) vs Adi Brahmo (Devendranath); Henry Derozio — Young Bengal (Academic Association); Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar (widow remarriage champion — Act XV 1856; Bengali primer; Metropolitan institution).

2. Western & southern reform

  • Prarthana Samaj 1867 (Atmaram Pandurang, Bombay); M.G. Ranade (widows'-remarriage push; Indian National Conference 1883; economic-decentralisation scholarship), R.G. Bhandarkar; Gopal Krishna Gokhale — Servants of India Society 1905.
  • Jyotirao Phule: Satyashodhak Samaj 1873 (Gulamgiri 1873; Savitribai girls' school Pune 1848); Sri Narayana Guru (Aruvippuram 1888 installation; SNDP Yogam 1903 (Dr. Palpu's backing)), Vaikom satyagraha 1924-25 support; Periyar E.V. Ramasamy: Vaikom hero, Self-Respect Movement 1925, Dravidar Kazhagam 1944; Ayyankali (Villuvandi yatra, Sadhujana Paripalana 1907).
  • Dr. B.R. Ambedkar: Bahishkrit Hitakarini Sabha 1924, Mahad 1927, Kalaram temple 1930, Annihilation of Caste 1936; 'Educate-Agitate-Organise'.

3. Revivalist & Muslim-Sikh reform

  • Swami Dayananda Saraswati — Arya Samaj 1875 Bombay (Satyarth Prakash; 'back to the Vedas'; Shuddhi reconversion; DAV schools 1886 Lahore; debates idolatry-caste-by-birth); Vivekananda: Chicago 1893; Ramakrishna Mission 1897 (guru Ramakrishna Paramahamsa; 'Daridra Narayana' service); Theosophical Society — Adyar shift 1882 (Olcott-Besant; Besant Central Hindu College BHU seed 1898 + Home Rule 1916).
  • Sir Syed Ahmed Khan: Scientific Society 1864 Aligarh; MAO College 1875 (→ Aligarh Muslim Univ 1920); Tahzeeb-ul-Akhlaq 1870; two-nation seed vs later nationalists; Deoband school 1866 (Muhammad Qasim Nanautavi — traditionalist opposite pole); Singh Sabha 1873 Amritsar (Khalsa College 1892; Tat Khalsa purge); Ahmadiya 1889 Qadian (Mirza Ghulam Ahmad) — contested minority stream; Parsi: Rahnumai Mazdayasnan Sabha 1851 (Naoroji line).

4. Social-legislation anchors

  • Sati 1829; Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act 1856 (Vidyasagar push); Female Infanticide Act 1870; Age of Consent Act 1891 (10→12; Rukhmabai-Phulmoni case); Sarda Act 1929 (marriage 14/18 — child marriage restraint); Special Marriage Act III 1872 (Brahmo); Devadasi system bans (Madras 1947).

MCQ hooks

  • Founder-year table: Brahmo 1828, Prarthana 1867, Arya 1875, Theosophy 1875 (NY)/Adyar 1882, Ramakrishna Mission 1897, Servants of India 1905, SNDP 1903, Self-Respect 1925.
  • Sati = Rammohan + Bentinck 1829 (Reg XVII).
  • Widow Remarriage 1856 = Vidyasagar; primer = 'Barnaparichay' book.
  • Aligarh = Sir Syed (MAO 1875); Deoband 1866 = traditionalist counter.
  • Phule's Gulamgiri 1873; Savitribai school 1848.
  • Age of Consent 1891 raised 10→12 (Sarda 1929 = 14/18).

8. The Bengal laboratory — Roy to Vivekananda, one continuous argument

The trunk lists Bengal's reformers chronologically; read them instead as one seventy-year argument about what "reform" means. Raja Rammohan Roy (1772–1833) opens it: Atmiya Sabha (1815) → Brahmo Sabha (1828), monotheism from the Upanishads, reason against sati (Regulation XVII, 1829, under Bentinck), polygamy, caste and idolatry — Persian-to-Greek learning deployed to prove Indians could modernise themselves. Title "Raja" from Akbar II; death at Bristol (1833); Samvad Kaumudi (1821) inventing the Bengali reform press. His successors split the inheritance three ways. Debendranath Tagore (Tattvabodhini Sabha; Adi Brahmo Samaj 1839) kept reform inside monotheistic devotion. Keshab Chandra Sen (joined 1857) organised it into a church-like Brahmo Samaj of India (1866 split), then fractured it again (Sadharan Brahmo Samaj, 1878) over his daughter's child marriage at 13 — the reformer caught by his own reform. Meanwhile Derozio's Young Bengal (Hindu College, late 1820s–1831; Bengal Gazette) rejected the premise: French-Revolution rationalism that attacked orthodoxy and Company authoritarianism alike, with students like Dakhinaranjan Mukherjee and Rasik Krishna Mallick.

Vivekananda (1863–1902) closes the argument by inverting it. Where Roy used reason to certify tradition, Vivekananda used spiritual confidence to certify the nation: Ramakrishna's disciple, Chicago (11 September 1893, "Sisters and brothers of America"), Ramakrishna Mission (1897) — service of humanity as service to God, practical Vedanta in schools and relief work. The trunk's significance line is exactly right — extremist leaders and Bengal secret societies cited him — but add the mechanism: Vivekananda converted reform's defensive posture ("prove we are civilised") into an offensive one ("we civilise the world"). Mains synthesis: Bengal moved from Roy's apologetic modernity through Keshab's schismatic organisation and Derozio's root-and-branch rationalism to Vivekananda's confident universalism — and each stage's press, schools and sabhas became Congress's ready-made infrastructure in 1885.

9. The western and southern axes — Phule, Ranade, and the two theories of oppression

If Bengal debated how to reform, western India debated who the oppressor is — and produced the two theories that still organise Indian social thought. Theory one — custom as oppressor (Ranade's Prarthana Samaj, 1867). Dadoba and Atmaram Pandurang's theistic reform, joined by M. G. Ranade and R. G. Bhandarkar: widow remarriage, caste softening, women's education — but keeping idol and sacrament, Brahmo influence without Brahmo rupture. Ranade's significance (boycotting the 1893 Industrial Conference linkage, Congress overlap) is the liberal premise: society reforms itself by persuasion, and political freedom follows social freedom. Theory two — Brahminism as oppressor (Phule's Satyashodhak Samaj, 1873). Jyotiba Phule (1827–90) with Savitribai: schools for girls and shudra-atishudra children, Gulamgiri (1873) analogising caste to American slavery, Brahmin supremacy named as conspiracy. The trunk's line is the crux — first movement to fuse caste and gender into a single system of oppression.

Set these against the southern and Parsi mirrors. Kandukuri Veeresalingam carried widow-remarriage and anti-purdah reform into Telugu country; Pandita Ramabai (Sharda Sadan 1889, Arya Mahila Samaj) and Savitribai Phule (1831–97; first woman teacher; Balhatya Pratibandhak Griha against infanticide) built women's institutions, not just arguments; Begum Rokeya (Sultana's Dream 1905; Anjuman-e-Khawatin-e-Islam) wrote Muslim feminism into being; the Rahnumai Mazdayasnan Sabha (1851) — Naoroji, Furdunji, Bengali, Rast Goftar — proves even tiny communities ran the same reform script. The Mains payoff: when asked "compare reform movements," do not list — triangulate: Ranade (reform custom, keep structure), Phule (attack structure, centre the oppressed), and the revivalists below (defend structure, attack the foreigner).

10. Revivalism and its afterlife — Arya Samaj, Deoband, and the legislative ledger

The trunk's revivalist set — Arya Samaj (1875, "Back to the Vedas," Satyarth Prakash, shuddhi, DAV schools 1886 Lahore), Deoband (1867, Nanautavi–Gangohi, Dar-ul-Uloom purism, anti-British/anti-Congress/anti-Aligarh), Wahabi militancy (Shah Waliullah's 1703–1762 call → Sayyid Ahmad Barelvi's Dar al-Harb jihad, Balakot 1831; Titu Mir at Narkelberia-Barasat 1831), Singh Sabha (1870s, anti-Hindu-accretion, Punjabi-Gurmukhi) → Akali movement (1920–25, Sikh Gurdwaras Act 1925), plus Theosophy's paradoxical Western validation (Blavatsky–Olcott 1875/1882 Adyar; Besant 1893, Home Rule 1916) and Aligarh's loyalist modernism (Scientific Society 1864, MAO College 1875 → AMU 1920, Tahzib-ul-Akhlaq) — must be read for afterlife, not just origin. Arya shuddhi–sangathan hardens into 1920s communal organisation; Deoband's clerics staff the Khilafat (1919–24) and Jamiat Ulema-e-Hind; Aligarh's graduates staff the Muslim League; Sikh reform births the Akali Dal. The examination line: revivalism began as religious self-defence and ended as political constituency-building — the road from sabha to party runs through every one of these movements.

Close with the legislative ledger the trunk tabulates — the years are Prelims gold and the pattern is Mains gold: Sati XVII (1829, Bentinck) → Widow Remarriage (1856, Vidyasagar) → Special Marriages III (1872, Brahma civil marriage, 14/18) → Age of Consent (1891, 12 years, post-Phulmoni 1889, Malabari's campaign) → Sarda (1929, 14/18). The pattern: each statute follows a reformer's campaign plus a scandal or death (sati debates, Phulmoni) — legislation ratifies what agitation has already won. Gopal Ganesh Agarkar (Sudharak, Deccan Education Society) supplies the coda: the rationalist who criticised even the reformers' orthodoxy, proving the public sphere had matured enough to argue with itself. Final synthesis (extending trunk §5): reform created the press, schools, and vocabularies of justice; revival created organised religious publics; together they handed Congress (1885) both its audience and its agenda.
