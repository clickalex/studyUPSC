East India Company and Expansion — Detailed Notes

Traders → rulers (1600–1858). Anchors: battles, Governor-Generals + reforms, land systems, subsidiary diplomacy.

1. Entry & early trade

  • EIC chartered 1600 (Elizabeth I); first factory Machilipatnam 1611; Surat factory after the Battle of Swally 1612 (vs Portuguese); Jahangir farman via Thomas Roe 1615-18; 1717 Farrukhsiyar farman = 'magna carta' duty-free Bengal trade (dastaks abused); Bombay ceded 1668 (Catherine dowry → Company lease).
  • Rivals: Portuguese (decline post-Swally), Dutch (Batavia focus), Danes, French Compagnie 1664; Dupleix — Carnatic wars 1746-63 (3 wars): Madras 1746, Trichinopoly-Severndroog arc, Wandiwash 1760 (Eyre Coote vs Lally) → Pondicherry ceded militarily-ended.

2. Conquest arc

  • Plassey 1757 (Clive; Siraj-ud-Daula; Mir Jafar conspiracy; Jagat Seth bankers; Omichund bribe plot), Buxar 1764 (Munro; Mir Qasim + Shuja-ud-Daula + Shah Alam II), Diwani 1765 (Treaty of Allahabad) → dual government (Clive) → Warren Hastings ends it 1772; Regulating Act 1773 + Pitt's India Act 1784 (Board of Control dual frame).
  • Mysore: 4 wars (Haidar Ali; Tipu 'Tiger'; Seringapatam 1799 death); Marathas: 3 wars (1775-82 Salbai; 1803-05 Assaye; 1817-18 final); Sikh wars 1845-46 (Sobraon), 1848-49 (Chilianwala, Multan, Gujarat; Dalhousie annexes Punjab 1849 — Koh-i-noor to Queen); Burma wars 1824-26, 1852, 1885 (later); Nepal 1814-16 (Sugauli); Sind 1843 (Napier 'Peccavi' legend).
  • Diplomatic frame: subsidiary alliance (Wellesley 1798+) — Hyderabad, Awadh, Tanjore, Peshwa (Bassein 1802); Doctrine of Lapse (Dalhousie 1848-56) — Satara 1848, Jaitpur-Sambalpur, Jhansi 1853, Nagpur 1854; Awadh 'misgovernment' annexation 1856.

4. Land revenue systems

  • Permanent Settlement 1793 (Cornwallis-John Shore; Bengal-Bihar-Odisha; zamindari; sunset law) — fixed demand, de-facto property; Ryotwari (Munro-Read; Madras-Bombay) — direct ryot assessment; Mahalwari (1822+; Holt Mackenzie, amended 1833 Bird; North-West, Punjab parts, Awadh later taluqdari) — village-mahal joint.

MCQ hooks

  • Wandiwash 1760 = French Indian defeat (Carnatic war III).

  • Sati 1829 (Bentinck-Rammohan), Lapse = Dalhousie (Jhansi 1853, Nagpur 1854, Awadh 1856).

13. The revenue machine — Permanent, Ryotwari, Mahalwari, and the drain

The trunk's revenue table (§7) is the most-marked page of this lesson; the Mains upgrade is to argue each system as a theory of colonial extraction, not a fact-row. Permanent Settlement (1793, Cornwallis on Shore's plan): fixed forever at ~1/11 of produce with hereditary zamindars in Bengal–Bihar–Orissa. The theory — a loyal landed gentry improving agriculture like English landlords. The verdict — zamindars became absentee rent-extractors, ryots became tenants-at-will under layered sub-infeudation, and the state permanently forfeited revenue growth from India's richest province; the sunset clause (estates auctioned for arrears) created a speculative land market. Ryotwari (1820s, Munro–Read, Madras–Bombay–Assam): settlement with the individual ryot at ~1/3–1/2, periodically revised. The theory — eliminate intermediaries, deal with the cultivator. The verdict — the state became the direct zamindar, revisions ratcheted demand upward on pessimistic assessments, and peasant arrears fed the sahukar; individual titles made land saleable, accelerating alienation. Mahalwari (1822, Holt Mackenzie, NWP–Punjab): joint village (mahal) responsibility at ~2/3, periodically revised. The theory — harness the village community. The verdict — joint liability punished the solvent for the insolvent, and the crushing ~2/3 demand converted the village brotherhood into a collective debt trap.

The common mechanism the trunk states — high demand → sahukar indebtedness → commodified land → absentee landlordism → broken village self-sufficiency — is the engine of the Drain of Wealth (Naoroji, then R. C. Dutt): revenue extracted in rupees bought sterling obligations (Home Charges, dividends, pensions) and the Company's "investment" (Bengal revenue financing EIC trade after Plassey solved the bullion problem — the coup's economic meaning from §2.3). Add the famine link: the Dual Government's (1765–72) power-without-responsibility — Reza Khan's nizamat shell over Company revenue hunger — culminated in the 1770 famine (~one-third of Bengal dead), the exhibit that ended dual rule under Hastings (1772). Mains synthesis: the revenue systems differed in whom they ruined first (ryot under ryotwari, community under mahalwari, future revenue under Permanent) but converged on the same political economy — maximum extraction with minimum obligation.

14. Collaboration and resistance — why 1857 was a culmination

The trunk's success-factors (§10) and resistance-list (§11) answer two halves of one question; fuse them. Why Indians collaborated: bankers (Jagat Seths financed Plassey), merchants profiting from Company trade, successor-state elites using the EIC against rivals (Maratha houses against each other at Bassein 1802; Nizam against Mysore), the sepoy army's regular pay and discipline (the coercive tool that later sparked 1857), and princely acceptance of subsidiary protection (Hyderabad 1798, Awadh 1801). The structural point from §10: naval supremacy (Royal Navy over French, Wandiwash 1760 → Paris 1763), Bengal-financed armies after 1765, Company autonomy (vs the dissolved 1769 French Compagnie), and the Subsidiary–Resident constitutional framework made collaboration rational for each actor even as it proved ruinous collectively — the classic collective-action trap. Why resistance kept erupting: the trunk's pre-1857 ledger — Sanyasi–Fakir (1770s+), Chuar (1766–1816), Vellore (1806) and Barrackpore (1824) mutinies, Paika (1817, Bakshi Jagabandhu), Bhil (1818–48), Kol (1831–32), Wahabi–Titumir militancy, Santhal Hul (1855–56, Sidhu–Kanhu, → Santhal Pargana).

The culmination thesis ties every grievance stream into 1857: Doctrine of Lapse annexations (Satara 1848 → Nagpur 1854; Awadh 1856 on "misgovernment," not lapse — the Prelims trap) alienated princes; the denied Bithur pension (Nana Sahib) carried Maratha memory into revolt; revenue ruin alienated peasants; the sepoy army's own grievances (pay, overseas service, cartridges) turned the coercive tool around; and Annexation-plus-reform (Sati 1829, Widow Remarriage 1856, Dalhousie's railways/telegraph/PWD modernisation) convinced the orthodox that Company rule meant civilisational erasure. The examination line: 1857 was not caused by the cartridge — the cartridge merely synchronised a century of discrete resistances (tribal, peasant, princely, sepoy) into a single military moment; read §11 backwards from 1857 and every entry becomes a rehearsal.

15. Governors-General as policy eras — and the answer method

The trunk's one-liners (§8) name the proconsuls; arrange them as policy eras for "trace the evolution" questions. Clive (1757–60, 1765–67): the coup era — Plassey conspiracy (Mir Jafar, Jagat Seths, Omichand, Rai Durlabh), Allahabad Diwani (1765, Shah Alam II), Dual Government. Hastings (1772–85, first GG of Bengal): the state-building era — ended dual rule, Rohilla/Afghan wars, impeached by Burke (the metropolitan conscience trial). Cornwallis (1786–93): the settlement era — Permanent Settlement, "Father of Civil Services," Seringapatam 1792. Wellesley (1798–1805): the expansion era — Subsidiary Alliance systematised, Fourth Mysore War (Tipu falls 4 May 1799), Second Maratha War (Assaye/Argaon/Laswari 1803). Hastings/Moira (1813–23): the paramountcy era — Third Maratha War, Peshwaship abolished 1818. Bentinck (1828–35, first GG of India): the reform era — Sati 1829, Macaulay's Minute 1835. Dalhousie (1848–56): the modernising-annexation era — Lapse, Punjab (29 March 1849, Koh-i-Noor to Victoria), railways (Bombay–Thane 1853), telegraph, PWD, Widow Remarriage 1856 — "Maker of Modern India" and maker of 1857's grievance bundle alike.

The answer method: (1) for "rise of the Company" questions, sequence Bengal (Plassey coup → Buxar legitimacy: Buxar > Plassey because it destroyed the Bengal–Awadh–Mughal combined arms under Munro against Mir Qasim + Shuja-ud-Daula + Shah Alam II, 23 October 1764) → Mysore (four wars 1767–99, Madras 1769 → Mangalore 1784, the last equal treaty → Seringapatam 1792 → 1799) → Marathas (Salbai 1782 → Bassein 1802 → 1818) → Sikhs (Lahore 1846 → Gujarat "Battle of Guns" 21 Feb 1849) → Lapse/misgovernment mop-up; (2) for "British vs French" questions, deploy §10's six factors with Wandiwash/Paris and Company-autonomy specifics; (3) for Tipu/Hyder/Ranjit Singh personality questions, use the moderniser evidence (Mysore rockets → Congreve; Tree of Liberty + Jacobin Club + Padshah styling; Amritsar 1809 → Khalsa army) and always close with the structural moral: individual genius lost to institutional systems — the EIC won because it was a system (revenue + sepoys + Residents + naval supply) fighting persons, however brilliant.
