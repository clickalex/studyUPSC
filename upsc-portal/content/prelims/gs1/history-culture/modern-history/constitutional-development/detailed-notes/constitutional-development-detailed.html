British Constitutional Development — Detailed Notes

Charter acts → Crown rule → gradual Indianisation: each act's one-line signature.

1. Company-era framework (1773-1858)

  • Regulating Act 1773: first written intervention — Governor-General (Warren Hastings) + 4-member council, Supreme Court Calcutta 1774 (Impey), Bombay-Madras subordination partial, parliamentary oversight seeds; Amending Act 1781 (clarifies SC vs council — Act of Settlement).
  • Pitt's India Act 1784: dual government — Board of Control (political) + Court of Directors (commercial) frame; 'British possessions in India' first constitutional phrase.
  • Charter Acts: 1793 (routine renewal; salaries from India), 1813 (trade monopoly ends except tea-China; ₹1 lakh education-religion; missionaries open), 1833 (Company becomes purely administrative trustee; GG-of-India title — Bentinck first; law member Macaulay; central legislative centralisation), 1853 (last charter; patronage ends → open competition for the civil services; separate legislative council 12 members; law commission).
  • GoI Act 1858: post-1857 — Company abolished; Secretary of State + 15-member Council of India; Viceroy (Canning first);Proclamation Nov 1858.

2. Crown-era gradualism (1861-1919)

  • Indian Councils Act 1861: Indians nominated (Raja of Benares etc.), portfolio system (Canning), ordinances, restored Bombay-Madras legislation; decentralisation start.
  • 1876 Royal Titles Act (Victoria 'Empress of India' — 1877 durbar); Indian Councils Act 1892: indirect election hint (recommendation channels), budget discussion rights, questions allowed.
  • Morley-Minto 1909: separate electorates (first statutory), legislative council expansion, first Indian in Viceroy's executive council (Satyendra Sinha).
  • Montagu-Chelmsford 1919 (GoI Act): dyarchy in provinces (transferred vs reserved), bicameral centre (Council of State + Legislative Assembly), direct elections widened, public-service commission promise (Lee 1924), statutory commission promise.

3. Endgame acts (1928-1947)

  • Simon Commission 1927 (two years early; all-white; Nehru report answer 1928; Jinnah's 14 points 1929); Nehru Report: dominion status, FRs, joint electorates; Calcutta session Dec 1928 → Purna Swaraj (Lahore Dec 1929, 26 Jan 1930 first celebration).
  • GoI Act 1935: provincial autonomy (from Apr 1937), proposed all-India federation (states never joined → dead), dyarchy at centre instead, RBI 1935, Federal Court 1937, Burma-Aden separated, Sindh-Orissa created, franchise ~14%.
  • August Offer 1940 (constituent assembly of Indians hinted — reject), Cripps 1942 (post-war dominion + CA + provincial opt-out — rejected), Wavell/Shimla 1945, Cabinet Mission 1946 (CA constituted: 389 seats; Nov-Dec 1946 first sitting — Sachchidananda Sinha temp chair, Rajendra Prasad president 11 Dec 1946; Objectives Resolution 13 Dec 1946 — Nehru), Independence Act 1947 (dominions 15 Aug; CA sovereign; GG title restored; lapsed Crown paramountcy).

MCQ hooks

  • 1773 = first GG + SC Calcutta; 1781 = Act of Settlement; 1784 = Board of Control.
  • 1813 monopoly ends (tea kept); 1833 pure-admin + GG-of-India; 1853 open competition + separate legislature.
  • 1858 pair: Secretary of State + Viceroy (Canning).
  • 1909 Sinha = first Indian in Viceroy's exec council; 1919 dyarchy = provinces only.
  • 1935 quartet: provincial autonomy, RBI, Federal Court, federation dead-letter.
  • CA chain: Cabinet Mission → first sitting 9 Dec 1946 → Objectives Res 13 Dec → Rajendra Prasad 11 Dec 1946.

10. The three through-lines — centralisation, representation, and communalism

The trunk's twelve turning points (1773–1947) read as a chronicle; the Mains upgrade is to read them as three pendulums swinging simultaneously. Pendulum one — centralisation vs devolution. The Regulating Act (1773) subordinated Bombay/Madras to Bengal only in war; Pitt's (1784) dual control centralised London oversight; the 1833 Charter made Bentinck the first Governor-General of India with all-India legislative power (centralisation's peak); 1861 restored legislative powers to Bombay/Madras; 1919 separated provincial budgets and devolved transferred subjects; 1935 granted provincial autonomy while reserving the centre — and 1950 froze the settlement as the Seventh Schedule with residuary powers at the Union. The line: every devolutionary Act was a confession that the previous centralisation had become ungovernable. Pendulum two — representation without power. 1861 nominated Indians (Patiala, Banaras 1862); 1892 smuggled in indirect election and budget debate (Naoroji–Gokhale–Mehta's platform); 1909 enlarged councils but kept an official majority at the centre; 1919 gave elected majorities and dyarchy with finance reserved; 1935 gave provincial responsibility hedged by 13 safeguards — Nehru's "new charter of slavery." The system's formula never changed, only its camouflage: concede the form of legislatures, retain the substance of the executive.

Pendulum three — from united electorate to communal fragmentation. 1909's separate Muslim electorates (Simla Deputation 1906, the "mother of separate electorates") → 1919's extension (Sikhs in Punjab and others) → Communal Award (1932) → the partition logic of 1946–47. The Mains point is structural, not moral: separate electorates made religious identity the currency of representation, so politicians invested in religious identity — the electorate manufactured the communalism it claimed to reflect. Pair this with the August Declaration (20 August 1917 — "progressive realisation of responsible government") to show the contradiction at the heart of late-colonial constitutionalism: responsibility promised to a polity the franchise was simultaneously fragmenting.

11. The 1935-to-1950 ledger — what was borrowed and what was refused

The trunk lists the 1935 inheritances (~250 provisions: three lists → Seventh Schedule; Governor's office and discretionary pattern; Emergency outline → Arts 352–360; PSCs → Arts 315–323; Federal Court 1937 → Supreme Court Arts 124–147; Union–State administrative relations → Arts 256–263) and names the irony — Congress ministries had governed under provincial autonomy in 1937–39, so the blueprint was familiar. The Mains task is to argue the ledger both ways. The borrowing case: a partitioned subcontinent needed a working state on 15 August 1947, and the 1935 Act — which continued as each dominion's operative constitution under the Independence Act (royal assent 18 July 1947) — was the only tested machinery; federalism, an all-India judiciary, and professional bureaucracy could not be invented between 1946 and 1950. The refusal case is equally constitutional: universal adult franchise replaced the one-sixth franchise; fundamental rights replaced safeguards against Indians with rights of Indians; responsible government at the centre replaced dyarchy-that-never-was (defence/external/ecclesiastical reserved, federation stillborn as princes never met accession thresholds); and separate electorates were buried (the Assembly's communal decision being reservation's time-bound, joint-electorate opposite).

Two 1935 features deserve argued treatment because PYQs revisit them. Provincial autonomy (acted 1937): Congress ministries proved Indians could govern — agrarian relief, education expansion, civil-liberties releases — yet resigned in 1939 over the war issue, teaching the Assembly both the viability and the fragility of responsible government under a paramount executive; hence 1950's insistence on a fully responsible Union executive. The safeguards regime (Governor-General's and Governors' special responsibilities): the Assembly kept the instruments (emergency powers, Governor's discretion) while inverting their accountability — from an appointed Viceroy answerable to Whitehall to elected executives answerable to legislatures. Final ledger line: 1950 borrowed 1935's skeleton and transplanted a democratic heart — the continuities are administrative, the ruptures are political, and confusing the two is the standard wrong answer.

12. Answer frames — compare, explain, and judge

Three master-frames cover nearly every question this lesson generates. Frame A — compare 1909/1919/1935 on four axes: (i) franchise (narrow property → ~one-third → ~one-sixth of adults, note the 1935 contraction relative to rhetoric); (ii) legislative power (discuss-but-not-control → elected Assembly majorities with certified legislation → provincial responsibility with safeguards); (iii) executive accountability (unaccountable → dyarchy's responsibility-without-finance → autonomy-with-override); (iv) communal architecture (Muslim separate electorates → extended → Award-entrenched). The verdict sentence: 1909 communalised representation, 1919 provincialised it, 1935 federalised it — each Act solved the previous Act's crisis by enlarging the arena of Indian participation while fortifying the citadel of British power. Frame B — why dyarchy failed (1919 provinces; 1935 centre stillborn): transferred ministers held education/agriculture/health while finance stayed reserved — responsibility without resources; Governors wielded casting votes, certification, and special responsibilities; Rowlatt–Jallianwala–Non-Cooperation destroyed the political oxygen before the machinery could prove itself.

Frame C — the final-acts judgment (1946–47). The Cabinet Mission Plan (June 1946) rejected both sovereign Pakistan and unitary India for a three-tier Union (foreign affairs/defence/communications common) with a Constituent Assembly elected by provincial legislatures; its breakdown → Direct Action Day (16 August 1946) → Interim Government (September 1946) → the Independence Act's two dominions, lapsed paramountcy (princes technically sovereign till accession), Mountbatten/Jinnah as Governors-General, and abolition of the Secretary of State and Council of India. The judgment line: the Mission failed not on constitutional design but on the trust deficit separate electorates had compounded since 1909 — by 1946 no procedural ingenuity could substitute for a shared electorate that had never been allowed to exist. Cite Austin's Cornerstone for the borrowing thesis and Bandyopadhyay for the communal-electoral mechanism.
